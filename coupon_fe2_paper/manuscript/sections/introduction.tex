\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The effective response of a heterogeneous material emerges from the deformation of its constituents and their interactions with the microstructure. At finite strains, this interaction can produce anisotropy and coupling between loading modes that are difficult to capture with a predetermined macroscopic constitutive law. Computational homogenization addresses this difficulty by resolving a representative volume element (RVE): an imposed deformation drives a microscopic boundary-value problem, from which effective stresses and constitutive derivatives are obtained~\cite{Miehe1999}. Rather than prescribing the effective law in advance, the RVE supplies it through the solution of the underlying mechanical problem.

This flexibility comes at a computational cost. Characterizing the material over a range of deformations already requires many nonlinear finite element solves; using the same RVE within a structural calculation multiplies those solves across macroscopic integration points and load increments. Much of this repeated work concerns closely related microscopic states. Surrogate modeling seeks to exploit that relationship by computing representative solutions offline and reusing their information in less expensive online evaluations. The objective is therefore not only to reduce execution time and the resources required to store and process microscopic fields, but to do so without losing the constitutive behavior that made the RVE description useful.

There are two complementary ways to pursue this objective. Non-intrusive constitutive surrogates learn the effective response from RVE input--output data and dispense with the microscopic solve online. Intrusive projection-based reduced-order models (PROMs) instead compress the microscopic displacement field and retain projected equilibrium equations, with hyperreduction limiting the local contributions needed to evaluate them. The former offers a direct constitutive evaluation; the latter retains access to an approximate microscopic state and the given constituent law. Mechanical knowledge and learning can enter either route: physics-augmented neural networks (PANNs) embed constitutive restrictions in the learned response, while artificial neural networks (ANNs) can enrich a PROM's displacement representation.

This work develops a polyconvex PANN for the effective anisotropic hyperelastic response of an RVE. The aim is to make the learned energy sufficiently expressive while preserving its mechanical structure, including an exactly stress-free reference state. PROM-based surrogates of the same RVE provide complementary accuracy--cost comparisons, and a tensile structural example examines how the constitutive approximations behave when deployed beyond isolated material evaluations. The constitutive construction is the central contribution; structural deployment supplies a further test of its usefulness.

\subsection{Physics-augmented neural networks}
The attraction of direct constitutive learning is that the expensive microscopic solver is needed to generate data, but not to evaluate the trained material model. Neural homogenization has demonstrated this separation~\cite{Aldakheel2023}, making the systematic generation of representative microscopic responses an important part of the offline workflow~\cite{kalina2023}. Once this separation is made, however, the inputs and architecture of the surrogate must carry the information that would otherwise be supplied by the microscopic problem.

For materials with memory, this information extends beyond the current deformation. Loading history can be retained through learned history-to-response maps~\cite{Liu2022} or through internal descriptors, such as the maximum directional stretches used in polymer models~\cite{Ghaderi2020}. The data-generation burden also grows when several interacting scales must be represented; staggered training of successive two-scale surrogates provides one way to manage that burden~\cite{AsadFarhat2026}. Here we consider history-independent hyperelasticity. Removing the need to represent memory simplifies the state description, but introduces a precise constitutive requirement: the stress response must be compatible with a stored-energy function.

Learning that function, rather than fitting each stress component independently, makes the stress work conservative and gives a major-symmetric constitutive tangent by differentiation. This is a central motivation for the mechanics-informed energy formulation of As'ad et al.~\cite{asad2022}. Expressing the energy through objective deformation measures then removes dependence on a superposed rigid rotation. In this way, PANNs shift part of the burden from learning mechanical identities from data to satisfying them through the representation itself~\cite{klein2022,linden2023}. Prediction accuracy remains to be established, but potential structure and objectivity need not depend on how densely the training domain has been sampled.

Preserving a potential does not, by itself, control its curvature. At finite strains, that control must also be imposed in appropriate variables. Convexity in Green--Lagrange strain, as used in Ref.~\cite{asad2022}, is attractive because stress and tangent follow directly from the learned energy. Its implication for curvature with respect to the deformation gradient nevertheless contains a stress-dependent term~\cite{GaoNeffRoventaThiel2017}, so it is not a global polyconvexity guarantee. Conversely, enforcing a positive-semidefinite matrix through a lower-triangular factorization~\cite{Xu2021} controls a matrix property, but does not by itself supply the integrability needed to recover a scalar energy. The modeling task is to preserve both the potential and the desired finite-strain curvature condition within one construction.

Polyconvexity provides a route to that combination. It represents the energy as a convex function of deformation minors, yielding a sufficient condition for rank-one convexity~\cite{ball1977}. For the two-dimensional model considered here, the independent arguments are the deformation gradient and its determinant. This framework also underpins existence results when the additional coercivity and admissibility hypotheses are met~\cite{Ciarlet1988}. We use it as a sufficient constitutive restriction, not as a necessary property of every homogenized material or a guarantee of structural uniqueness. The remaining challenge is representational: the restrictions that provide a guarantee must still leave enough freedom to describe the anisotropic RVE response.

\subsection{Projection-based reduced-order models}
An alternative to learning the effective energy is to retain the microscopic problem and reduce the space in which it is solved. In a conventional PROM, solution snapshots supply a reduced-order basis, usually through proper orthogonal decomposition (POD), and projected equilibrium determines the coefficients of the displacement field in that basis. This allows information from previous RVE solves to be reused without replacing the constituent law, as demonstrated in reduced finite-strain homogenization~\cite{yvonnet2007,Hernandez2014}. Retaining a field representation also supports multiscale settings in which reduced periodic substructures must exchange kinematic information~\cite{Hernandez2020}. For the present RVE, the question is how much of this field information can be retained at a small online cost.

The first limitation is the approximation space. A family of solutions may depend on only a few loading parameters and still be poorly represented by a small global linear basis. The Kolmogorov $n$-width makes this distinction precise: if the best worst-case error over all $n$-dimensional linear spaces decays slowly, adding modes remains expensive even when the solution family has a low-dimensional parametrization~\cite{barnett2023,aresdeparga2026nonlinear}. Local bases address this difficulty by distributing the approximation among regions of the solution set~\cite{Amsallem2012}. Nonlinear manifolds pursue the same objective without restricting each representation to an affine space, using, for example, autoencoder mappings~\cite{Lee2020} or quadratic corrections~\cite{Barnett2022}.

This motivates retaining a rich POD representation while reducing the number of coefficients that must be determined independently. The PROM--ANN formulation~\cite{barnett2023} achieves this by solving for a small set of primary coordinates and using an ANN to reconstruct the secondary ones.\footnote{The closure need not be neural: the latent-space framework of Ares De Parga et al.~\cite{aresdeparga2026nonlinear} also considers Gaussian-process regression (GPR) and radial-basis-function (RBF) closures.} The displacement field can thus retain the contribution of many POD modes without introducing an equilibrium unknown for each one. We adopt this primary/secondary representation with an ANN closure for the RVE, seeking to retain the accuracy of the richer displacement space at the cost of a smaller nonlinear system.

Reducing the unknowns does not eliminate a second cost: evaluating the microscopic contributions entering the projected equations. Even a very small reduced system can remain expensive if its residual and tangent require constitutive evaluations throughout the original mesh. Hardware acceleration can lower the cost of those evaluations, as in GPU-assisted reduced homogenization~\cite{Fritzen2016}; hyperreduction instead reduces how many contributions must be evaluated. It is this second approximation that turns a PROM into a hyperreduced projection-based reduced-order model (HPROM).

Two broad families approach this task from different directions~\cite{Grimberg2021,AresDeParga2023}. Approximate-then-project methods, notably discrete empirical interpolation (DEIM)~\cite{Chaturantabut2010}, reconstruct a high-dimensional nonlinear quantity from selected samples before projecting its approximation. For a mechanical model, however, accurate reconstruction of that quantity does not automatically preserve the variational structure of the reduced equations. Project-then-approximate methods target the reduced quantities directly, often by replacing full assembly with a weighted sum of selected local contributions. Optimized cubature~\cite{An2008}, energy-conserving sampling and weighting (ECSW)~\cite{Farhat2014,farhat2015}, and the empirical cubature method (ECM)~\cite{hernandez2017} follow this route. The structure-preservation results for ECSW in structural dynamics~\cite{farhat2015} illustrate the value of designing the approximation around the mechanical equations, although they do not constitute a stability guarantee for every sampling-and-weighting method. For the RVE considered here, this route also has a direct computational advantage: selected finite element contributions can be evaluated on a reduced mesh using the existing constituent law. We adopt ECM within this setting.

The original ECM construction~\cite{hernandez2017} obtains a sparse integration rule by selecting spatial points and nonnegative weights that reproduce a training space of integrands. Its economy comes from replacing the many contributions in the full discretization by a much smaller weighted subset. The resulting rule nevertheless retains two restrictions: points are selected from a discrete candidate set, and one weight vector must serve the entire training space. Subsequent developments show that either restriction can be relaxed to seek greater compression. The continuous empirical cubature method (CECM)~\cite{Hernandez2024} takes an initially feasible discrete rule and removes points while readjusting both the positions and weights of those that remain. Here the extra freedom is spatial: the integration points are no longer confined to the original candidates.

For reduced models built from local spaces or nonlinear manifolds, the second restriction becomes especially relevant. Once different regions of the solution set have their own local bases, requiring one fixed weight vector to reproduce the conditions from all of them can impose an unnecessarily global integration problem. Subspace-adaptive weights ECM (SAW--ECM)~\cite{Bravo2024} exploits this observation by retaining a common set of points while assigning different nonnegative weights to each local basis. As the local representation was refined in the examples of that study, the required common support decreased. The gain came from adapting how the selected points contribute, rather than from moving their locations.

Manifold-adaptive weights ECM (MAW--ECM)~\cite{hernandez2026} carries this idea from a collection of local spaces to a continuously parameterized solution manifold: latent coordinates replace the local-basis index, and each retained spatial entity receives a weight field over the latent domain. The integration rule can thus follow the reduced state instead of reproducing the global linear span of all training conditions with one fixed vector. This is the connection to the nonlinear closure above. The MAW--ECM study illustrates it with an RBF-based reconstruction; the present work uses an ANN closure and neural weight predictors. The adaptive-weight principle concerns the manifold and its integrands, not a particular regression architecture.

These developments motivate two complementary reduced evaluators for the present comparison: an affine HPROM with fixed ECM, and a nonlinear-manifold HPROM--ANN with adaptive weights. A direct mode, D--HPROM--ANN, additionally uses strain-informed coordinates to reconstruct the microscopic response without reduced equilibrium iterations. Comparing these modes examines how displacement compression, integration compression, and online equilibrium work affect accuracy and cost. Their constitutive outputs must still be assessed on their own terms: retaining the constituent law and positive cubature weights does not alone establish that every approximate effective stress derives from one energy.

\subsection{Feature representation and position relative to prior work}
\label{sec:discussion}
The two surrogate routes therefore place the approximation in different parts of the problem. A PROM compresses the microscopic state while continuing to evaluate the given material law; an energy-based PANN must represent the effective law itself. For an anisotropic response, this requires more than a flexible scalar network. The inputs must express directional dependence while remaining compatible with objectivity and the desired convexity condition.

Structural tensors provide a way to encode material directions in objective scalar quantities and have enabled anisotropic polyconvex energies~\cite{SchroderNeff2003}. The directional description matters as much as the scalar response assigned to it: models of dispersed fibers, for example, account for a distribution of orientations rather than one preferred axis~\cite{Gasser2006}. In a learned constitutive law, the corresponding feature map determines which distinctions between deformation states the network can use. Encoding directions is therefore part of the model, not merely a preprocessing step.

An ICNN provides a convex scalar core~\cite{amos2017}, but composing that core with nonlinear features requires additional care. A sufficient construction uses features convex in the independent deformation minors and a core that is also nondecreasing in each feature~\cite{klein2022}. The latter condition links admissibility to expressiveness. If two states satisfy $\bm z(\F_a)\leq\bm z(\F_b)$ componentwise, a monotone core must satisfy $H(\bm z(\F_a))\leq H(\bm z(\F_b))$. Increasing its width cannot reverse that ordering. When reference corrections and growth terms are present, the comparison concerns the part of the energy represented by the core, but the restriction remains. An unsuitable feature family can therefore prevent an otherwise admissible architecture from fitting the target response.

This is not merely a formal possibility. In the cubic-lattice example of Klein et al.~\cite{klein2022}, an invariant-based polyconvex model has difficulty representing shear, while a deformation-gradient-based formulation improves the fit. The latter uses data augmentation for approximate objectivity, whereas the invariant model is exactly objective and performs well for their transversely isotropic example. These results point to the representation, rather than polyconvexity alone, as a potential source of limited accuracy. They motivate enlarging the admissible feature family before concluding that a convex architecture is inadequate for a material.

Allowing the directional description to adapt during training is one way to gain that flexibility. Generalized-structure-tensor models learn aspects of the anisotropy~\cite{Kalina2024}, and objective triclinic polyconvex constructions show that a nontrivial material symmetry group need not be prescribed in advance~\cite{klein2026}. Yet directional flexibility must also be reconciled with the reference state. A learned anisotropic energy can produce a nonzero reference stress, and cancelling that stress with an arbitrary strain correction can destroy polyconvexity. The compatible normalization and growth terms developed in Ref.~\cite{linden2023} show why these corrections belong to the constitutive construction itself. This leads to the design question pursued here: can the directional features be made flexible while controlling their reference derivatives so that stress normalization remains admissible?

Once the features convey the required directional information, the scalar core must resolve how the energy varies with them. This flexibility can be placed in the coefficients of admissible energy terms, as in constitutive artificial neural networks (CANNs)~\cite{Linka2023}, or in learned monotone energy derivatives~\cite{Tac2022}. For the present construction, an ICNN supplies a compatible core through weighted compositions of fixed convex activations. A spline-based Kolmogorov--Arnold network instead learns the shapes of its univariate edge functions, providing a complementary way to distribute that flexibility. Constitutive KAN formulations~\cite{Abdolazizi2025} motivate this alternative, but preserving the feature-composition proof requires more than using a scalar energy output: each edge must also respect the required convexity and monotonicity. Monotonic input-convex KANs (ICKANs) provide this connection~\cite{thakolkaran2025}. We therefore assess the paired features with both cores and use analytically joined splines so that the admissibility argument also covers their tails. The comparison tests the scalar approximation while retaining the same type of mechanically constrained feature representation.

\subsection{Present contribution}
We address the feature and normalization requirements together through paired directional features with trainable orientations, stretch powers, and determinant exponents constrained by explicit joint-convexity bounds. Pairing makes each feature's strain derivative at the undeformed state proportional to the identity tensor, even though its response away from that state remains anisotropic. A single scalar correction affine in the determinant can then cancel the complete reference stress without tying that correction to the independently adjustable volumetric stiffness. The feature family thus gains flexibility while retaining an analytically controlled reference state.

The family is coupled to a smooth ICNN and an integrated-hat spline ICKAN, both convex and componentwise nondecreasing in the features. For either core, the proof covers the complete energy, including normalization and growth terms, and establishes objectivity, an exactly stress-free reference, two-dimensional polyconvexity, and the specified growth limits. The spline realization uses analytically matched tails. Global nonnegative energy is addressed separately by a sufficient lower bound evaluated for the trained parameters. The contribution builds on established convex-network and affine-determinant normalization principles; it is their realization with the paired anisotropic family and its accompanying analytical conditions.

Figure~\ref{fig:constitutive_pipeline} summarizes the resulting constitutive construction. RVE responses train a family of paired directional features and either of two monotone convex cores. The learned contribution is then completed by analytic reference and growth terms, producing a stored energy from which stress and tangent follow by differentiation. The numerical study places this construction beside a flexible energy reference to interpret the cost of the guarantees, and beside reduced microscopic evaluators to contextualize structural accuracy and online cost.

\subsection{Assessment strategy and scope}
The examples address two successive questions. First, how accurately can the constrained energy represent anisotropic RVE responses, and what does learning the paired features contribute? The constitutive assessment examines independent response errors and the fixed/learned-feature comparison. Derivative checks and saved-parameter energy bounds complement the analytical construction, following the broader motivation to examine constitutively relevant derivatives as well as fitting errors~\cite{Tac2024}. Sampled curvature tests check the implementation; the polyconvexity guarantee comes from the proof.

Second, how do those constitutive approximations behave within a nonlinear structural calculation? A tensile coupon supplies this deployment test. In the first-order two-level finite element formulation, denoted FE$^2$, each macroscopic integration point queries the same RVE response. Replacing that query by each surrogate propagates its approximation through a common equilibrium problem. A nested full-order calculation (FOM--FE$^2$) supplies the reference, and field-level comparisons complement aggregate displacement and stress errors~\cite{Chmiel2024}. The example thus connects material-level assessment to structural accuracy and online cost without making the development of a new FE$^2$ scheme the subject of the paper.

The mechanical guarantees and the numerical comparisons answer distinct questions. The construction establishes the properties of the energy, while independent RVE and structural evaluations assess its approximation accuracy and computational usefulness. Additional closed-cycle and curvature diagnostics are documented in the supplementary material.

The structural comparison concerns material A under plane-strain kinematics and one loading path. FOM--FE$^2$ agreement measures surrogate error within the adopted homogenization model, not its accuracy against a structure resolving every microscopic cell. The study assesses the feature family, normalization, and core together; isolating their individual contributions requires matched-budget ablations. Likewise, the reported online times compare the implemented workflows at the stated process/thread counts and repetition levels, excluding offline data generation and training. They do not quantify memory savings or an offline-inclusive break-even point. Within this scope, the aim is to establish the usefulness and constitutive properties of the proposed representation, not universality across microstructures, general three-dimensional completeness, or guaranteed robustness under microscopic instability.
