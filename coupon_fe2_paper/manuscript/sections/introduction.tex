\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Computational homogenization provides a way to predict the response of heterogeneous materials without prescribing a macroscopic constitutive equation in advance. Instead, the effective stress and tangent are obtained by solving a boundary-value problem on a representative volume element (RVE)~\cite{Miehe1999}. In a two-level finite element calculation, commonly denoted FE$^2$, this problem is coupled to the macroscopic finite element model: the deformation at each macroscopic integration point drives an RVE calculation, whose homogenized response enters the structural equilibrium equations. This allows the influence of the constituents and microstructure to be carried into a structural analysis, including anisotropy and coupling between deformation modes at finite strains.

The computational burden is substantial. Each macroscopic load increment can require several Newton iterations, and each iteration entails constitutive evaluations at many integration points. In FE$^2$, these evaluations are themselves nonlinear finite element solves. Their repetition, together with the storage and manipulation of microscopic states and operators, can make the overall calculation prohibitively demanding in execution time and computational resources. This motivates surrogate models that replace the repeated full-order RVE calculations with less expensive evaluations. Representative full-order solutions are generated offline; the objective is then to exploit this information to reduce the online cost while retaining the accuracy needed by the macroscopic solver.

Two broad routes can be distinguished by their access to the microscopic governing equations. Non-intrusive constitutive surrogates learn the relation between macroscopic deformation and homogenized response from RVE data, without requiring access to the microscopic residual and tangent operators. Once trained, they replace the online RVE solve with a direct evaluation of stress and tangent. Intrusive projection-based reduced-order models (PROMs), in contrast, use those operators to construct and solve a low-dimensional approximation of the microscopic equilibrium problem. Hyperreduction further limits the elements or integration points needed to evaluate the reduced equations. Both routes seek computational savings, but they replace different parts of the full-order calculation.

This distinction is not a division between models with and without physics, nor between models with and without neural networks. A non-intrusive surrogate can embed constitutive restrictions in its architecture, as in physics-augmented neural networks (PANNs), while a PROM can use an artificial neural network (ANN) to enrich its approximation space and still retain a reduced equilibrium solve. The questions are therefore what the surrogate learns, which equations or constitutive properties it retains, and how these choices affect structural accuracy and online cost. These questions motivate the present comparison of PANNs and PROM-based surrogates for anisotropic hyperelasticity.

\subsection{Physics-augmented neural networks}
The non-intrusive route begins with a constitutive approximation: given the macroscopic deformation, predict the response that would otherwise require an RVE solve. Neural models of homogenized material behavior~\cite{Aldakheel2023} and physics-constrained networks coupled to automated microscopic data generation~\cite{kalina2023} illustrate this approach. Its attraction is that the online model no longer needs a microscopic mesh or equilibrium iterations. Its central difficulty is ensuring that the learned relation remains a suitable constitutive law when used inside a structural solver. The required state description also depends on the material: learning microscopic histories~\cite{Liu2022}, directional polymer models with history variables~\cite{Ghaderi2020}, and staggered training across interacting scales in woven materials~\cite{AsadFarhat2026} address path-dependent behavior. The present work concerns history-independent hyperelasticity, for which the current deformation describes the constitutive state.

Even in this setting, fitting stress values is not sufficient to define a mechanically consistent constitutive law. For a hyperelastic material, the stress must derive from a stored-energy function. Direct regression of its components does not enforce that relation: an accurate fit at sampled states can coexist with nonzero net work around a closed deformation cycle. Learning a scalar energy and obtaining stress by differentiation enforces the potential structure, as emphasized by As'ad et al.~\cite{asad2022}, and supplies the corresponding major symmetry of the tangent. Objectivity can also be imposed through deformation measures that leave the energy unchanged under superposed rigid rotations. PANNs incorporate such constitutive structure into the learned representation~\cite{klein2022,linden2023}. The term identifies this modeling strategy, not a single architecture or a guarantee that every PANN satisfies the same set of conditions. Which properties hold must be established for the particular construction.

Curvature introduces a further distinction. An energy potential need not satisfy a convexity condition, and a convexity condition must specify its variables. As'ad et al.~\cite{asad2022} use an input-convex neural network (ICNN) in Green--Lagrange strain. Convexity in that strain measure has a tensile-state stability implication, but the second Piola stress enters the relation between strain-space and deformation-gradient curvature~\cite{GaoNeffRoventaThiel2017}. Similarly, a positive-matrix factorization in an incremental stress update~\cite{Xu2021} is not, by itself, an energy Hessian. These formulations therefore address different constraints.

Polyconvexity~\cite{ball1977} provides a tractable sufficient condition for rank-one convexity by representing the energy as a convex function of deformation minors. In two dimensions, a convex representation in the deformation gradient and its determinant is sufficient. Together with appropriate growth, coercivity, and admissibility hypotheses, this structure also supports existence results in nonlinear elasticity~\cite{Ciarlet1988}. It does not guarantee uniqueness or convergence of a particular Newton algorithm. For anisotropic hyperelasticity, the challenge is to impose the selected conditions without making the representation too restrictive to fit the homogenized response. This is the constitutive question pursued here. The alternative PROM route approaches the acceleration problem from the governing equations rather than from a prescribed macroscopic energy representation.

\subsection{Projection-based reduced-order models}
The intrusive route retains the governing equilibrium equations instead of learning only their homogenized constitutive output. Projection-based model order reduction (PMOR) seeks their solution in a low-dimensional approximation space. For a microscopic displacement problem, a conventional PROM represents the displacement field in an affine space spanned by a reduced-order basis, usually constructed by proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) of solution snapshots. The basis coefficients are the generalized coordinates solved for online. Reduced finite-strain homogenization~\cite{yvonnet2007,Hernandez2014} and potential-based two-scale calculations with GPU acceleration~\cite{Fritzen2016} establish this route to reducing microscopic cost. Reduced periodic-substructure methods also operate in domain-decomposition settings~\cite{Hernandez2020}; here, the reduced model serves as the microscopic evaluator within first-order FE$^2$.

There are two distinct costs to reduce. The first is the number of independent unknowns. A small affine space may represent a curved family of microscopic solutions poorly, even when that family depends on few loading parameters. Local reduced bases~\cite{Amsallem2012}, autoencoder-based manifolds~\cite{Lee2020}, and quadratic approximation manifolds~\cite{Barnett2022} address this representational difficulty in different ways. The PROM--ANN approach of Barnett et al.~\cite{barnett2023} and the latent-space closure framework of Ares De Parga et al.~\cite{aresdeparga2026nonlinear} instead partition the reduced coordinates into primary and secondary groups. The primary generalized coordinates remain independent unknowns, while a learned closure predicts the secondary generalized coordinates from them. A rich displacement representation can therefore be reconstructed while solving for only a few independent coordinates. We adopt this primary/secondary nomenclature.

The second cost is the evaluation of the reduced equations. Reducing the number of coordinates is insufficient if every residual evaluation still requires constitutive calculations and assembly over the full microscopic mesh. Sampling-and-weighting hyperreduction approximates these evaluations through a selected subset of local contributions and associated weights. Optimized cubature~\cite{An2008}, energy-conserving sampling and weighting (ECSW)~\cite{Farhat2014,farhat2015}, and the empirical cubature method (ECM)~\cite{hernandez2017} provide established constructions, including extensions to local bases and Petrov--Galerkin projection~\cite{Grimberg2021,AresDeParga2023}. A hyperreduced projection-based reduced-order model (HPROM) thus combines an approximation of the microscopic state with an approximation of the integrals entering its reduced equations.

The integration rule can itself exploit the state representation. Continuous empirical cubature adjusts point locations and weights~\cite{Hernandez2024}; subspace-adaptive cubature uses common locations with weights specific to each reduced subspace~\cite{Bravo2024}. Manifold-adaptive weights extend the latter idea to weights varying continuously with the reduced coordinates~\cite{hernandez2026}. We use fixed-weight ECM for the affine HPROM and manifold-adaptive cubature for HPROM--ANN, both on actual reduced meshes. These models retain a reduced equilibrium solve. A direct operating mode, D--HPROM--ANN, additionally uses the imposed strain to prescribe strain-informed coordinates and reconstruct the microscopic response without equilibrium iterations. Although this mode also avoids an online equilibrium solve, it is derived from the intrusive reduced-order construction and still evaluates the microscopic material law on selected elements. It therefore differs from a PANN that directly represents the homogenized energy.

\subsection{Feature representation and position relative to prior work}
\label{sec:discussion}
The comparison between these routes brings the representational question into focus. A PROM approximates the microscopic displacement field and evaluates the given constituent law, whereas an energy-based PANN must represent the effective anisotropic energy itself. For the latter, the choice of constitutive inputs is part of the model. Structural tensors encode material directions, and invariants formed from these tensors provide objective measures of deformation. Polyconvex invariant formulations have been developed for anisotropic elasticity~\cite{SchroderNeff2003}, while models of distributed fiber orientations illustrate why the directional description can affect predicted deformation mechanisms~\cite{Gasser2006}. In a neural constitutive model, these scalar measures act as the network's features.

A useful sufficient construction combines polyconvex features with an energy core that is convex and nondecreasing in every feature. ICNNs provide the convex core~\cite{amos2017}; anisotropic invariant formulations and compatible growth and reference-state corrections are developed by Klein et al.~\cite{klein2022} and Linden et al.~\cite{linden2023}. Other structured parameterizations include constitutive artificial neural networks~\cite{Linka2023} and polyconvex neural ordinary differential equations~\cite{Tac2022}. For spline-based Kolmogorov--Arnold networks (KANs), constitutive KANs combine energy modeling, partial monotonicity, and symbolic interpretation~\cite{Abdolazizi2025}, whereas monotonic input-convex KANs (ICKANs) add the convexity constraints used in their polyconvex invariant composition~\cite{thakolkaran2025}.

The features determine which responses this constrained core can represent. Let $\bm z$ be the feature vector and $H$ a nondecreasing core. For two deformation states,
\[
 \bm z(\F_a)\leq\bm z(\F_b)
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 H\!\left(\bm z(\F_a)\right)\leq H\!\left(\bm z(\F_b)\right),
\]
where the feature inequality is componentwise. A target core response with the opposite ordering cannot be fitted by increasing the network width. Additional reference and volumetric terms must be included before applying this reasoning to the complete energy, but they do not remove the core's ordering restriction. This simple observation makes feature design consequential before optimization begins.

Klein et al.~\cite{klein2022} provide a pertinent example: their invariant model struggles with shear in a cubic lattice, while a deformation-gradient-based polyconvex model fits that response more accurately. The invariant approach performs well on their transversely isotropic example. The contrast identifies a representation issue, rather than a general failure of polyconvexity. More recent generalized-structure-tensor models learn aspects of anisotropy~\cite{Kalina2024}, and objective triclinic polyconvex formulations can be constructed without prescribing a nontrivial material symmetry group~\cite{klein2026}. Accordingly, the question addressed here is not whether anisotropy can be learned at all, but how a particular admissible feature family can gain flexibility while retaining a compatible stress-free reference.

\subsection{Present contribution}
The constitutive contribution is a paired directional feature family with trainable orientations, stretch powers, and determinant exponents subject to explicit joint-convexity bounds. Pairing is chosen so that each feature's strain derivative at the undeformed state is proportional to the identity tensor. The resulting reference stress can then be cancelled by a single scalar correction affine in the determinant, while the positive volumetric stiffness terms remain independently adjustable. Away from the reference state, the paired terms can still produce an anisotropic response. The construction thus links representational flexibility to a normalization that preserves polyconvexity.

The same feature family is coupled to a smooth ICNN and an integrated-hat spline ICKAN. Both realizations enforce objectivity, an exactly stress-free reference, two-dimensional polyconvexity, and the specified growth behavior. For the spline realization, the tails have analytically matched derivatives. Nonnegative energy is treated separately through a sufficient lower bound evaluated for the selected trained parameters. The construction builds on existing ICNN and ICKAN principles and affine-determinant normalization; the specific contribution is their realization with this paired anisotropic family and the accompanying analytical conditions.

The computational contribution is a common assessment of PANNs and PROM-based surrogates against nested full-order FE$^2$ (FOM--FE$^2$). The affine HPROM, iterative HPROM--ANN, and direct decoder mode quantify the consequences of different levels of online microscopic approximation. POD, latent-space closure, ECM, and manifold-adaptive cubature are established ingredients, not new algorithms introduced here. Their inclusion makes it possible to assess whether the additional cost of retaining microscopic equilibrium yields useful structural accuracy compared with direct constitutive evaluation. \subsection{Assessment strategy and scope}
The numerical examples are organized around two questions. The periodic-cell study asks whether the constrained feature family can represent the homogenized anisotropic response and what the imposed constraints add beyond pointwise stress accuracy. Four constitutive tiers are considered: direct stress Regression, an unrestricted scalar-energy model termed Free, and the ICNN and ICKAN energies. Closed-cycle work tests integrability, while rank-one curvature addresses a condition not implied by potential structure alone. This attention to constitutively relevant derivatives is also motivated by the benchmarking study of Tac et al.~\cite{Tac2024}. The tensile-coupon study then measures how these constitutive approximations and the reduced microscopic models propagate into structural fields and complete online wall time. Field comparisons complement aggregate errors~\cite{Chmiel2024}.

The assessment concerns one periodic material under plane-strain kinematics, a prescribed training domain, and one structural loading path. The coupon is a deployment of the same RVE, not a second material validation. Within this scope, the study demonstrates compatibility between accurate fitting and the proposed polyconvex construction; it does not establish universality across microstructures, three-dimensional constitutive completeness, or history-dependent behavior. The effects of feature learning, reference normalization, and network parameterization are assessed jointly in the proposed construction. Attributing the gain separately to feature learning, determinant exponents, normalization, or spline curvature requires matched-budget ablations.

Constitutive counterexamples are interpreted within their reference coverage. A nonzero closed-cycle work exposes nonconservativity in Regression, whereas sampled curvature tests complement, but do not replace, an analytical guarantee. The present extrapolative Free-model curvature witness lacks a converged FOM reference and is reported as a model-level observation, not as an FOM-validated spurious instability. Successful coupon convergence likewise does not certify a constitutive property. Finally, the online cost comparison excludes offline training and data-generation costs and reports the actual process/thread configurations and repetition counts. It compares the implemented online workflows under the reported configurations; it does not constitute a matched-resource statistical ranking or an offline-inclusive break-even analysis. Memory savings are a motivation for model reduction, but are not quantified in this assessment.
