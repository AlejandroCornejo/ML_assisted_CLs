\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The effective response of a heterogeneous material emerges from the deformation of its constituents and their interactions with the microstructure. At finite strains, this interaction can produce anisotropy and coupling between loading modes that are difficult to capture with a predetermined macroscopic constitutive law. Computational homogenization addresses this difficulty by resolving a representative volume element (RVE): an imposed deformation drives a microscopic boundary-value problem, from which effective stresses and constitutive derivatives are obtained~\cite{Miehe1999,kouznetsova2001approach,geers2010multi}. Rather than prescribing the effective law in advance, the RVE supplies it through the solution of the underlying mechanical problem.

This flexibility comes at a computational cost. Characterizing the material over a range of deformations already requires many nonlinear finite element solves; using the same RVE within a structural calculation multiplies those solves across macroscopic integration points and load increments~\cite{feyel2003multilevel,geers2010multi}. Much of this repeated work concerns closely related microscopic states. Surrogate modeling seeks to exploit that relationship by computing representative solutions offline and reusing their information in less expensive online evaluations. The objective is therefore not only to reduce execution time and the resources required to store and process microscopic fields, but to do so without losing the constitutive behavior that made the RVE description useful.

Two complementary approximation routes are relevant, but they play different roles in this work. Non-intrusive constitutive surrogates learn the effective response from RVE input--output data and dispense with the microscopic solve online~\cite{le2015computational}. Intrusive projection-based reduced-order models (PROMs) instead compress the microscopic displacement field and retain projected equilibrium equations~\cite{benner2015survey,yvonnet2007,Hernandez2014}, with hyperreduction limiting the local contributions required to evaluate them~\cite{hernandez2017}. We focus on the first route: learning the effective stored energy while embedding the constitutive restrictions needed for its mechanical use. The intrusive route is retained as a secondary reference because it preserves an approximate microscopic state and the underlying constituent law.

The central contribution is a family of learnable paired directional features for polyconvex anisotropic energies. Their orientations and exponents adapt to the RVE response, while their paired construction permits an admissible exact normalization at the undeformed state. Coupling these features to either an input-convex neural network (ICNN) or an input-convex Kolmogorov--Arnold network (ICKAN) gives two realizations of the same constitutive design. PROM-based surrogates of the same RVE then provide secondary accuracy--cost references, and a tensile structural example tests how the learned energies behave beyond isolated material evaluations.

\subsection{Energy-based constitutive learning and polyconvexity}
Direct constitutive learning moves the expensive microscopic solves to the data-generation stage and replaces them online by a trained material model. Neural homogenization has demonstrated this separation~\cite{Aldakheel2023}, making systematic sampling of representative microscopic responses an essential part of the offline workflow~\cite{kalina2023}. What the surrogate must represent depends on the material class: history-dependent responses may require learned history-to-response maps~\cite{Liu2022} or internal descriptors such as maximum directional stretches~\cite{Ghaderi2020}, while interacting scales may motivate staggered data generation and training~\cite{AsadFarhat2026}. Here, by contrast, we consider history-independent hyperelasticity. This removes memory from the state but imposes a precise constitutive requirement: the stress must derive from a stored-energy function.

Learning that function, rather than fitting each stress component independently, makes the stress work conservative and gives a major-symmetric constitutive tangent by differentiation. This is a central motivation for the mechanics-informed energy formulation of As'ad et al.~\cite{asad2022}. Expressing the energy through objective deformation measures then removes dependence on a superposed rigid rotation. In this way, physics-augmented neural networks (PANNs) shift part of the burden from learning mechanical identities from data to satisfying them through the representation itself~\cite{klein2022,linden2023}. Prediction accuracy remains to be established, but potential structure and objectivity need not depend on how densely the training domain has been sampled.

Preserving a potential does not, by itself, control its curvature. At finite strains, that control must also be imposed in appropriate variables. Convexity in Green--Lagrange strain, as used in Ref.~\cite{asad2022}, is attractive because stress and tangent follow directly from the learned energy. Its implication for curvature with respect to the deformation gradient nevertheless contains a stress-dependent term~\cite{GaoNeffRoventaThiel2017}, so it is not a global polyconvexity guarantee. Conversely, Xu et al.~\cite{Xu2021} parameterize an incremental tangent through a lower-triangular factorization to enforce positive semidefiniteness. This controls the tangent matrix but does not by itself define the scalar potential required for integrability. The modeling task is therefore to preserve both the potential and the desired finite-strain curvature condition within one construction.

Polyconvexity provides a route to that combination. It represents the energy as a convex function of deformation minors, yielding a sufficient condition for rank-one convexity~\cite{ball1977}. For the two-dimensional model considered here, the independent arguments are the deformation gradient and its determinant. This framework also underpins existence results when the additional coercivity and admissibility hypotheses are met~\cite{Ciarlet1988}. We use it as a sufficient constitutive restriction, not as a necessary property of every homogenized material or a guarantee of structural uniqueness. The remaining challenge is representational: the restrictions that provide a guarantee must still leave enough freedom to describe the anisotropic RVE response.

\subsection{Directional features and representational expressiveness}
\label{sec:discussion}
The remaining representational challenge is especially consequential for anisotropy: a flexible scalar core can distinguish only the deformation states that its inputs distinguish. Structural tensors encode material directions in objective scalar quantities and underpin established anisotropic polyconvex energies~\cite{SchroderNeff2003}. The directional description is itself a constitutive choice. Models of fiber-reinforced tissue, for example, must account for a distribution of orientations rather than one perfectly aligned family when that dispersion affects the mechanical response~\cite{Gasser2006}. A learned feature map must likewise expose the relevant directional distinctions while remaining compatible with objectivity and convexity in the deformation minors. It is therefore part of the constitutive model, not merely a preprocessing step.

A convex scalar core does not resolve this choice. An ICNN provides a convex function~\cite{amos2017}, but a sufficient composition with nonlinear features requires those features to be convex in the independent deformation minors and the core to be componentwise nondecreasing~\cite{klein2022}. This monotonicity imposes an order on feature space: if $\bm z(\F_a)\leq\bm z(\F_b)$ componentwise, then $H(\bm z(\F_a))\leq H(\bm z(\F_b))$. Increasing the network width cannot reverse that ordering. When reference corrections and growth terms complete the energy, the comparison applies to the contribution represented by the core, but the restriction remains. A poorly chosen feature family can therefore impose a representational obstruction before network capacity or optimization becomes limiting.

The numerical evidence of Klein et al.~\cite{klein2022} illustrates this distinction. For their cubic beam-lattice data, an exactly objective invariant-based polyconvex model fails to represent the shear response, whereas a model formulated in $\F$, $\cof\F$, and $\det\F$ gives a substantially better fit while approximating objectivity through data augmentation. The same invariant-based model performs very well for their analytically generated transversely isotropic data. These results do not identify polyconvexity alone as the source of limited accuracy; they show that success depends on whether the admissible inputs expose the distinctions required by the material response. This motivates enlarging the feature family before concluding that a convex core is inadequate.

The natural response is to make the directional description adaptive without abandoning the admissible composition. Generalized-structure-tensor models calibrate the structural tensors together with the energy model, allowing the data to identify aspects of the anisotropy~\cite{Kalina2024}. Recent triclinic invariant constructions and group symmetrization further broaden the polyconvex descriptions available for finite material-symmetry groups~\cite{klein2026}. Greater directional freedom, however, must be reconciled with the undeformed state. A learned anisotropic contribution need not produce zero reference stress, while cancelling that stress with an arbitrary strain correction can violate polyconvexity or material symmetry. The invariant-based normalization and analytic growth terms of Linden et al.~\cite{linden2023} demonstrate why these operations must belong to the admissible constitutive construction. The feature family must therefore do two jobs at once: distinguish the relevant directional response and control its reference derivatives so that exact stress normalization remains compatible with polyconvexity. This is the role of the paired features introduced here.

The feature map determines what the model can distinguish; the scalar core determines how those distinctions are combined into an energy. Prior constructions distribute this flexibility in different ways. Constitutive artificial neural networks (CANNs) learn coefficients and exponents within an interpretable catalog of admissible energy terms~\cite{Linka2023}, whereas neural ordinary differential equations can learn monotone energy derivatives and recover convex energy contributions by integration~\cite{Tac2022}. In the present work, an ICNN combines the paired features through nonnegative weights and fixed convex activations. A Kolmogorov--Arnold network (KAN) instead places trainable univariate functions on its edges, a flexibility explored in constitutive KANs~\cite{Abdolazizi2025}. A scalar energy output alone does not preserve the feature-composition argument; the edge functions must satisfy the required convexity and monotonicity, as in monotone input-convex KANs (ICKANs)~\cite{thakolkaran2025}. We therefore compare a smooth ICNN with an integrated-hat ICKAN under the same paired features and analytic completion, joining the learned splines to admissible tails. The comparison isolates how the scalar dependence is represented rather than changing the directional information or the mechanical guarantees.

\subsection{Projection-based reduced-order models}
The preceding approach learns the effective energy and thereby removes the microscopic solve from the online stage. Projection-based reduction makes a different compromise: it retains the RVE boundary-value problem, but seeks its solution in a space learned from previous microscopic states. In a conventional PROM, those states define a basis, usually through proper orthogonal decomposition (POD), and projected equilibrium determines the coordinates of each new displacement field. Reduced finite-strain homogenization has shown that this strategy can reuse information from earlier RVE solves without replacing the constituent law~\cite{yvonnet2007,Hernandez2014}. Moreover, because the result is still an approximate microscopic field, it can support settings in which reduced periodic substructures exchange kinematic information~\cite{Hernandez2020}. PROMs therefore provide a natural physics-based reference for the present study: they preserve more of the microscopic problem than an effective-energy surrogate, but pay for it by retaining an online equilibrium solve.

The affordability of that solve depends first on the geometry of the solution family. A small number of loading parameters does not necessarily imply that the corresponding microscopic states lie close to a small global linear space. When their Kolmogorov $n$-width decays slowly, many basis vectors---and hence many equilibrium unknowns---are required to achieve adequate accuracy. Considerable effort has therefore been devoted to mitigating this Kolmogorov barrier with richer reduced representations~\cite{Amsallem2012,Lee2020,Barnett2022,barnett2023,sibuet2025discrete,aresdeparga2026nonlinear}. Local methods construct a piecewise-linear approximation by replacing one global space with several spaces associated with different regions of the solution set~\cite{Amsallem2012}. Manifold methods go further by allowing a few coordinates to describe a curved approximation, using constructions such as autoencoder mappings~\cite{Lee2020}, quadratic corrections~\cite{Barnett2022}, or learned closures within a POD coordinate space~\cite{barnett2023,sibuet2025discrete,aresdeparga2026nonlinear}. The last of these offers the particular compromise sought here: PROM--ANN enriches the geometry of the reduced state without giving up either its POD description or projected equilibrium~\cite{barnett2023}; a subsequent hypersonic-flow assessment compared this nonlinear representation with affine and quadratic alternatives~\cite{Chmiel2024}. Equilibrium determines only a small set of primary coordinates, while an artificial neural network (ANN) reconstructs the secondary coordinates that populate a much richer POD expansion.\footnote{The closure need not be neural: the latent-space framework of Ares De Parga et al.~\cite{aresdeparga2026nonlinear} also considers Gaussian-process regression (GPR) and radial-basis-function (RBF) closures.} The microscopic displacement can thereby draw on many modes while the online nonlinear system remains small, which motivates the primary--secondary construction adopted for our RVE.

Reducing the number of unknowns, however, does not by itself make their equilibrium equations inexpensive. At every nonlinear iteration, assembling the reduced residual and tangent may still require constitutive evaluations over the complete microscopic mesh. Faced with this remaining cost, one may either perform the same full-mesh work faster or reduce how much of it must be performed. Hardware acceleration pursues the first option~\cite{Fritzen2016}. Hyperreduction pursues the second, reconstructing the required operators from sparse information and thereby turning a PROM into a hyperreduced PROM (HPROM). Choosing this latter path raises a further design question: should the high-dimensional nonlinear quantities be approximated before projection, or should projection come first so that only the reduced equations are approximated? These alternatives define the approximate-then-project and project-then-approximate viewpoints, respectively~\cite{Grimberg2021,AresDeParga2023}. The former reconstructs a sampled high-dimensional quantity before projecting it, as exemplified by discrete empirical interpolation (DEIM)~\cite{Chaturantabut2010}. The latter acts directly on the reduced equations, replacing full assembly by weighted contributions from a small subset of finite elements or other spatial entities. This route includes the linear-programming empirical quadrature procedure (EQP) for reduced-basis models~\cite{patera2017lp,yano2019lp}, energy-conserving sampling and weighting (ECSW)~\cite{Farhat2014,farhat2015}, and the empirical cubature method (ECM)~\cite{hernandez2017,Hernandez2020}. The structure-preservation results obtained for ECSW in structural dynamics reinforce the value of targeting the projected equations~\cite{farhat2015}, although they do not extend automatically to every sampling-and-weighting construction. Within the elementwise finite element setting adopted here, both ECSW and ECM are natural choices: each replaces full assembly by a nonnegative weighted sum of selected contributions while leaving the local constituent evaluations intact. We follow ECM because a sequence of developments in our previous work extends its fixed rule toward movable points and subspace- and manifold-adaptive weights~\cite{Hernandez2024,Bravo2024,hernandez2026dimensional}, providing the methodological path needed to couple hyperreduction with PROM--ANN.

That path begins with a limitation of the classical rule. ECM can remove most element evaluations, but one fixed support and one vector of nonnegative weights must represent the entire training space~\cite{hernandez2017}. CECM first relaxed the spatial part of this restriction: starting from a feasible discrete rule, it readjusts both point positions and weights during construction so that the final support is no longer confined to the original candidates~\cite{Hernandez2024}. This additional freedom can produce a sparser rule, but the resulting weights must still serve the full state space. SAW--ECM addressed that remaining globality for a collection of local linear spaces. It retains a common support, yet assigns a different nonnegative weight vector to each subspace, allowing the rule to follow which local representation is active~\cite{Bravo2024}. Once those discrete local spaces are replaced by a continuously parameterized nonlinear manifold, the natural continuation is to make the weights continuous as well. MAW--ECM therefore promotes the subspace-specific vectors to weight fields over the latent coordinates~\cite{hernandez2026dimensional}. This final step closes the loop with PROM--ANN: the same primary coordinates that locate the microscopic state on the learned manifold can drive both the decoder of its secondary coordinates and the adaptive weights used to evaluate its equilibrium and stress.

This sequence produces a controlled ladder of intrusive references for the structural comparison. The affine HPROM retains a linear POD state, solves projected equilibrium, and evaluates it with fixed ECM. HPROM--ANN replaces the linear state by the primary--secondary manifold and the fixed rule by adaptive integration, but still determines its primary coordinates through equilibrium. D--HPROM--ANN removes that final online solve by inferring the microscopic state directly from the imposed strain. Moving across the three models therefore removes successive portions of the online microscopic work while preserving an approximate microscopic state and selected evaluations of the constituent law. Their purpose is to contextualize the accuracy and cost of the proposed energy surrogates, not to supply the same constitutive guarantees: neither the retained constituent law nor nonnegative cubature weights alone ensures that the approximate effective stress is the derivative of a single effective potential.

\subsection{Present contribution}
Against these intrusive physics-based references, the central route pursued in this work is an effective-energy surrogate whose anisotropy is learned without relinquishing polyconvexity or exact reference-state normalization. These requirements are coupled: a learnable anisotropic contribution generally produces a nonzero stress at the undeformed state, whereas cancelling that stress with an arbitrary correction can destroy the admissible constitutive structure. We resolve this tension through paired directional features with trainable orientations, stretch powers, and determinant exponents constrained by explicit joint-convexity bounds.

The pairing is designed so that the strain derivative of every feature at the undeformed state is proportional to the identity tensor, even though its response away from that state remains anisotropic. The complete learned contribution can consequently be normalized by a single scalar correction affine in the determinant. This correction cancels the reference stress without tying that cancellation to the independently adjustable volumetric stiffness, allowing the directional description to adapt to the RVE while the reference state remains under analytic control.

Once the directional information has been exposed in this admissible form, two monotone convex cores provide alternative representations of its scalar energetic dependence: a smooth ICNN and an integrated-hat spline ICKAN with analytically matched tails. In both cases, the learned core, reference correction, and growth terms form one complete energy for which the analysis establishes objectivity, an exactly stress-free reference, two-dimensional polyconvexity, and the specified growth limits. Global nonnegative energy is treated separately through a sufficient lower bound evaluated for the trained parameters. The novelty lies in bringing established convex-network and affine-determinant normalization principles together through the paired anisotropic family and its explicit analytical conditions.

The numerical study then places these models beside a flexible energy reference to interpret the cost of the guarantees and beside the reduced microscopic evaluators just described to contextualize structural accuracy and online cost.

\subsection{Assessment strategy and scope}
The assessment follows the two levels at which the surrogates are intended to operate. At the constitutive level, it asks how accurately the constrained energies represent independent anisotropic RVE responses and what is gained by learning the paired directions rather than prescribing them. Response errors and the fixed/learned-feature comparison address approximation quality, while derivative checks and saved-parameter energy bounds interrogate behavior that a fit metric alone cannot reveal~\cite{Tac2024}. Sampled curvature tests serve here as implementation diagnostics; the polyconvexity guarantee itself comes from the analytical construction.

At the structural level, the question becomes whether those constitutive approximations remain accurate and useful after being embedded in a nonlinear boundary-value problem. A tensile coupon provides this deployment test. Within the first-order two-level finite element formulation, denoted FE$^2$, an RVE problem supplies the material response at each macroscopic integration point~\cite{feyel2003multilevel}; here every point queries the same periodic cell. Replacing that query by a surrogate propagates its approximation through a common equilibrium problem. A nested full-order model (FOM)--FE$^2$ calculation supplies the reference, while field-level comparisons complement aggregate displacement and stress errors. The intrusive reduced models then provide physics-based accuracy--cost benchmarks without turning the development of a new FE$^2$ scheme into a second objective of the paper.

These two levels deliberately separate analytical guarantees from empirical adequacy. The constitutive construction establishes the stated properties of the complete energy, whereas independent RVE and structural evaluations assess how well that admissible representation approximates the selected material and how much online work it avoids. Additional closed-cycle and curvature diagnostics are documented in the supplementary material.

The resulting claims are necessarily tied to the scope of the study: the SC-RVE, plane-strain kinematics, and the loading path applied to the coupon. Agreement with FOM--FE$^2$ measures surrogate error within the adopted homogenization model, not error relative to a structure in which every microscopic cell is resolved explicitly. The comparisons assess the feature family, normalization, and convex core as a combined constitutive design rather than attributing performance exhaustively to each component. Reported timings concern the implemented online workflows; offline data generation, training, memory savings, and break-even behavior remain outside the comparison. The objective within these boundaries is to establish the usefulness and constitutive properties of the proposed representation, not universality across microstructures, general three-dimensional completeness, or robustness under every possible microscopic instability.
