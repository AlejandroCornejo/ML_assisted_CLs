\subsection{Constitutive requirements and the variables they constrain}
\label{sec:requirements}
A constitutive approximation must be assessed at several levels. Objectivity concerns a change of observer; potential structure concerns work along a path; convexity conditions concern particular variations of deformation; reference normalization fixes the undeformed state. These requirements should not be collapsed into a single label such as physics-informed.

For a superposed proper rotation $\bm Q$, objectivity requires
\begin{equation}
 W(\bm Q\F)=W(\F),\qquad
 \bm P(\bm Q\F)=\bm Q\bm P(\F).
\end{equation}
Using $\C=\F^T\F$ as an input to an energy enforces the first identity. A direct map from $\E$ to the material stress $\Sg$ can also be objective without possessing a potential. Objectivity therefore does not distinguish Regression from Free in this study. Material symmetry is different: a right action $\F\mapsto\F\bm Q_m$ represents a symmetry of the reference material only for transformations in its material symmetry group. Directional features transform with the reference material, not with the observer.

For a differentiable hyperelastic energy, stress is its derivative in work-conjugate variables. In the convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:voigt},
\begin{equation}
 \ssv(\ee)=\nabla_{\ee}W(\ee),\qquad
 \oint_{\Gamma}\ssv\cdot\dd\ee=0 .
 \label{eq:conservative_requirement}
\end{equation}
The closed-path identity is exact for a single-valued differentiable potential. On a simply connected strain domain, a continuously differentiable stress map has such a potential if its Jacobian is symmetric. Direct stress regression need not impose the cross-derivative identities
$\partial s_i/\partial e_j=\partial s_j/\partial e_i$~\cite{asad2022}. A small pointwise stress error alone does not establish them.

Potential structure does not impose a stability condition. The Legendre--Hadamard condition for a twice differentiable energy is
\begin{equation}
 \partial^2_{\F\F}W(\F)[\bm a\otimes\bm b,\bm a\otimes\bm b]\geq0
 \quad\hbox{for all }\bm a,\bm b .
 \label{eq:lh_background}
\end{equation}
Strict positivity for nonzero directions is a stronger statement than this nonnegative condition. In two dimensions, a sufficient condition is a representation
\begin{equation}
 W(\F)=\mathcal P(\F,\det\F),\qquad
 \mathcal P\text{ convex in its independent arguments}.
 \label{eq:polyconvex_definition}
\end{equation}
This is two-dimensional polyconvexity. The cofactor is linear in the entries of a $2\times2$ matrix, so it need not be introduced as an additional independent argument. Along a rank-one line $\F+t\bm a\otimes\bm b$, both $\F$ and its determinant depend affinely on $t$. Convexity of $\mathcal P$ then implies convexity of the restriction of $W$ to every admissible segment of that line.

The corresponding three-dimensional definition uses $(\F,\cof\F,\det\F)$~\cite{ball1977,Ciarlet1988}. The restriction of a three-dimensional polyconvex law to plane-strain kinematics is compatible with a two-dimensional polyconvex representation. The converse does not follow: establishing Eq.~\eqref{eq:polyconvex_definition} does not establish an admissible three-dimensional extension with arbitrary out-of-plane deformation. The proposed construction and its guarantees are stated here for the actual in-plane model.

Convexity in $\E$ is a different property. To see why, take an arbitrary perturbation $\bm H$ of $\F$. Along $\F(t)=\F+t\bm H$,
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\E}=\operatorname{sym}(\F^T\bm H),\qquad
 \ddot{\E}=\bm H^T\bm H,\qquad
 \frac{\dd^2 W}{\dd t^2}
   =\dot{\ee}^{\,T}\bm D\dot{\ee}
     +\Sg:(\bm H^T\bm H).
 \label{eq:strain_vs_F_curvature}
\end{equation}
Even if $\bm D$ is positive semidefinite, the stress term can be negative in compression. This is the distinction behind the stress-dependent arguments in As'ad et al.~\cite{asad2022} and Gao et al.~\cite{GaoNeffRoventaThiel2017}. Convexity in $\C$ and in $\E$ are equivalent up to their affine change of variables, but neither should be relabeled as polyconvexity. Similarly, a positive-matrix factor in an incremental stress update~\cite{Xu2021} is not automatically a Hessian of a scalar energy: the required compatibility between derivatives still has to be established.

Reference normalization imposes
\begin{equation}
 W(\I)=0,\qquad \bm P(\I)=\bm0.
\end{equation}
The first condition fixes an arbitrary additive energy constant. The second is a substantive stress condition and depends on how the energy is corrected. Subtracting a linear function of $\E$ preserves a potential but need not preserve polyconvexity in $\F$. An affine function of an independent deformation minor, in contrast, preserves convexity of $\mathcal P$. The normalization below exploits precisely this distinction.

Finally, the inequalities $W(\F)\geq0$, the barrier $W\to\infty$ as $J\to0^+$, and the existence of minimizers are not synonyms. Ciarlet's presentation of Ball's existence argument~\cite{Ciarlet1988}, especially Sections 4.9 and 7.7, includes coercivity, admissible boundary data, and finite-energy competitors in addition to polyconvexity. Neither the existence theory nor a constitutive barrier guarantees convergence of an unconstrained Newton step. In this work, architectural statements, saved-parameter bounds, and numerical observations are therefore identified separately.

\subsection{Why the feature map matters before the network}
\label{sec:feature_order}
Consider a feature vector $\bm z(\F)$ and a core $H$ nondecreasing in each input. For any two deformation states,
\begin{equation}
 \bm z(\F_B)\geq\bm z(\F_A)\text{ componentwise}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H(\bm z(\F_B))\geq H(\bm z(\F_A)).
 \label{eq:feature_ordering}
\end{equation}
This implication holds regardless of the network's width. If the complete constitutive energy is $W=H(\bm z)+W_{\rm corr}$, the ordered quantity is $W-W_{\rm corr}$, not $W$ alone. Thus a decreasing reference energy along such a path indicates an obstruction only after the complete correction is accounted for. The diagnosis must examine the feature map and analytic terms together.

This observation explains why changing an admissible feature construction can be more effective than increasing the capacity of its convex core. The original 15-feature model and the paired 32-feature model are not different numbers of independent strains: both represent the same three-dimensional engineering-strain state. They are different embeddings of that state into variables on which the network is constrained to be monotone and convex. Additional features can enlarge the admissible response family, but their number alone does not prove completeness.

The paired construction below couples directional stretch to volume through admissible determinant exponents. A feature can therefore decrease along a path when its denominator grows sufficiently, even if a numerator increases. Moreover, its reference derivative is controlled analytically, allowing normalization and volumetric stiffness to be set independently. These are changes to the representation, not changes to the training strain box.
