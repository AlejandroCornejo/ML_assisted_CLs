\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The macroscopic response of a heterogeneous solid depends on how its constituents and microstructure accommodate the applied deformation. At finite strains, this interaction can generate anisotropy and coupling between loading modes that are difficult to capture with a predetermined constitutive equation. Computational homogenization addresses this difficulty by computing the effective response from a microscopic boundary-value problem~\cite{Miehe1999}. The macroscopic deformation is imposed on a representative volume element (RVE), microscopic equilibrium determines its deformation, and the resulting fields provide the effective stress and tangent needed by the macroscopic solver.

In a two-level finite element calculation, denoted FE$^2$, a microscopic problem is attached to each macroscopic integration point. Repeated microscopic solves during load increments and Newton iterations can make the structural calculation prohibitively expensive. A natural strategy is to compute representative microscopic solutions offline and reuse that information online. Two approximation routes follow from this strategy. Projection-based reduced micromechanics compresses the microscopic state and its governing equations, whereas constitutive learning approximates the effective stress--strain relation directly. Both can employ artificial neural networks (ANNs), but they learn different objects and retain different parts of the microscopic problem.

The distinction is central to the present work. A network that reconstructs microscopic displacements does not have the same role as one that predicts a macroscopic energy, even when both are trained from the same RVE solutions. We investigate these routes within a common finite-strain homogenization problem, with particular attention to the relation between constitutive accuracy, imposed mechanical structure, and online cost. The constitutive question is how to construct a sufficiently flexible anisotropic energy while retaining polyconvexity and an exactly stress-free reference state. The computational question is how this direct constitutive approximation compares with reduced microscopic models that retain progressively less online equilibrium work.

\subsection{Projection-based reduced micromechanics}
Projection-based model order reduction (PMOR) uses the governing equations to determine a solution within a low-dimensional approximation space. For a microscopic displacement problem, a conventional projection-based reduced-order model (PROM) represents the displacement field in an affine space spanned by a reduced-order basis, usually constructed by proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) of solution snapshots. The basis coefficients are the generalized coordinates solved for online. Reduced finite-strain homogenization~\cite{yvonnet2007,Hernandez2014} and potential-based two-scale calculations with GPU acceleration~\cite{Fritzen2016} establish this route to reducing microscopic cost. Reduced periodic-substructure methods also operate in domain-decomposition settings~\cite{Hernandez2020}; here, the reduced model serves as the microscopic evaluator within first-order FE$^2$.

There are two distinct costs to reduce. The first is the number of independent unknowns. A small affine space may represent a curved family of microscopic solutions poorly, even when that family depends on few loading parameters. Local reduced bases~\cite{Amsallem2012}, autoencoder-based manifolds~\cite{Lee2020}, and quadratic approximation manifolds~\cite{Barnett2022} address this representational difficulty in different ways. The PROM--ANN approach of Barnett et al.~\cite{barnett2023} and the latent-space closure framework of Ares De Parga et al.~\cite{aresdeparga2026nonlinear} instead partition the reduced coordinates into primary and secondary groups. The primary generalized coordinates remain independent unknowns, while a learned closure predicts the secondary generalized coordinates from them. A rich displacement representation can therefore be reconstructed while solving for only a few independent coordinates. We adopt this primary/secondary nomenclature.

The second cost is the evaluation of the reduced equations. Reducing the number of coordinates is insufficient if every residual evaluation still requires constitutive calculations and assembly over the full microscopic mesh. Sampling-and-weighting hyperreduction approximates these evaluations through a selected subset of local contributions and associated weights. Optimized cubature~\cite{An2008}, energy-conserving sampling and weighting (ECSW)~\cite{Farhat2014,farhat2015}, and the empirical cubature method (ECM)~\cite{hernandez2017} provide established constructions, including extensions to local bases and Petrov--Galerkin projection~\cite{Grimberg2021,AresDeParga2023}. A hyperreduced projection-based model (HPROM) thus combines an approximation of the microscopic state with an approximation of the integrals entering its reduced equations.

The integration rule can itself exploit the state representation. Continuous empirical cubature adjusts point locations and weights~\cite{Hernandez2024}; subspace-adaptive cubature uses common locations with weights specific to each reduced subspace~\cite{Bravo2024}. Manifold-adaptive weights extend the latter idea to weights varying continuously with the reduced coordinates~\cite{hernandez2026}. We use fixed-weight ECM for the affine HPROM and manifold-adaptive cubature for HPROM--ANN, both on actual reduced meshes. These models retain a reduced equilibrium solve. A direct operating mode, D--HPROM--ANN, additionally uses the imposed strain to prescribe strain-informed coordinates and reconstruct the microscopic response without equilibrium iterations. This last approximation allows the cost and accuracy of bypassing reduced equilibrium to be examined explicitly.

\subsection{Mechanics-informed constitutive learning}
Constitutive learning removes the online microscopic solve by approximating its effective response directly. Neural models of homogenized material behavior~\cite{Aldakheel2023} and physics-constrained networks coupled to automated microscopic data generation~\cite{kalina2023} illustrate this approach. Its state description depends on the material: learning microscopic histories~\cite{Liu2022}, directional polymer models with history variables~\cite{Ghaderi2020}, and staggered training across interacting scales in woven materials~\cite{AsadFarhat2026} address path-dependent behavior. The present work concerns history-independent hyperelasticity, for which the current deformation describes the constitutive state.

Even in this setting, fitting stress values is not sufficient to define a mechanically consistent constitutive law. For a hyperelastic material, the stress must derive from a stored-energy function. Direct regression of its components does not enforce that relation: an accurate fit at sampled states can coexist with nonzero net work around a closed deformation cycle. Learning a scalar energy and obtaining stress by differentiation enforces the potential structure, as emphasized by As'ad et al.~\cite{asad2022}, and supplies the corresponding major symmetry of the tangent. Objectivity can also be imposed through deformation measures that leave the energy unchanged under superposed rigid rotations. These are structural properties of the model rather than consequences of its fitting error.

Curvature introduces a further distinction. An energy potential need not satisfy a convexity condition, and a convexity condition must specify its variables. As'ad et al.~\cite{asad2022} use an input-convex neural network (ICNN) in Green--Lagrange strain. Convexity in that strain measure has a tensile-state stability implication, but the second Piola stress enters the relation between strain-space and deformation-gradient curvature~\cite{GaoNeffRoventaThiel2017}. Similarly, a positive-matrix factorization in an incremental stress update~\cite{Xu2021} is not, by itself, an energy Hessian. These formulations therefore address different constraints.

Polyconvexity~\cite{ball1977} provides a tractable sufficient condition for rank-one convexity by representing the energy as a convex function of deformation minors. In two dimensions, a convex representation in the deformation gradient and its determinant is sufficient. Together with appropriate growth, coercivity, and admissibility hypotheses, this structure also supports existence results in nonlinear elasticity~\cite{Ciarlet1988}. It does not guarantee uniqueness or convergence of a particular Newton algorithm. This distinction motivates assessing approximation error, constitutive structure, and structural solution behavior separately.

\subsection{Feature representation and position relative to prior work}
\label{sec:discussion}
For anisotropic materials, the choice of constitutive inputs is part of the model. Structural tensors encode material directions, and invariants formed from these tensors provide objective measures of deformation. Polyconvex invariant formulations have been developed for anisotropic elasticity~\cite{SchroderNeff2003}, while models of distributed fiber orientations illustrate why the directional description can affect predicted deformation mechanisms~\cite{Gasser2006}. In a neural constitutive model, these scalar measures act as the network's features.

A useful sufficient construction combines polyconvex features with an energy core that is convex and nondecreasing in every feature. ICNNs provide the convex core~\cite{amos2017}; anisotropic invariant formulations and compatible growth and reference-state corrections are developed by Klein et al.~\cite{klein2022} and Linden et al.~\cite{linden2023}. Other structured parameterizations include constitutive artificial neural networks~\cite{Linka2023} and polyconvex neural ordinary differential equations~\cite{Tac2022}. For spline-based Kolmogorov--Arnold networks (KANs), constitutive KANs combine energy modeling, partial monotonicity, and symbolic interpretation~\cite{Abdolazizi2025}, whereas monotonic input-convex KANs (ICKANs) add the convexity constraints used in their polyconvex invariant composition~\cite{thakolkaran2025}.

The features determine which responses this constrained core can represent. Let $\bm z$ be the feature vector and $H$ a nondecreasing core. For two deformation states,
\[
 \bm z(\F_a)\leq\bm z(\F_b)
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 H\!\left(\bm z(\F_a)\right)\leq H\!\left(\bm z(\F_b)\right),
\]
where the feature inequality is componentwise. A target core response with the opposite ordering cannot be fitted by increasing the network width. Additional reference and volumetric terms must be included before applying this reasoning to the complete energy, but they do not remove the core's ordering restriction. This simple observation makes feature design consequential before optimization begins.

Klein et al.~\cite{klein2022} provide a pertinent example: their invariant model struggles with shear in a cubic lattice, while a deformation-gradient-based polyconvex model fits that response more accurately. The invariant approach performs well on their transversely isotropic example. The contrast identifies a representation issue, rather than a general failure of polyconvexity. More recent generalized-structure-tensor models learn aspects of anisotropy~\cite{Kalina2024}, and objective triclinic polyconvex formulations can be constructed without prescribing a nontrivial material symmetry group~\cite{klein2026}. Accordingly, the question addressed here is not whether anisotropy can be learned at all, but how a particular admissible feature family can gain flexibility while retaining a compatible stress-free reference.

\subsection{Present contribution}
The constitutive contribution is a paired directional feature family with trainable orientations, stretch powers, and determinant exponents subject to explicit joint-convexity bounds. Pairing is chosen so that each feature's strain derivative at the undeformed state is proportional to the identity tensor. The resulting reference stress can then be cancelled by a single scalar correction affine in the determinant, while the positive volumetric stiffness terms remain independently adjustable. Away from the reference state, the paired terms can still produce an anisotropic response. The construction thus links representational flexibility to a normalization that preserves polyconvexity.

The same feature family is coupled to a smooth ICNN and an integrated-hat spline ICKAN. Both realizations enforce objectivity, an exactly stress-free reference, two-dimensional polyconvexity, and the specified growth behavior. For the spline realization, the tails have analytically matched derivatives. Nonnegative energy is treated separately through a sufficient lower bound evaluated for the selected trained parameters. The construction builds on existing ICNN and ICKAN principles and affine-determinant normalization; the specific contribution is their realization with this paired anisotropic family and the accompanying analytical conditions.

The computational contribution is a common assessment against reduced micromechanics and nested full-order FE$^2$ (FOM--FE$^2$). The affine HPROM, iterative HPROM--ANN, and direct decoder mode quantify the consequences of different levels of online microscopic approximation. POD, latent-space closure, ECM, and manifold-adaptive cubature are established ingredients, not new algorithms introduced here. Their inclusion makes it possible to assess whether the additional cost of retaining microscopic equilibrium yields useful structural accuracy compared with direct constitutive evaluation. \subsection{Assessment strategy and scope}
The numerical examples are organized around two questions. The periodic-cell study asks whether the constrained feature family can represent the homogenized anisotropic response and what the imposed constraints add beyond pointwise stress accuracy. Four constitutive tiers are considered: direct stress Regression, an unrestricted scalar-energy model termed Free, and the ICNN and ICKAN energies. Closed-cycle work tests integrability, while rank-one curvature addresses a condition not implied by potential structure alone. This attention to constitutively relevant derivatives is also motivated by the benchmarking study of Tac et al.~\cite{Tac2024}. The tensile-coupon study then measures how these constitutive approximations and the reduced microscopic models propagate into structural fields and complete online wall time. Field comparisons complement aggregate errors~\cite{Chmiel2024}.

The assessment concerns one periodic material under plane-strain kinematics, a prescribed training domain, and one structural loading path. The coupon is a deployment of the same RVE, not a second material validation. Within this scope, the study demonstrates compatibility between accurate fitting and the proposed polyconvex construction; it does not establish universality across microstructures, three-dimensional constitutive completeness, or history-dependent behavior. A second microstructure would need to test a distinct deformation mechanism. Moreover, the revised construction changes several ingredients together, so the observed improvement is assessed for the combined model. Attributing the gain separately to feature learning, determinant exponents, normalization, or spline curvature requires matched-budget ablations.

Constitutive counterexamples are interpreted within their reference coverage. A nonzero closed-cycle work exposes nonconservativity in Regression, whereas sampled curvature tests complement, but do not replace, an analytical guarantee. The present extrapolative Free-model curvature witness lacks a converged FOM reference and is reported as a model-level observation, not as an FOM-validated spurious instability. Successful coupon convergence likewise does not certify a constitutive property. Finally, the online cost comparison excludes offline training and data-generation costs and reports the actual process/thread configurations and repetition counts. It measures the archived deployments rather than a matched-resource statistical ranking or an offline-inclusive break-even analysis.
