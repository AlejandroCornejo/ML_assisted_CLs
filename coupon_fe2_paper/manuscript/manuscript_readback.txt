                                                     Abstract
         Replacing microscopic boundary-value problems by inexpensive constitutive maps can substantially
     reduce the cost of ﬁnite-strain multiscale analysis. However, interpolation accuracy, mechanical admissi-
     bility, and computational eﬃciency are diﬀerent requirements. This work investigates their interaction
     for an anisotropic porous hyperelastic material using two families of approximations: projection-based
     hyperreduced micromechanics and learned macroscopic constitutive laws. For the latter, we formu-
     late paired directional features with trainable powers and determinant exponents, coupled to convex
     nondecreasing neural or spline networks. The construction preserves objectivity and two-dimensional
     polyconvexity, while aﬃne determinant normalization separates the stress-free reference condition from
     volumetric stiﬀness. A separate suﬃcient condition addresses global nonnegative energy for the selected
     trained parameters. A periodic cell with a rotated elliptical void ﬁrst tests constitutive accuracy and
     mechanical consistency. The selected ICNN and spline-ICKAN models attain aggregate held-out stress
     errors of approximately 0.65%. A closed strain cycle exposes nonconservative work in direct stress regres-
     sion, whereas a search for negative rank-one curvature in an unconstrained energy network is reported
     with its reference-validation limitations. A tensile coupon then evaluates structural errors and online cost
     against a nested full-order reference. The polyconvex models produce displacement errors below 0.43%,
     while optimized hyperreduced micromechanics is more accurate but generally more expensive. All com-
     pared models converge on this nominal load path. The results support accurate, inexpensive polyconvex
     surrogates for this material and deformation domain, without establishing unrestricted extrapolation or
     universality across microstructures.
Keywords: computational homogenization; hyperelasticity; polyconvex neural networks; input-convex
splines; empirical cubature; nonlinear model reduction.


1    Introduction
In ﬁrst-order computational homogenization, a macroscopic constitutive evaluation is obtained from the
solution of a microscopic boundary-value problem. This avoids prescribing an eﬀective constitutive equation,
but transfers a considerable computational burden to each macroscopic integration point. For nonlinear
problems, the microscopic response and its tangent may be needed repeatedly during every macroscopic
load increment. Reducing this burden without obscuring the mechanical content of the resulting model is
the central concern of this work.
    Two complementary strategies are considered. Projection-based methods compress the microscopic dis-
placement ﬁeld and continue to impose reduced equilibrium. The reduced model multiscale method of
Yvonnet and He [1] is an early ﬁnite-strain example of this approach. For nonlinear ﬁnite element models,
reducing the number of unknowns does not remove the cost of evaluating their residuals. Empirical cubature
addresses this second cost by selecting integration contributions and associated weights [2]. Nonlinear ap-
proximation manifolds can further reduce the number of online coordinates; the PROM–ANN construction
of Barnett et al. [3] learns a closure in a reduced latent space, and manifold-adaptive empirical cubature has
recently been developed for nonlinear ﬁnite element models [4]. These ingredients serve here as established
building blocks, not as newly introduced reduction algorithms.
    The alternative is to replace microscopic equilibrium by a learned macroscopic constitutive relation.
As’ad et al. [5] explain why direct stress regression need not deﬁne a conservative material, even when its
training data are hyperelastic, and construct energy-based models using objective strains. Their convexity
condition is imposed in Green–Lagrange strain; the associated stability discussion explicitly distinguishes


                                                          1
tensile and compressive states. It must not be relabelled as a general polyconvexity certiﬁcate. The FEANN
framework of Kalina et al. [6] combines physics-constrained energy networks with automated multiscale data
mining. These works also motivate distinguishing the selection of informative deformation states from the
approximation of their constitutive response.
    Polyconvexity supplies a diﬀerent architectural constraint. In the framework introduced by Ball [7], the
energy is represented by a convex function of deformation minors. Klein et al. [8] implement polyconvex
anisotropic neural energies using invariant-based and deformation-gradient-based constructions. The latter
uses data augmentation for approximate objectivity; the former is objective by construction. Linden et
al. [9] give compatible reference-state and growth corrections, while treating global nonnegative energy as
a separate question. More recently, Klein et al. [10] develop objective polyconvex models based on triclinic
invariants, with optional group symmetrization. Consequently, the absence of an imposed material symmetry
group is not by itself a novelty claim for the present formulation. Input-convex Kolmogorov–Arnold networks
provide another choice of convex nondecreasing core: Thakolkaran et al. [11] develop such models for isotropic
compressible hyperelasticity, including symbolic regression and ﬁnite element applications.
    The unresolved practical issue addressed here is narrower than universal constitutive approximation.
A mathematically admissible feature set can be too restrictive for a particular anisotropic homogenized
response. Increasing network width cannot, in general, remove a restriction introduced before the network.
Conversely, removing convexity constraints may improve a ﬁt while removing a constitutive guarantee. We
therefore study a feature family that couples directional distortions to the determinant, and compare its
predictive and mechanical behavior with unrestricted learned models and with reduced micromechanics.
    The contributions of this study are threefold. First, a paired-direction construction provides trainable
polyconvex features whose reference derivatives remain isotropic, allowing a single aﬃne determinant cor-
rection to normalize stress. Second, the same construction is implemented with a smooth ICNN and a C 2
convex spline core, with analytical properties separated from audits of particular trained parameters. Third,
constitutive and structural tests are organized to answer diﬀerent questions: the cell assesses approximation
and mechanical consistency, whereas the coupon measures error propagation, solver behavior, and online
cost against the same nested reference. The present evidence concerns one microstructure; the two tests are
not presented as independent demonstrations across materials.

The D-HPROM–ANN evaluates the slave amplitudes directly from the macroscopic engineering strain. In

                                       adirect (e) = VM A e + VS n(e).                                  (19)

The microscopic equilibrium iterations are absent; the reconstructed state is used in the adaptive stress

4.4    Removing online dependence on discarded elements
The reduced implementations restrict lifting operators and decoder rows to the active MDPA degrees of
freedom. Local force and stiﬀness contributions are projected directly, avoiding unnecessary full-dimensional
assembly. Stress and tangent stencil evaluations are batched. For the direct model, decoder Jacobians that
are unused by the ﬁnite-diﬀerence stress tangent are not computed. These are implementation changes to
the same numerical models, not changes in supports, reduced dimensions, tolerances, or constitutive laws.
Agreement with the retained original implementations and independent derivative checks is assessed before
complete timing runs.

5.3    From a restrictive feature family to an accurate ﬁt
The earlier 15-feature construction combines tr C, twelve balanced directional fourth- and sixth-power fea-
tures of F and cof F , and J, J 2 . Its reference correction uses −r log J. The revised formulation changes both
the feature family and the separation of reference normalization from volumetric stiﬀness; it is not simply a
wider network or a relabelling of the old inputs.
    A convex nondecreasing core preserves the componentwise ordering of its inputs. Consequently, if all
features increase along a deformation path, the core output cannot decrease. Whether the complete energy
decreases also depends on its analytic correction terms. An incompatibility with a ﬁxed feature/correction
family is a representational restriction of that family, not a contradiction of polyconvexity as a mathematical
framework. The revised directional powers and determinant exponents provide additional ways to capture
the observed deformation response while maintaining the suﬃcient convexity conditions.
    The saved original ICNN and ICKAN have held-out aggregate stress errors of approximately 22.78%
and 18.33%, respectively. The selected revised models reduce these errors to approximately 0.65%; Table 2
reports the independently audited selected checkpoints. The Free model’s corresponding aggregate stress
errors are 1.2643% on the test set and 5.5024% on the ﬁnite probe set. Thus the polyconvex models improve
this test ﬁt but do not outperform Free on aggregate probe error. This distinction matters when discussing
extrapolation.

6     Numerical example II: structural accuracy and online cost
6.1    Purpose, boundary conditions, and coverage
This example asks how the constitutive approximations aﬀect a complete nonlinear structural calculation.
The material is the same periodic cell as in Section 5; the new evidence concerns error propagation and
online cost, not a second microstructure. The dog-bone proﬁle has overall length 165 mm, grip width 19
mm, narrow-section width 13 mm, narrow-section length 57 mm, and ﬁllet radius 76 mm. It is a plane-strain
computational benchmark using an ASTM-inspired in-plane proﬁle, not a simulation certiﬁed to reproduce
an ASTM tensile test.
    The two end faces receive equal-and-opposite axial resultants of 100 kN per end, applied in 20 equal
increments. Three point constraints remove rigid modes: the axial and transverse displacement at the left
mid-edge node, and transverse displacement at the right mid-edge node. Entire end faces are not clamped.
Consequently, a small skew of the deformed ends is not ruled out by the boundary conditions. The anisotropic
response can couple axial extension to shear.
    The macro mesh contains 690 quadratic triangles, 1501 nodes, and 2070 integration points. The reference
extrusion thickness used to convert the two-dimensional stress integrals into the stated force resultants is
50 mm. This force convention is part of the plane-strain benchmark, not an ASTM specimen-thickness
speciﬁcation. The physical cell size is declared as 1.3 mm, corresponding to ten cells across the narrow
width. This provides a scale-separation assumption, not a direct numerical validation of homogenization
error at that ratio. All saved converged integration-point strains for every reported model remain inside the
unchanged training box. This check does not automatically cover unsaved intermediate Newton iterates or
certify another load case.

6.2    Online models and implementation veriﬁcation
The linear HPROM uses 39 reduced coordinates, a residual rule supported on 135 elements, and a stress rule
on 73 elements; their union contains 183 elements. The iterative HPROM–ANN uses three latent coordinates
and 36 learned slave amplitudes, with ten residual and ten stress elements and a union of 19. The direct
model evaluates stress on ten elements and solves no microscopic equilibrium equations. These counts refer
to actual reduced MDPA meshes, not masked evaluations on the full RVE mesh. Figure 5 shows their spatial
distribution and the overlap of residual and output rules.
    For the optimized linear and direct implementations, the maximum normalized stress diﬀerences from
the original implementations over the veriﬁcation states are 4.71 × 10−14 and 4.20 × 10−13 . Corresponding
tangent diﬀerences are 2.68 × 10−10 and 7.25 × 10−10 . Independent ﬁnite-diﬀerence checks give discrepancies
of approximately 3.51 × 10−9 and 2.38 × 10−9 . Such comparisons check that optimization preserves the
deployed model; they do not establish that either model exactly reproduces full microscopic equilibrium.

For a saved nodal or integration-point array A, the reported relative error is

The FOM uses 20 worker processes with one numerical-library thread per worker. The three optimized
microscopic surrogates use the same process conﬁguration and three sequential fresh complete runs. PANN
evaluations are batched in one process and use the separately investigated thread settings shown in Table 5.

to the initial increment residual falls below 10−7 . The periodic FOM uses a relative microscopic displacement-
increment tolerance of 10−10 , with continuation when needed. The archived FOM and selected PANN times
each have one repetition in this comparison. Thus their ratios are observed online speedups under the stated
implementations, not matched-thread eﬃciency estimates or repeated-run conﬁdence intervals. Table 6
exposes the available reduced-model variability rather than selecting its fastest repetition. A publication-ﬁnal
timing study should repeat every tier under a common resource-budget protocol and retain all repetitions.

6.4    Accuracy–cost trade-oﬀ and Newton behavior

HPROM gives the smallest displacement error, approximately 0.00565%, at a median online time of 67.35 s.

comparison against the old linear implementation would overstate this gain. The direct closure reduces the
time to 3.79 s and has a displacement error of approximately 0.0232% on this path. Its lower error than the
iterative nonlinear model is an observed outcome, not a consequence of a general error ordering.
    The ICNN and ICKAN times are 1.73 s and 3.67 s, with displacement errors of approximately 0.425%

7.2     What the present evidence supports
The principal positive result is practical compatibility between accurate ﬁtting and a polyconvex architecture
for the investigated anisotropic cell. The original training diﬃculty does not show that polyconvexity is
unreliable; it shows that suﬃcient constructions diﬀer in expressive restrictions. The paired features and
independent volume terms substantially improve the ﬁt, but the experiment changes several ingredients
simultaneously. A frozen-budget ablation is still needed to attribute the gain separately to feature learning,
determinant exponents, reference normalization, and spline curvature ﬂexibility.
    The mechanical tests address diﬀerent requirements. Closed-cycle work detects the lack of a potential
in Regression. Rank-one curvature addresses an additional condition not implied by the potential structure
of Free. The current Free counterexample is outside the reference-validated region and is not an FOM-
conﬁrmed artiﬁcial instability. Nominal coupon convergence therefore complements these tests rather than
replacing them. It would be scientiﬁcally incorrect to report that all unconstrained alternatives fail while all
constrained alternatives are accurate everywhere.
    The results concern a single material, plane-strain kinematics, one structural loading path, and a pre-
scribed training box. They do not demonstrate universality across microstructures, transfer of one trained
network to another material, three-dimensional constitutive completeness, or history-dependent behavior.
A second microstructure should answer an additional question—for example, whether the feature family
remains eﬀective under qualitatively diﬀerent deformation mechanisms—rather than simply duplicate the
same tensile test. Such a result is not included before its data exist.
    Finally, the current online comparison excludes potentially substantial oﬄine costs and has unequal
repetition counts and thread conﬁgurations. These limitations are visible in the tables. Break-even query
counts, deployment robustness under domain exit, and a common repeated timing protocol are necessary
complements for a publication-ﬁnal assessment of computational eﬃciency.

8     Conclusions
Paired directional features with constrained trainable powers and determinant exponents provide a practical
polyconvex constitutive construction for the anisotropic periodic material studied here. Their isotropic
reference derivatives allow exact stress normalization through an aﬃne determinant correction without tying
the normalization coeﬃcient to the volumetric barrier stiﬀness. Both a smooth ICNN and an integrated-hat
spline-ICKAN achieve approximately 0.65% held-out aggregate stress error and below 0.43% displacement
error in the nominal coupon simulation.
    Optimized reduced microscopic models occupy a diﬀerent accuracy–cost range. They are more accurate
on this coupon, while direct learned constitutive evaluation is generally faster. A closed-cycle test exposes
a conservative-mechanics limitation of the regression baseline that nominal ﬁnite element convergence does
not reveal. In contrast, the available Free-model curvature witness is not yet supported by a converged FOM
reference and is reported only within that limited scope. The study supports the usefulness of the proposed
constrained feature family for the present problem, not unrestricted extrapolation or a universal ranking of
constitutive and reduced-order models.
