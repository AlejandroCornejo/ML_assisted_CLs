                                                                                                            Assessment of Projection-Based Model Order Reduction for a
                                                                                                                      Benchmark Hypersonic Flow Problem

                                                                                                                                            Matthew R. Chmiel∗ , Joshua Barnett† and Charbel Farhat‡
                                                                                                                                                 Stanford University, Stanford, California 94305

                                                                                                                        Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is essential for assisting in the aerodynamic design of
                                                                                                                    a hypersonic vehicle, predicting its performance, and complementing or assisting in its flight
                                                                                                                    testing. However, parametric, high-dimensional, high-fidelity, viscous CFD can be prohibitively
                                                                                                                    expensive. Projection-based model order reduction (PMOR) followed by hyperreduction is a
                                                                                                                    candidate mathematical technique for greatly alleviating the computational cost of parametric
                                                                                                                    CFD while maintaining a desirable level of accuracy. In recent years, the least-squares Petrov-
Downloaded by Stanford University on September 7, 2026 | http://arc.aiaa.org | DOI: 10.2514/6.2024-0250




                                                                                                                    Galerkin (LSPG) method has emerged as a state-of-the-art nonlinear PMOR method for
                                                                                                                    realistic, convection-dominated, challenging CFD applications. Its parametric performance is
                                                                                                                    assessed here for the numerical prediction of a variable speed hypersonic flow past a double-cone
                                                                                                                    obstacle, which is a benchmark problem in hypersonic CFD. Present in the steady state solution
                                                                                                                    of the flow are attached and detached shocks; a separation shock; a transmitted shock; multiple
                                                                                                                    shock-shock interactions; a recirculation region at the intersection of the conical surfaces; and
                                                                                                                    a shear layer of supersonic shock and expansion wave reflections along the rear cone surface.
                                                                                                                    First, a high-dimensional, high-fidelity, viscous flow model (HDM) is constructed for this
                                                                                                                    application, verified, and validated. Next, its dimensionality is reduced using LSPG and three
                                                                                                                    different approximation manifolds: an affine one; a quadratic one; and an arbitrarily nonlinear
                                                                                                                    manifold constructed using a deep artificial neural network (ANN). Each resulting global,
                                                                                                                    projection-based reduced-order model (PROM) is hyperreduced using the ECSW method and
                                                                                                                    transformed into a hyperreduced PROM (HPROM), and each PROM/HPROM is trained in the
                                                                                                                    range 8 ≤ 𝑀∞ ≤ 13 using an adaptive sampling procedure. Finally, the accuracy of each trained
                                                                                                                    PROM/HPROM is assessed for the numerical prediction of various quantities of interest and
                                                                                                                    contrasted with that achieved using snapshot interpolation. For this purpose, three different
                                                                                                                    error measures are considered, contrasted, and discussed in the context of shock-dominated flow
                                                                                                                    problems. Wall-clock time and CPU time speedup factors are also reported. Overall, it is shown
                                                                                                                    that using a relatively small set of training data, all constructed global, viscous, CFD-based
                                                                                                                    LSPG PROMs and HPROMs are nonlinearly stable, real-time capable, and highly predictive,
                                                                                                                    despite the complex nature of the flow; and that the LSPG HPROM variant constructed
                                                                                                                    using the arbitrarily nonlinear approximation manifold and an ANN is in this case the most
                                                                                                                    computationally efficient.

                                                                                                                                                              I. Introduction
                                                                                                              or more than a decade now, the role of computational fluid dynamics (CFD) in the analysis and support of air vehicles
                                                                                                          F   has continuously increased [1]. However, the turnaround time associated with CFD remains today one of the factors
                                                                                                          limiting its use in the design and creation of aerodynamic databases [2]. For this reason, various forms of metamodeling
                                                                                                          have been developed over the years to accelerate the CFD-based prediction of aerodynamic and aerothermal quantities
                                                                                                          of interest (QoIs), including surrogate modeling [3, 4], and projection-based model order reduction (PMOR) [5, 6].
                                                                                                               A surrogate model typically refers to a metamodel trained using a purely data-driven approach, such as a response
                                                                                                          surface [7] with or without kriging [8]; a Gaussian process [9]; or a regression artificial neural network [10]. Its training
                                                                                                          data is obtained by intelligently probing several locations in the bounded (design) parameter domain Ω 𝜇 ⊂ R 𝑁𝜇 ; and
                                                                                                          predicting at each probed location 𝝁𝑖 the QoI by post-processing the output of an aerodynamic simulation performed at
                                                                                                          this parameter point using a high-fidelity, high-dimensional computational model (HDM). In general, a surrogate model
                                                                                                          is most appropriate when the QoIs are predetermined and scalar-valued; and their number is relatively small.
                                                                                                               On the other hand, PMOR is a mathematical technique for reducing the computational complexity of an HDM in
                                                                                                          numerical simulations in order to reduce their computational cost. Its outcome is a projection-based reduced-order model
                                                                                                          (PROM) that is typically low-dimensional. Then, a PROM can be hyperreduced [11] to obtain a more computationally
                                                                                                          efficient hyperreduced PROM (HPROM); and the HPROM can be exercised to compute rapidly, via the post-processing
                                                                                                          of simulation outputs, the QoIs. As in the case of a surrogate model, the training data of a PROM/HPROM is obtained
                                                                                                          by intelligently sampling a number of parameter points 𝝁𝑖 ∈ Ω 𝜇 ; and exercising the HDM at each sampled parameter
                                                                                                          point 𝝁𝑖 to generate one or multiple solution snapshots. In general, a PROM/HPROM is most appropriate when
                                                                                                          the QoIs cannot be necessarily predetermined, but are rapidly discovered via the exploration of Ω 𝜇 using the global
                                                                                                          PROM/HPROM – that is, the PROM/HPROM trained in the entirety of Ω 𝜇 ; when they are spatio-temporal fields; and
                                                                                                          when their number is relatively large.
                                                                                                               This paper focuses on the application of PMOR to the acceleration of the solution of parametric CFD problems in the
                                                                                                          hypersonic flow regime; and more specifically, on the least-squares Petrov-Galerkin (LSPG) [6, 12] method which has
                                                                                                          emerged during the last decade as the state of the art of PMOR for convection-dominated transport problems. Indeed,


                                                                                                                                                                       3
                                                                                                          the robustness, accuracy, and computational efficiency of the nonlinear PMOR method LSPG have been demonstrated in
                                                                                                          a range of settings including the unsteady, Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (URANS) simulations of viscous flow past
                                                                                                          the Ahmed body [6, 12]; the detached eddy simulations (DES) of unsteady, turbulent flow past an F-16 Block 40 aircraft
                                                                                                          configuration at high angles of attack [13]; the large eddy simulation (LES) of a turbulent compressible flow over a
                                                                                                          NACA airfoil and the direct numerical simulation (DNS) of the Taylor-Green vortex [14]; the prediction of steady, high
                                                                                                          Reynolds number RANS flows around a shape-parametric NASA Common Research Model [15] (CRM) [16]; and the
                                                                                                          aeroelastic tailoring of a flexible instance of the CRM with 58 structural and aerodynamic shape design parameters [17].
                                                                                                          It is often asserted however that when a PMOR method is applied using the traditional solution approximation on an affine
                                                                                                          subspace, its computational efficiency is challenged by shock-dominated flow problems – particularly when the shocks
                                                                                                          are strong and undergo large motions [18, 19]. Consequently, PMOR methods equipped with alternative, nonlinear
                                                                                                          approximation manifolds have been recently developed to address the presence of shocks and other discontinuities in the
                                                                                                          solution [20, 21]. In most cases, however, the aforementioned assertions and alternative solution approximations have
                                                                                                          been illustrated and demonstrated, respectively, in the context of simple academic problems. In this paper, however, this
                                                                                                          issue is investigated in more depth and in the context of hypersonic flows, where the shock waves are very strong.
                                                                                                               Surely, the hypersonic flow regime has recently gained extensive attention worldwide. While it continues to be
Downloaded by Stanford University on September 7, 2026 | http://arc.aiaa.org | DOI: 10.2514/6.2024-0250




                                                                                                          most relevant to national defense [22], it is also pertinent to manned space exploration [23] and future commercial air
                                                                                                          travel [24]. Furthermore, the testing of a hypersonic system is more challenging and expensive than that of any other
                                                                                                          aerodynamic system, whether performed in a wind tunnel using a scale model or in flight and at full scale. This makes
                                                                                                          CFD most critical today for hypersonic applications. The hypersonic flow regime is typically characterized by five
                                                                                                          distinguishing features: thin shock layers; nonnegligible viscous-inviscid interactions; an entropy layer; high-temperature
                                                                                                          effects and extreme heat transfer; and low-density flows (typically due to re-entry or high altitude) [25]. The requirements
                                                                                                          for capturing and/or resolving each of these features and/or their effects on the application of interest increase the
                                                                                                          complexity of the HDM and thus constitute an additional motivation for PMOR; yet, the robustness, accuracy, and
                                                                                                          computational efficiency of PMOR for hypersonic applications remain to be seen. Indeed, to the authors’ best knowledge,
                                                                                                          the application of PMOR to a hypersonic flow has been discussed so far in the literature only once [26], in the context of
                                                                                                          the Hypersonic International Flight Research Experimentation 1 (HIFiRE-1). However, while the work presented in [26]
                                                                                                          centered around LSPG, it considered only the traditional affine subspace approximation, an ad-hoc approach based on
                                                                                                          clipping functions for achieving nonlinear numerical stability, and a crude hyperreduction procedure (simple collocation
                                                                                                          via random sampling) in the computational grid that furthermore proved to be fragile. Most importantly, it considered a
                                                                                                          limited range for the parameter freestream Mach number (5 ≤ 𝑀∞ ≤ 7) within which the QoIs exhibit little variability
                                                                                                          (in this range, two solution snapshots capture nearly 100% of the singular value energy); was not specific as to how the
                                                                                                          accuracy of the constructed PROMs was assessed; did not incorporate an explicit validation process; and was rather
                                                                                                          pessimistic about the computational efficiency that can be achieved using the described PMOR technology.
                                                                                                               Hence, the main objective of this paper is twofold: to verify, validate, and assess the computational efficiency of a
                                                                                                          state-of-the-art LSPG framework for advanced nonlinear PMOR with and without hyperreduction, for a challenging,
                                                                                                          parametric, steady, hypersonic flow problem where 8 ≤ 𝑀∞ ≤ 13 – namely, the double-cone benchmark problem in
                                                                                                          hypersonic CFD, for which both computational and experimental data is available in the literature [27]; and to discuss,
                                                                                                          through this benchmark problem, the proper assessment of the accuracy of a PROM/HPROM in the presence of flow
                                                                                                          discontinuities and particularly strong shocks. To this end, the remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
                                                                                                               Section II describes the formulation of the CFD-based HDM considered in this work, with particular attention to the
                                                                                                          mitigation of the dreaded carbuncle effect for hypersonic flow computations. Section III overviews a unified LSPG
                                                                                                          framework for PMOR equipped with the finite volume (FV) version [13] of the hyperreduction method ECSW [28]
                                                                                                          (energy-conserving sampling and weighting), in the context of three different approximation manifolds: the traditional
                                                                                                          affine manifold; a quadratic manifold [29]; and an arbitrarily nonlinear manifold constructed using a deep artificial
                                                                                                          neural network (ANN) [30]. Section IV raises the issue of how to meaningfully assess the accuracy of a PROM/HPROM
                                                                                                          in the presence of strong shocks; and addresses this issue by introducing two alternative and/or complementary error
                                                                                                          measures to the infinity, 𝐿 2 , and Euclidean error measures. Section V applies the LSPG framework equipped with
                                                                                                          an adaptive parameter sampling procedure and the three aforementioned solution approximations to the fast solution
                                                                                                          of the parametric double-cone hypersonic benchmark problem. It reports on the verification and validation of the
                                                                                                          HDM and each of the constructed PROMs/HPROMs; on their accuracy using the proper error measures; and on the
                                                                                                          wall-clock time and CPU time speedup factors each constructed PROM/HPROM delivers. Finally, Section VI offers
                                                                                                          some conclusions based on the obtained numerical results.

                                                                                                          shock-capturing finite-volume methods designed to preserve the contact discontinuities – tend to be vulnerable to a
                                                                                                          numerical instability associated with shock-induced anomalies and known as the carbuncle phenomenon [31]. It affects
                                                                                                          the flow and temperature field downstream of the bow shock, all the way to the surface, and thus produces uneven and
                                                                                                          spurious temperature and wall-shear distributions. To combat this phenomenon without introducing excessive numerical
                                                                                                          dissipation, an FV-based scheme based on a Riemann solver can be reconfigured to combine two or three (different)
                                                                                                          convective flux schemes based on (approximate) Riemann solvers; and therefore to be transformed into a flux scheme

                                                                                                          the other and oriented in a dominant upwinding direction that may be chosen to align with the unit normal 𝒏𝑠 to the
                                                                                                          shocks (when they exist), in order to suppress shock instabilities. The other two convective flux schemes should be
                                                                                                          oriented in two directions perpendicular to the dominant upwinding direction. In this work specifically, the semi-discrete
                                                                                                          convective flux 𝚽cv is constructed as a combination of two flux schemes based each on the HLLC (Harten-Lax-van Leer

                                                                                                          ∥𝐶𝑖 ∥ denotes the volume of 𝐶𝑖 , and 𝑚 denotes the number of unknowns defined at each vertex of the CFD grid – e.g.,
                                                                                                          𝑚 = 5 for a laminar viscous flow in three dimensions (e.g., see [36] for a derivation of (3)). Typically, Eq. (3) is
                                                                                                          pseudo-time-integrated using the first-order Backward Euler scheme. At each local time-step, the Newton iterations are

                                                                                                          sufficiently close to the sought-after solution to promote the convergence of Newton’s method by controling the local
                                                                                                          time-step size. At each Newton iteration, the linearized system of algebraic equations is solved using an iterative method.
                                                                                                          In summary, the main idea here is twofold: in the limit when τ𝑖 → ∞, the solution of Eq. (3) converges to the steady
                                                                                                          state solution governed by Eq. (2); and local rather than global (∀𝑖, τ𝑖 = 𝑡) time-stepping accelerates the convergence of
                                                                                                          the computed solution to the steady state solution (e.g., see [37] for a convergence analysis).

                                                                                                          B. Hyperreduction Using ECSW
                                                                                                              Independently of the chosen solution approximation (4), the complexity of the solution of problem (5) scales not
                                                                                                          only with the small dimension 𝑛 of the PROM but also with the large dimension 𝑁 of the HDM. Hyperreduction [11] is
                                                                                                          one effective approach for eliminating this computational bottleneck. Here, the version of ECSW [40] tailored in [13] for
                                                                                                          Petrov-Galerkin PROMs is chosen for hyperreducing any of the nonlinear PROMs outlined above, because of its track
                                                                                                          record established in [13, 40] for many different large-scale CFD applications. The main idea of this hyperreduction
                                                                                                          method is to approximate a projected quantity or PROM such as (5) using a cubature approach whose complexity is

                                                                                                                                IV. Error Measures in the Presence of Flow Discontinuities

                                                                                                          A. Basic Issue
                                                                                                               For a hypersonic flow problem, measuring an approximation error – for example, the error of a (generic)
                                                                                                          PROM/HPROM-based solution relative to its HDM-based reference – is complicated by the presence of numerous
                                                                                                          solution discontinuities at various locations in Ω𝐹 . At such locations, the strengths of the discontinuities can be very
                                                                                                          large. For example, the pressure may increase by a factor of 29 across a normal shock with a pre-shock Mach number of
                                                                                                          5: in this case, a tiny error in the PROM/HPROM-based prediction of a shock location – for example, one grid point
                                                                                                          away from the counterpart location predicted using the HDM – can lead to a relative error of 2,900% at that location.
                                                                                                          For this reason, measuring the accuracy of a PROM/HPROM-based hypersonic flow solution using the infinity norm
                                                                                                          (∥ ∥ ∞ ) can be misleading. Measuring it using the 𝐿 2 or Euclidean norm may mitigate this issue: However, it can be
                                                                                                          either impractical, if one has to avoid the locations of discontinuities in Ω𝐹 ; or still misrepresentative of an otherwise
                                                                                                          accurate PROM/HPROM approximation. From these observations, it follows that for hypersonic applications, a suitable
                                                                                                          error measure is one which, in addition to the standard quantitative criteria, reflects a reasonable qualitative assessment


                                                                                                                                                                      11
                                                                                                          of the accuracy of a numerical approximation; and discerns between truly better or worse approximations, consistently
                                                                                                          with apparent, visual comparisons (when these are possible).
                                                                                                              Different approaches can be adopted to address the issue outlined above. To begin, the computational fluid domain
                                                                                                          Ω𝐹 can be decomposed into regions that contain solution discontinuities, which are collectively referred to here as Ω𝐹𝑑 ;
                                                                                                          and the complementary region Ω𝑠𝐹 = Ω𝐹 \Ω𝐹𝑑 , where the solution is smooth. Then, a local measure of the error can
                                                                                                          be computed in each subdomain to enable informative and precise accuracy assessments. In this case, the size of Ω𝐹𝑑
                                                                                                          can be tailored to account for the importance of the shock location (and shape) relative to that of other flow features,
                                                                                                          which is application dependent. The 𝐿 2 norm, its discrete counterpart the Euclidean norm, or any other conventional or
                                                                                                          preferred norm can be applied in Ω𝑠𝐹 ; but an alternative or complementary error measure that addresses the complication
                                                                                                          outlined above is desirable in Ω𝐹𝑑 . Here, two such measures are proposed. In addition to the usual quantitative criteria,
                                                                                                          both measures are shown in Section V to reflect a reasonable qualitative assessment of the accuracy of a numerical
                                                                                                          approximation in the presence of shocks, and to discern between truly better or worse approximations consistently with
                                                                                                          visual comparisons. However, both proposed error measures are one-dimensional in the sense that their support is
                                                                                                          one-dimensional – for example, a line Γℓ that cuts through Ω𝐹 . Nevertheless, such supports are often used in practice to
Downloaded by Stanford University on September 7, 2026 | http://arc.aiaa.org | DOI: 10.2514/6.2024-0250




                                                                                                          analyze in detail the variations of QoIs such as pressure, velocity, and other flow and/or flow-related variables along
                                                                                                          direction(s) or path(s) of the computational fluid domain that are most important for the given application.
                                                                                                              REMARK 3. It is noted that computing the global error in Ω𝐹 of a numerical approximation using the 𝐿 2 or
                                                                                                          Euclidean norm is often more practical than computing the accuracy of the approximation using the partitioned approach
                                                                                                          outlined above, particularly in the presence of numerous shocks – as in the case of the parametric double-cone hypersonic
                                                                                                          benchmark problem considered in this paper. Still, computing the global error in the presence of strong shocks must be
                                                                                                          performed with care. For external flows, it may require sizing appropriately Ω𝐹 to enable a relevant assessment of the
                                                                                                          global accuracy of a numerical approximation using the 𝐿 2 or Euclidean norm. For this reason, both error measures
                                                                                                          proposed below are appropriate alternative and/or complementary measures.


                                                                                                          B. Dynamic Time Warping
                                                                                                              In the spirit of building off of the classic 𝐿 2 norm, but also considering spatial effects such as those associated
                                                                                                          with solution discontinuities, dynamic time-warping (DTW) [41] offers an alternative and more relevant distance-like
                                                                                                          measure. Developed primarily for aligning time signals with different frequencies or speeds, it can also be applied to
                                                                                                          compare signals defined in a one-dimensional spatial domain, as described below.
                                                                                                              Let 𝒒 ∈ R ⊂ R 𝑁 denote a subset of R 𝑁 identifying a discrete, vector-valued QoI defined along a one-dimensional
                                                                                                          path P within a given three-dimensional CFD grid. The DTW algorithm stretches two different such vectors – for
                                                                                                          example, 𝒒 PROM ∈ R and 𝒒 HDM ∈ R – onto a common set of discrete entities of P – for example, grid points of the
                                                                                                          CFD grid or point locations in Ω𝐹 – such that the 𝐿 2 norm of the difference (𝒒 PROM − 𝒒 HDM ) is minimized, which
                                                                                                          defines the DTW distance-like measure dtw(𝒒 PROM , 𝒒 HDM ) between 𝒒 PROM and 𝒒 HDM . In this context, “stretching”
                                                                                                          means that the DTW algorithm repeats each component of each vector as many times as necessary to achieve the
                                                                                                          aforementioned objective, but does not delete or reorder any component; and each component of a vector-valued QoI 𝒒
                                                                                                          represents or pertains to a flow variable or quantity. Then, the relative error associated with the PROM/HPROM-based
                                                                                                          prediction of 𝒒 is measured as follows
                                                                                                                                                                            dtw (𝒒 PROM/HPROM , 𝒒 HDM )
                                                                                                                                         𝜀 DTW (𝒒 PROM/HPROM , 𝒒 HDM ) =
                                                                                                                                                                                     ∥𝒒 HDM ∥ 2
                                                                                                          It follows that, by construction, 𝜀 DTW (𝒒 PROM/HPROM , 𝒒 HDM ) ≤ 𝜀 𝐿 2 (𝒒 PROM/HPROM , 𝒒 HDM ).
                                                                                                               In the above, the wording “distance-like” measure is due to the fact that the measure provided by DTW does not
                                                                                                          necessarily verify the triangular inequality; thus, strictly speaking, it does not define a norm. However, in the specific
                                                                                                          context of solutions with discontinuities, it provides an alternative to the infinity, 𝐿 2 , and Euclidean norms that attempts
                                                                                                          to eliminate the distance between the true and predicted shock locations prior to computing the norm of the error. Hence,
                                                                                                          when a PROM/HPROM perfectly predicts pre- and post-shock flow quantities but mislocates shocks, 𝜀 DTW approaches
                                                                                                          the zero value. Consequently, DTW does not risk overestimating the error in the vicinity of a solution discontinuity due
                                                                                                          to a relatively small mislocation of the discontinuity; it can only tone down the effect of the mislocation.

                                                                                                                                  V. Verification, Validation, and Performance Assessment
                                                                                                              The unified LSPG framework for PMOR equipped with all three solution approximations outlined in (6), (8),
                                                                                                          and (10), and with ECSW for hyperreduction, was implemented over several years in the massively parallel compressible
                                                                                                          flow solver AERO-F [45]. Here, it is applied, together with the high-dimensional computational capabilities of AERO-F,
                                                                                                          to the numerical solution of a variable speed, hypersonic, steady flow problem past a double-cone obstacle.
                                                                                                              All HDM-based computations discussed below are performed using a second-order finite volume discretization
                                                                                                          in space, where second-order accuracy is attained using a MUSCL-type linear reconstruction scheme equipped with
                                                                                                          the Venkatakrishnan limiter [35]; and in double-precision floating-point arithmetic on 144 cores of a Linux cluster,
                                                                                                          where each compute node has two six-core Intel® Xeon® Gold 6128 processors running at 3.40 GHz and 192 GB of
                                                                                                          memory. In this case, all steady state solutions are computed by discretizing (3) using the implicit backward Euler
                                                                                                          scheme equipped with a domain-decomposition-based Newton-Krylov method grounded in the Generalized Minimal
                                                                                                          RESidual (GMRES) linear solver; this iterative solver is preconditioned by the restricted additive Schwartz method
                                                                                                          based on an incomplete lower-upper ILU(1) factorization [46] .
                                                                                                              All counterpart HPROM-based computations are also executed in double-precision floating-point arithmetic, but on
                                                                                                          a single core of the same Linux cluster. In this case, all steady state solutions are computed first by solving (5) or (11)
Downloaded by Stanford University on September 7, 2026 | http://arc.aiaa.org | DOI: 10.2514/6.2024-0250




                                                                                                          using Newton’s method equipped with the initialization procedure described in Section III.C and a direct linear equation
                                                                                                          solver; and then by reconstructing the high-dimensional approximations using (6), (8), or (10), as desired.


                                                                                                          A. Double-Cone Hypersonic Benchmark Problem and Parameterization
                                                                                                               The three-dimensional double-cone hypersonic problem considered here is the benchmark hypersonic steady flow
                                                                                                          problem described, for example, in [27], for which wind tunnel and reference computational data are available in the
                                                                                                          literature. Its axisymmetric geometry is illustrated in Figure 1(a), where the axis of symmetry is represented by the
                                                                                                          green dashed line. The fore-cone has a half-angle of 25◦ and the aft-cone has a half-angle of 55◦ . The nose of the
                                                                                                          fore-cone is blunted, with a radius of 0.25 in (6.35 mm). The lengths and heights of each individual cone and those of
                                                                                                          the whole double-cone are annotated in the same Figure 1(a).

                                                                                                          Table 1. The working gas used in the wind tunnel experiment was Nitrogen, which is modeled here as a perfect gas with

                                                                                                          location. Aft of the transmitted shock begins a supersonic shear layer that contains reflections of alternating shock and

                                                                                                          the recirculation region at the cone junction and that within the shear layer, and the alternating reflections of shock and
                                                                                                          expansion waves in the shear layer are highly nonlinear. Therefore, once parameterized, the double-cone hypersonic flow

                                                                                                          This Mach interval is sufficiently large for realistically assessing the performance of a PMOR method. Indeed, Figure 4
                                                                                                          of Section V.C shows that in this Mach interval, the flow features move and significantly change with 𝑀∞ .


                                                                                                          B. Construction, Verification, and Validation of a CFD-based Viscous HDM
                                                                                                              Since the full three-dimensional geometry of the double-cone is axisymmetric, AERO-F is a three-dimensional flow
                                                                                                          solver, and all incoming and outgoing solution waves of this problem are supersonic, Ω𝐹 is chosen to be a sector with
                                                                                                          one degree angle about the axis of symmetry, and farfield boundaries that are not located at any significant distance
                                                                                                          away from the body surface. To resolve the dominant flow features when 𝑀∞ is varied within Ω 𝜇 = [8, 13], this
                                                                                                          computational fluid domain is discretized by 650 885 tetrahedra with a single layer of elements across the sector, as
                                                                                                          follows. This layer of elements connects a structured grid with 512 grid points along the body surface and 128 grid
                                                                                                          points into the freestream, and a similar grid is obtained by extrusion of the aforementioned grid. Hence, the total
                                                                                                          number of distinct grid points is 130 944. The flow is assumed to be laminar and thus the dimension of the HDM is
                                                                                                          𝑁 = 654 720 (five unknowns per grid point). The initial off-wall grid spacing is 𝑦 1 = 5 × 10−5 m; the steady growth of
                                                                                                          this spacing from the wall towards the inlet boundary is graphically depicted in Figure 2(b).
                                                                                                              Symmetry boundary conditions are prescribed on both lateral boundary surfaces of Ω𝐹 . Also, in concurrence with
                                                                                                          previously published computational work, the isothermal boundary condition 𝑇wall = 297 K is applied on the wall
                                                                                                          boundary of Ω𝐹 [27].
                                                                                                              For the freestream flow conditions given in Table 1, the HDM solution computed by AERO-F is verified and validated
                                                                                                          here using the computational and experimental results given in [27], respectively. It is noted however that although the
                                                                                                          geometry and flow conditions considered here are identical to those considered in [27], the computational results are not
                                                                                                          expected to be strictly identical to those published in that reference. This is because in [27], the double-cone hypersonic
                                                                                                          problem was solved using a truly two-dimensional axisymmetric flow solver equipped with adaptive grid refinement;
                                                                                                          the two-dimensional computational domain was discretized by quadrilateral cells; and parallelepipeds are generally
                                                                                                          preferable to tetrahedra for semi-discretizing hypersonic flow computations.

                                                                                                                                                     VI. Summary and Conclusions
                                                                                                               During the last decade, the least-squares Petrov-Galerkin (LSPG) method equipped with a piecewise affine
                                                                                                          approximation and with the ECSW method for hyperreduction has emerged as a state-of-the-art nonlinear projection-
                                                                                                          based model order reduction (PMOR) method for accelerating the solution of parametric, convection-dominated, viscous
                                                                                                          flow problems. Originally developed in the context of the approximation of the solution on an affine subspace, it was
                                                                                                          recently extended to two other approximation manifolds, namely, a quadratic manifold and an arbitrarily nonlinear
                                                                                                          manifold constructed using a deep artificial neural network (ANN). This paper unifies all three variants of LSPG into a
                                                                                                          single LSPG framework for PMOR and assesses the potential of this computational framework for accelerating the
                                                                                                          solution of hypersonic flow problems while maintaining a desired level of accuracy. To this end, the paper raises first
                                                                                                          the issue of how to meaningfully assess the accuracy of a solution approximation in the presence of strong shocks;
                                                                                                          and addresses this issue by introducing two alternative and/or complementary measures to the traditional 𝐿 2 norm or
                                                                                                          its discrete counterpart: an error measure based on the dynamic time warping algorithm for evaluating the similarity
                                                                                                          between two signals; and another error measure based on the quadratic Wasserstein metric. Specifically, the paper
                                                                                                          evaluates the potential of the unified LSPG framework for PMOR that it presents on a variable speed (𝑀∞ ∈ [8, 13])
                                                                                                          version of the double-cone benchmark problem in hypersonic CFD. This benchmark steady flow problem features
Downloaded by Stanford University on September 7, 2026 | http://arc.aiaa.org | DOI: 10.2514/6.2024-0250




                                                                                                          attached and detached shocks; a separation shock; a transmitted shock; multiple shock-shock interactions; a recirculation
                                                                                                          region at the intersection of the conical surfaces; and a shear layer of supersonic shock and expansion wave reflections
                                                                                                          along the rear cone surface. Hence, it is representative of challenging hypersonic flow problems. It is found that all
                                                                                                          instances of the unified LSPG framework for PMOR are capable of accurately predicting the hypersonic flow behavior
                                                                                                          on the surface of the double-cone and within the computational fluid domain surrounding it. It is also found that LSPG
                                                                                                          configured with the approximation of the solution on an affine subspace or a quadratic manifold and with ECSW for
                                                                                                          hyperreduction accelerates the solution of the high-dimensional CFD problem (HDM) by more than two orders of
                                                                                                          magnitude and reduces the required amount of CPU time by more than four orders of magnitude while maintaining
                                                                                                          desired levels of accuracy and fidelity. Most importantly, it is found that when configured with the approximation of the
                                                                                                          solution on an arbitrarily nonlinear manifold constructed using a deep ANN, LSPG accelerates the HDM-based flow
                                                                                                          solution by more than three orders of magnitude and reduces the required amount of CPU time by more than five orders
                                                                                                          of magnitude, also while maintaining desired levels of accuracy and fidelity. All these results suggest a strong potential
                                                                                                          of LSPG for accelerating the preliminary and detailed design and analysis of hypersonic systems.
