1. Introduction

    Heterogeneous materials have become a cornerstone of modern engineering applications [1–3]. Yet, in contrast to their ho-
mogeneous counterparts, they remain poorly understood. Such materials can exhibit highly nonlinear stress-strain relationships,
path-dependence, and complex failure mechanisms that existing homogeneous theories or models fail to capture. As a result, fully
exploiting their superior properties poses signiﬁcant scientiﬁc and engineering challenges.
    A particularly important class of heterogeneous materials is woven fabrics, which play a central role in aerospace engineering as
the canopy broadcloth of parachutes. For instance, woven fabrics have been used in the supersonic Disk-Gap-Band (DGB) parachutes
that NASA has deployed to land rovers on Mars [4–6]. Despite numerous successful deployments, parachute failures have been
reported where the broadcloth ruptured at loads well below its design capacity [7]. These incidents underscore the critical need for
a deeper understanding of the mechanical behavior of woven fabrics.
    Like many heterogeneous materials, woven fabrics possess inherent ﬁne-scale structures. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the broadcloth
of the 16 meter diameter Mars 2020 parachute canopy consists of ﬁber bundles woven into yarns, with microscale features on
the order of micrometers. These structures give rise to highly complex and nonlinear behavior, including compressive weakness,
tensile stiﬀening, shear locking, biaxial tensile coupling, anisotropy, and path-dependence. Capturing these eﬀects in simulations

requires accounting for ﬁne-scale structures. However, direct numerical resolution is computationally prohibitive: a fully resolved
discretization of the Mars 2020 DGB canopy is estimated to require 2 × 1015 solid elements — far beyond practical computational or
storage limits. Consequently, woven fabrics provide both a clear motivation for multiscale homogenization and an ideal candidate
for its application.
    Homogenization is widely employed in solid mechanics to capture the inﬂuence of ﬁne-scale structures on macroscopic behavior
without explicitly resolving the small scales [8,9]. A prominent paradigm is the locally attached microstructure concept [10], which
associates each macroscopic material point with a localized microscale model. This principle underlies the Finite Element Squared
(FE2 ) framework [11,12], wherein the macroscale response is governed by concurrent microscale FE simulations performed on Rep-
resentative Volume Elements (RVEs). Prior studies have applied FE2 to dry woven fabrics, but most were restricted to yarn-level
resolution at the ﬁner scale and to elastic material behavior [13–16], thereby neglecting the intra-ﬁber interactions and viscoelastic
eﬀects that strongly inﬂuence fabric response. Other works incorporated ﬁber-level resolution at the ﬁnest scale [17,18], typically
leveraging beam element formulations, but were likewise limited to elastic responses.
    Despite its versatility, FE2 remains computationally prohibitive for anything beyond small-scale problems. The high cost stems
from the need to repeatedly solve Boundary Value Problems (BVPs) on the microscale RVE, with complexity worsening in multiscale
settings where the number of required BVP solves increases exponentially with the number of scales.
    To address this challenge, considerable eﬀort has been devoted to accelerating FE2 , particularly for two-scale problems. Prominent
strategies include Projection-based Model Order Reduction (PMOR) [19,20], polynomial regression [14], reduced cubature [21],
Artiﬁcial Neural Networks (ANNs) [22], Mechanics-Informed Artiﬁcial Neural Networks (MI-ANNs) [15,23], convolutional neural
networks [24], and transformer architectures [25]. While these techniques enable rapid online evaluations, they predominantly still
depend on expensive oﬄine data generation – a process that becomes prohibitive for large RVE discretizations or when multiple scales
are involved. Recently, some attention has turned to accelerating FE2 speciﬁcally in the context of three or more scales [26,27].
    This paper aims to develop a computationally eﬃcient framework for the multiscale homogenization of heterogeneous, path-
dependent materials by combining MI-ANNs [15,23] with a staggered training procedure. Complex multiscale problems are decom-
posed into successive two-scale subproblems, with the scales bridged through neural network surrogate models that, by construction,
satisfy fundamental laws of continuum mechanics, including various stability criteria, the Clausius-Duhem principle, fading mem-
ory, zero-stress conditions, objectivity, material non-inversion, and material symmetries. The staggered training strategy ensures that
the oﬄine computational cost grows linearly with the number of scales, rather than exponentially, making the framework signiﬁ-
cantly more eﬃcient than conventional nested multiscale FE methods. The proposed approach is developed and demonstrated in the
context of woven fabrics, accurately capturing viscoelastic and ﬁber-resolved behaviors-such as rate-dependent hysteresis, biaxial
tensile coupling, and warp-weft asymmetry-while retaining computational eﬃciency. The results show that the framework achieves
high-ﬁdelity predictions comparable to fully resolved models derived from real-material imaging, establishing a versatile and general
methodology for modeling complex, many-scale materials.
    For clarity of exposition, the mesoscale/microscale terminology used throughout this work is deﬁned as follows: an RVE is referred
to as mesoscale if it resolves the yarns as the smallest structural entity of a woven fabric, and as microscale if it resolves the individual
ﬁbers comprising the yarns.
    The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the fundamentals of FE2 , yarn-resolved homogenization
of woven fabrics, and surrogate-based acceleration. Section 3 introduces the two-scale and three-scale ﬁber-resolved models, along
with the staggered training procedure. Section 4 presents the oﬄine training and online performance assessment on a fabric coupon
test case as well as a large-scale parachute inﬂation problem. Finally, Section 5 provides concluding remarks.


2. Background

2.1. Computational homogenization of continua with locally attached microstructures

    In solid mechanics, homogenization captures the inﬂuence of ﬁne-scale structures on macroscale behavior without explicitly
resolving them in the macroscale discretization. A widely used class of homogenization schemes relies on the concept of the locally
attached microstructure [10].

2.1.2. The FE2 method
    The FE2 method [12] is a computational homogenization framework founded on the locally attached microstructure concept. At
the coarse scale, the material is discretized using an FE model, and each integration point is endowed with a locally attached ﬁne-scale
RVE, which is itself modeled using ﬁnite elements. This results in a multi-level concurrent FE formulation involving nested solves,
driven by the localization and homogenization rules outlined above.
    The solution procedure begins at scale 𝑘 = 0, where boundary conditions are speciﬁed by the physical problem. At each solution
step (time step or iteration, e.g., in a Newton-Raphson method), the local deformation at each integration point is transmitted to
the attached RVE at scale 𝑘 + 1 to deﬁne its boundary conditions. This process is repeated recursively until the ﬁnest scale 𝑘 = 𝑁𝑠 ,
where the constitutive law is explicitly known and the boundary value problem can be solved. Information is then passed upward:
homogenized stresses from the RVE at scale 𝑘 are transmitted to the corresponding integration point at scale 𝑘 − 1, enabling the
coarse-scale problem to advance. This top-down and bottom-up exchange is repeated at each solution step, with partial intermediate
passes when iterative or multirate time-integration strategies are required.

2.2. Two-scale yarn-resolved model for woven fabric

    Two-scale computational homogenization of plain woven fabrics has been studied previously [13–16,18]. Of particular relevance
to this work is the two-scale model described in [14,15], in which a yarn-resolved RVE of type F-111 nylon fabric was attached to
macroscale membrane elements, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The minimal repeatable unit cell comprised four interwoven yarns, each
discretized with solid elements and modeled as homogeneous, isotropic, elastic nylon. Yarn-yarn interactions were represented via
frictionless contact, and PBCs were applied, though not via the reference-node method described in Section 2.1.2. The full FE model
of the RVE contained 1 024 elements.
    To ensure compatibility between the homogenized stress tensor and the plane-stress assumption of the macroscale membrane, a
Newton-Raphson-like iterative scheme was used, following [32], to enforce vanishing out-of-plane stresses. This procedure delivered
an eﬀective mapping between in-plane strains and in-plane stresses.
    The primary motivation for this modeling eﬀort was to enable the homogenization of canopy fabric in simulations of the inﬂation
of a 16 m diameter supersonic parachute. A fully resolved simulation of such a parachute is estimated to require approximately 2 × 1015
elements, rendering it utterly intractable. By contrast, the homogenized model reduced the problem to roughly 270 000 macroscale
membrane elements.
    Although this represents a substantial reduction in computational cost, the problem remained far from tractable. Solving approxi-
mately 270 000 RVE problems at every macroscale time step would require nearly 500 years of wall-clock time on 96 computing cores
for just one second of simulation time. This highlights the critical need to accelerate the homogenization process.

2.3. Accelerated homogenization using mechanics-informed artiﬁcial neural networks

    To overcome the computational intractability highlighted in Section 2.2, it is essential to eliminate the need for a prohibitively
large number of online BVP solves, whose cost scales with both the size of the macroscale FE model and the number of solution
steps. The remedy is to adopt an oﬄine/online procedure: surrogate models are trained oﬄine at a ﬁxed cost and then evaluated
rapidly online during the macroscale simulation, thereby replacing the expensive FE2 process with a more computationally eﬃcient
framework.
    Surrogate models in this context can be broadly classiﬁed into two categories. The ﬁrst comprises internal representations,
which approximate the full numerical simulation (and hence the underlying physical governing equations) by projecting the high-
dimensional state vector into a reduced latent space. PMOR exempliﬁes this approach: it constructs reduced bases from snapshots
of the high-dimensional solution and has been successfully applied to accelerate FE2 homogenization [19,33,34]. Although physics-
based, these methods can remain unnecessarily costly for problems where only speciﬁc quantities of interest (QoIs) are required, and
they often require intrusive modiﬁcations of the governing equations.
    The second category consists of external representations, which directly approximate the mapping between input parameters and
QoIs without reconstructing the full solution. In homogenization, this amounts to approximating the functional mapping between
the strain history and the stress response at scale 𝑘, eﬀectively serving as a constitutive law. For example, [14] employed ANNs to
map strain to stress in the nonlinear elastic setting. This approach is highly eﬃcient, but its neglect of governing physics can lead to
violations of essential mechanical principles.
    The frontier of surrogate modeling therefore lies in physics-informed external representations: QoI-driven surrogates that embed
mechanical constraints [35–38]. MI-ANNs, introduced by the authors in the elastic [15] and viscoelastic [23] contexts, illustrate this
paradigm. MI-ANNs are form-agnostic, capable of representing highly nonlinear constitutive mappings, and constrained by construc-
tion to satisfy essential physical principles, including stability, material frame indiﬀerence, energy consistency, the second law of
thermodynamics, rigid-body invariance, and symmetry requirements where relevant.
    The training of MI-ANNs is based on data generated by expensive FE2 simulations, conducted oﬄine using simpliﬁed “coupon
tests.” In these tests, the macroscale consists of a single element with a single integration point, constrained so that all relevant
strain components can be realized. Repeated homogenization over prescribed displacement histories produces pairs of strain-stress
trajectories, {𝑬(𝑡𝑚 ), 𝑺(𝑡𝑚 )}𝑚 , which form the training data. Once trained, the MI-ANN surrogate replaces the ﬁne-scale RVE solves

3. Fiber-resolved computational homogenization of fabric

    The two-scale yarn-resolved homogenized model introduced in [14], described in Section 2.2 and illustrated in Fig. 2, success-
fully captures certain key characteristics of fabric behavior, such as compressive and shear weakness. However, it also suﬀers from
signiﬁcant limitations that diminish its accuracy in representing the true mechanical behavior of woven fabrics.

F. As’ad and C. Farhat                                                      Computer Methods in Applied Mechanics and Engineering 452 (2026) 118666

 • Viscoelastic behavior: Woven nylon fabrics exhibit viscoelasticity arising from polymeric dissipation within the ﬁbers and intra-ﬁber
   friction [41]. The model outlined in Section 2.2 is formulated within an elastic framework and therefore cannot capture hysteresis
   or other path-dependent eﬀects.
 • Yarn heterogeneity and anisotropy: Yarns are heterogeneous, anisotropic bundles of ﬁbers with well-deﬁned directionality. The

   two-scale yarn-resolved model outlined in Section 2.2 assumes an isotropic, homogeneous constitutive law at the yarn level,
   and therefore cannot capture the inherently heterogeneous and anisotropic response of the yarns. Compared to a homogeneous
   yarn, a ﬁber bundle has lower bending stiﬀness [42] and oﬀers negligible resistance to transverse tensile strains, as the ﬁbers are
   unbonded. Additionally, because ﬁbers are not tightly packed in the undeformed state, the bundle exhibits minimal transverse
   compressive resistance until the ﬁbers become closely packed.
 • Warp-weft asymmetry: Woven fabrics typically display distinct tensile stiﬀnesses along warp and weft directions [43]. The model

   described in Section 2.2 uses an RVE with four identical yarns based on nominal measurements, resulting in complete symmetry
   and failing to capture these diﬀerences. In reality, yarn cross-sectional shapes and crimp vary according to ﬁber arrangement,
   which is determined by the manufacturing process.
 • Biaxial tensile coupling: Plain-woven fabrics often exhibit strong biaxial tensile coupling: an increase in strain along one direction

   signiﬁcantly raises the tensile stiﬀness in the perpendicular direction [44]. This is primarily due to the phenomena of de-crimping
   and crimp interchange between perpendicular ﬁbers [45]. The two-scale yarn-resolved model described in Section 2.2, however,
   does not capture this eﬀect, as the axial stresses respond only to their corresponding axial strains.

     These limitations motivate the development of a higher-ﬁdelity, ﬁber-resolved model for woven fabric homogenization.


3.1. Two-scale ﬁber-resolved model

    To overcome the limitations of yarn-level homogenization, we enhance model ﬁdelity by introducing ﬁber-level resolution within a
viscoelastic framework. Speciﬁcally, we employ a ﬁber-resolved geometry of the broadcloth material used in the Mars 2020 parachute,
provided by the authors of [46]. The fabric, custom-developed for the Mars 2020 parachute project by Heathcoat Fabrics [47], consists
of plain-woven yarns, each composed of bundles of nylon 6,6 ﬁbers with a nominal diameter of 20.4 μm. The provided geometry
represents a 0.57 mm × 0.55 mm × 0.21 mm region of interest – suﬃcient to capture a single repeating unit of the weave – and was
reconstructed from X-ray microtomographic imaging using a ﬁber segmentation algorithm [48]. The microtomography data and
the corresponding reconstructed geometry are shown in Fig. 3. The resulting ﬁber-resolved model comprises 80 ﬁbers in total: 40
nominally aligned with the warp direction and 40 with the weft. This ﬁner-scale representation enables the explicit capture of the
heterogeneous architecture and intra-ﬁber mechanisms that govern the nonlinear, path-dependent response of woven fabrics.
    Nonetheless, due to the ﬁnite resolution of the microtomography, the ﬁber segmentation procedure signiﬁcantly underestimates
the nominal cross-section of each ﬁber relative to the known diameter of 20.4 μm. In addition, the inherent randomness of the ﬁber
paths renders the geometry non-periodic, preventing the straightforward application of PBCs. To address these issues, we propose
a constrained optimization approach that seeks the minimal smooth distortion of the geometry such that it becomes periodic, each
ﬁber attains the correct radius, and no ﬁbers interpenetrate. The formulation and solution procedure for this optimization problem
are detailed in Appendix A. A comparison of the altered and original geometries is shown in Fig. 4.
    A solid-element FE discretization of the ﬁber-resolved geometry is constructed, with each element assigned a Mooney-Rivlin
constitutive law augmented by a three-term Prony series, calibrated from relaxation tests of nylon 6,6 ﬁbers reported in [49]. Fiber-
to-ﬁber interactions are modeled via Coulomb frictional contact, with a constant friction coeﬃcient of 0.37 corresponding to nylon-
on-nylon values [50]. It is worth noting that this frictional contact does not introduce any residual stresses into the RVE, as the ﬁbers
are suﬃciently spaced to avoid contact in the reference conﬁguration. PBCs are imposed on the nodes at the RVE boundaries.

dataset using the two-scale ﬁber-resolved model to train a surrogate is estimated to require approximately 130 days on 96 computing
cores.

3.2. Three-scale ﬁber-resolved model

    The prohibitive training cost of the two-scale ﬁber-resolved model arises from its RVE being itself a multiscale structure, containing
both the yarn and ﬁber scales. This dual-scale nature necessitates an extremely ﬁne discretization, resulting in a large number of
elements and DoFs. This outcome is not unexpected, since woven fabric is inherently a three-scale material system – comprising the
membrane, yarn, and ﬁber scales-as illustrated in Fig. 6.
    To achieve a homogenized model with ﬁber-level ﬁdelity at the ﬁnest scale while alleviating the computational burden of the
two-scale ﬁber-resolved model, we therefore propose a three-scale homogenization framework. The goal of this approach is to fully
separate the scales: membrane resolution at the macroscale, yarn resolution at the mesoscale, and ﬁber resolution at the microscale.
In this way, each RVE is restricted to a single distinct scale.
    The mesoscale RVE consists of four plain-woven yarns, each representing a ﬁber bundle, similar to that of the two-scale yarn-
resolved model described in Section 2.2. Unlike that model’s RVE, however, the geometry of each yarn is informed by Mars 2020
material imaging: the cross-sections of each yarn are computed as the best-ﬁt superellipses [51] of the corresponding ﬁber-bundle
cross-sections. As a result, the yarns are generally distinct from one another and vary in cross section along their respective paths.
On average, their height and width are 60 μm and 200 μm, respectively.
    Each yarn is discretized with solid elements whose constitutive behavior is derived from BVP solutions on the microscale RVE.
Since the microscale RVE response is anisotropic, material frames aligned with the yarn paths are assigned to the solid elements.
Yarn-to-yarn interactions are modeled through frictional contact, and PBCs are applied to the boundary nodes. The plane-stress
requirement on the homogenized stress tensor is satisﬁed automatically using the reference node method described in Section 2.1.2.
The resulting mesoscale RVE contains 2 832 solid elements, 423 LMPCs, 3 024 contact surface elements, and 12 822 DoFs.
    The microscale RVE consists of a single central ﬁber segment modeled as a complete cylinder, surrounded by four neighboring
ﬁber segments, each represented as a quarter cylinder. All ﬁbers have a diameter of 20.4 μm. The ﬁber spacing is chosen such that

3.3. Computationally tractable staggered training procedure

    The preceding discussion highlights that, despite the reduction in RVE DoFs, the conventional three-scale model remains compu-
tationally prohibitive due to the exponential growth in the number of BVP solves. Clearly, any practical multiscale homogenization
scheme must circumvent this bottleneck, especially as the number of scales increases. To address this challenge, we propose a staggered
training approach that dramatically accelerates homogenization of problems with many scales by eliminating the nested structure of
FE2 BVP solves. In essence, it decomposes an (𝑁𝑠 + 1)-scale homogenization problem into 𝑁𝑠 successive two-scale problems, bridged
by economical surrogate models. When only two scales are present, the method reduces to the surrogate-based homogenization
strategy described in Section 2.3.
    Concretely, consider an (𝑁𝑠 + 1)-scale system with scales 𝑘 = 0, … , 𝑁𝑠 . The staggered procedure begins at 𝑘 = 𝑁𝑠 − 1, where a
two-scale problem is solved between a single-element coupon at level 𝑘 and an RVE at level 𝑘 + 1 under a variety of time-varying

boundary conditions. This generates time-labeled strain-stress data, from which a surrogate   ̃ 𝑘 is trained to map the strain history
at level 𝑘 to the corresponding stress. The surrogate then serves as the constitutive law for the material at level 𝑘 in the two-scale
problem coupling a coupon at level 𝑘 − 1 with the RVE at level 𝑘. This process is repeated recursively until the macroscale (𝑘 = 0)
is reached, ultimately producing a mapping between macroscale strain history and stress that accounts for all ﬁner-scale eﬀects. A
similar approach was recently proposed in [26] for path-independent material responses and monotonic loading paths, employing
standard deep feedforward neural networks as surrogate models.
    The key advantage is that the number of BVP solves now grows linearly with the number of scales, rather than exponentially.
Moreover, the dominant cost stems from generating training data and ﬁtting surrogate models, which is independent of the number
of elements or steps in the ﬁnal macroscale simulation. Instead, the cost is tied to the complexity of the constitutive relation being
learned: more intricate behaviors require more data. Since MI-ANNs have proven robust to sparse training data, the amount of data
needed is expected to remain modest, making this approach genuinely eﬃcient.
    The only approximation relative to classical concurrent FE2 arises from replacing direct localization and homogenization of each
ﬁne-scale RVE with its surrogate. If a surrogate reproduces the exact constitutive response of its RVE, the macroscale predictions are
identical to those of FE2 , but at a fraction of the computational cost. Conversely, any surrogate error at level 𝑘 propagates upward
through all coarser levels 𝑘′ < 𝑘, potentially accumulating at the macroscale. Furthermore, each surrogate model must satisfy stability
conditions, grounded in fundamental principles of continuum mechanics, to be reliably used as a constitutive law in the coarser-scale
simulation. Consequently, the success of this framework critically depends on the expressivity, generalization capacity, and physical
admissibility of the surrogate models at each scale – all of which are ensured by MI-ANNs.
    The staggered training procedure, as applied to the three-scale woven fabric model, is illustrated in Fig. 8. At the meso-micro
stage, the microscale ﬁber-bundle RVE is coupled to a single tetrahedral coupon with one integration point, where six independent
displacements control the six unique components of the strain tensor. By varying these displacements over time and solving the
corresponding BVPs on the microscale RVE, homogenized mesoscale data is generated. A mesoscale surrogate model, implemented
as a MI-ANN, is then trained on this data.
    Next, the trained mesoscale surrogate is used in the macro-meso homogenization problem as the constitutive law for the solid
elements in the meso-scale yarn-level RVE. This RVE is coupled to a coupon with a single membrane element and one integration
point, where three independent displacements prescribe the in-plane strain components. As in the meso-micro stage, time-varying
displacements are applied to generate homogenized macroscale data, from which a macroscale MI-ANN surrogate model is trained.
This surrogate provides a constitutive law at the macroscale that consistently incorporates the contributions of both the meso- and
microscales.



4. Applications and veriﬁcation of the multiscale models

   The purpose of this section is twofold: (i) to evaluate the additional ﬁdelity gained by ﬁber-resolved homogenization of woven
fabric compared to yarn-resolved homogenization, and (ii) to assess whether ﬁber-resolved homogenization can be performed in a
computationally eﬃcient manner using the three-scale staggered training approach, with minimal loss of accuracy. Speciﬁcally, three

     from real-material imaging, and avoids surrogate modeling error. It is therefore taken as the reference model for accuracy in lieu
     of experimental data.

4.1. Training the three-scale ﬁber-resolved model’s MI-ANNs

    Staggered training of the three-scale model introduced in Section 3.2 requires two surrogate models: a mesoscale MI-ANN   ̃ 1,
trained on a meso-micro coupon problem, and a macroscale MI-ANN     ̃ 0 , trained on a macro-meso coupon problem. The training of
each MI-ANN surrogate follows the procedure described in [23], which integrates naturally into the staggered approach employed
here to capture the dependence of the macroscale MI-ANN on the mesoscale MI-ANN. The training procedures are summarized below.

4.1.1. Mesoscale MI-ANN
    The mesoscale MI-ANN is trained via homogenization of a tetrahedral coupon coupled with the ﬁber-level microscale RVE. A
total of 55 randomly generated strain paths, each spanning 5 seconds, are applied, and the corresponding homogenized stresses are
collected. The feasible domains used to generate these strain paths – i.e., the physically and numerically meaningful ranges of strain
for RVE training – are reported in Table 1. Since the microscale RVE exhibits symmetries along all three canonical planes, it is not
necessary to generate training data for negative shear values; the shear constitutive response is anti-symmetric with respect to the
shear strain, and this material symmetry is explicitly enforced in the MI-ANN. Of the 55 paths, 50 are used for training the model
and 5 for testing. The mesoscale MI-ANN’s ﬁt to a representative training path is shown in Fig. 9a, while its prediction on an unseen
strain path is shown in Fig. 9b.

4.1.2. Macroscale MI-ANN
   The trained mesoscale MI-ANN is then used to facilitate training of the macroscale MI-ANN. Homogenization of a membrane
coupon coupled with the yarn-level mesoscale RVE is performed for 30 randomly generated planar strain paths, each spanning 5
seconds. The feasible domains – deﬁned as above – used to generate these strain paths are reported in Table 1. Of the 30 paths, 25
are used for training and 5 for testing. The macroscale MI-ANN is validated by comparing its ﬁt to a training path (shown in Fig. 10a)
and its prediction on an unseen path (shown in Fig. 10b).

4.3. Parachute inﬂation problem

    Next, we simulate the inﬂation of a DGB parachute to demonstrate the feasibility and computational eﬃciency of the 3S-FR-NN2
model on a large-scale problem. The FE model is based on the ASPIRE SR03 ﬂight-test parachute [5], used as a qualiﬁcation for the
Mars 2020 aerodynamic decelerator. It comprises 267 878 triangular membrane elements – each assigned the 3S-FR-NN2 constitutive
law – representing the canopy, 80 000 nonlinear bar elements for the suspension lines, and 4 968 rigid solid elements representing
the forebody. All relevant material properties and dimensions are reported in [55].
    Inﬂation is driven by a pressure force applied to the interior of the canopy, linearly ramped from 0 to 1 800 Pa on the disk and
1 200 Pa on the band over the ﬁrst 1 s, then held constant for 4 s. The canopy undergoes self-contact, modeled as frictionless. The FE
system starts in a tightly folded, accordion-like conﬁguration [55], and is integrated forward in time using explicit central diﬀerences
with a time step of 5 × 10−6 s until 𝑡 = 5 s. Fig. 14 shows a snapshot of the parachute at 𝑡 = 2 s during the simulation.
    The canopy area projected onto a plane perpendicular to the forebody axis is used as a representative QoI of the inﬂation behavior
and is plotted against time in Fig. 15. As expected, the viscoelasticity of the 3S-FR-NN2 model – being the sole structural dissipative
mechanism in the FE model – dampens the large initial oscillations, allowing the projected area to gradually approach a steady-state
value.
    Table 3 reports the computational cost of the parachute inﬂation simulation using the 3S-FR-NN2 model, alongside the estimated
cost for the 2S-FR model. The results reveal an impressive speedup of 3.2 × 106 , reducing the estimated wall-clock time from 35 365
years to only 95 hours. This demonstrates that the 3S-FR-NN2 model is not only feasible, but also enables eﬃcient, homogenized
simulations of parachute inﬂation with full viscoelastic and ﬁber-level ﬁdelity – computations that would be entirely intractable
using the 2S-FR model.

5. Conclusions

     In summary, this paper presented a three-scale homogenization model for woven nylon fabric, achieving ﬁber-level resolution at
the ﬁnest scale, combined with a staggered training procedure that is broadly applicable to multiscale problems. By decomposing a
many-scale problem into successive two-scale subproblems and leveraging surrogates, the staggered approach reduces oﬄine costs
from exponential to linear scaling with the number of scales, making it computationally tractable. The three-scale ﬁber-resolved model
matches the accuracy of the costly two-scale ﬁber-resolved homogenization based on real-material RVEs obtained from imaging, while
signiﬁcantly outperforming the two-scale yarn-resolved model, highlighting the importance of ﬁber-level resolution in woven fabric
homogenization. The framework is further demonstrated on a large-scale disk-gap-band parachute inﬂation simulation, showing that
it is both feasible and eﬃcient for highly resolved ﬁnite element multiscale models. Overall, the method provides a general, accurate,
and computationally eﬃcient approach for multiscale analysis, and the staggered training procedure is a key enabler of tractable
computation across a wide range of many-scale problems.

Appendix A. Generation of periodic and high-ﬁdelity RVEs from imaged geometries

    In general, a geometry obtained from imaging of a fabric material is not immediately suitable as a Representative Volume Element
(RVE) for two main reasons: (i) the ﬁnite resolution of imaging often leads ﬁber segmentation procedures to underestimate the
nominal cross-section of each ﬁber relative to its actual diameter [48]; (ii) the inherent randomness of ﬁber paths implies that a given
fabric segment is unlikely to be exactly periodic, preventing straightforward application of Periodic Boundary Conditions (PBCs).
Therefore, we seek a minimal distortion of the imaged geometry such that the resulting RVE has correct ﬁber diameters, exhibits
planar periodicity, maintains smooth geometry, and avoids interpenetrating ﬁbers.
    Let 𝑁𝑓 denote the number of ﬁbers in the RVE, and let ﬁber 𝑖 be parametrized by its centerline 𝑿𝑖0 (𝑠) along its abscissa 𝑠 in the
original conﬁguration. Each ﬁber centerline is discretized into 𝑛 evenly spaced control points 𝑿𝑖0 (𝑠𝑗 ), 𝑗 = 1, … , 𝑛, and 𝑚 evenly spaced
pin-balls 𝑿𝑖0 (𝑝𝑘 ), 𝑘 = 1, … , 𝑚. Let 𝑟𝑖 denote the target radius of ﬁber 𝑖.

starts and ends on opposite faces of the RVE must be determined. This is achieved by ﬁnding a bijective mapping Λ of the ﬁbers onto
themselves, minimizing the total cost
