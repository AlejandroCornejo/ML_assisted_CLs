                                                    SUMMARY

The FE2 method is a renown computational multiscale simulation technique for solid materials with fine-
scale microstructure. It allows for the accurate prediction of the mechanical behavior of structures made of
heterogeneous materials with nonlinear material behavior. However, the FE2 method leads to excessive CPU
time and storage requirements, even for academic two-dimensional problems. In order to allow for realistic
three-dimensional two-scale simulations, a significant reduction of the CPU and memory usage is required.
For this purpose, the authors have recently proposed a reduced basis homogenization scheme based on a
mixed incremental variational principle. The approach exploits the potential structure of generalized stan-
dard materials. Thereby, important speed-ups and memory savings can be achieved. Using high-performance
GPUs, the reduced-basis method can be further accelerated. In the present contribution, our previous works
are combined and extended to form the FE2 -reduced method: the FE2R . The FE2R can be used to simu-
late three-dimensional structural problems with consideration of the nonlinearity and microstructure of the
underlying material at acceptable computational cost. Thereby, it allows for a new level of complexity in
nonlinear multiscale simulations. Numerical examples illustrate the capabilities of the chosen approach.

                                              1. INTRODUCTION

In modern engineering applications materials with pronounced heterogeneity on the microscale
become more and more popular. Such heterogeneities can either be due to varying crystal orien-
tations, for example, in polycrystalline metals or ceramics, or due to different micro-constituents.
The latter is the case for composite materials with fiber and particle reinforcement. Because of
advantageous properties of the composites, for example e. g., with respect to damping or the stiffness
to weight ratio, they are increasingly applied. For example, each generation of aircrafts in the pre-
ceding century has seen an increase in the amount of composites used. The Boeing 787 Dreamliner
(first delivered in 2011) features the current peak percentage at around 50% of the total mass of the
aircraft being made of composites.

   These numbers clearly illustrate that a thorough understanding of the mechanical behavior of
composite materials is of crucial importance. Considering also the decrease in physical experiments
and the increase in virtual experiments based on numerical simulations, the urgent need for fast sim-
ulation methods is apparent. Analytical methods that can be used to predict the mechanical response
of heterogeneous materials go back to the upper Voigt [1] and the lower Reuss [2] estimates, which
provide rigorous bounds on the linear behavior of the composite. More sophisticated methods based
on Eshelby’s seminal work [3] have been exploited in many of the subsequent developments. Some
popular methods are the bounds of Hashin and Shtrikman [4] and the Mori–Tanaka scheme [5].
These analytical estimates have limitations with respect to the consideration of the geometric com-
plexity, that is, if the micro-morphology is not ellipsoidal, or in the presence of nonlinear material
behavior. For the latter case, variational methods [6–9] can be used. However, these methods still
have limitations with respect to the underlying material behavior and the microstructural geometry.
   Computational homogenization methods can overcome the limitations with respect to the geome-
try and the constitutive models. In the context of solid mechanics, the Finite Element Method (FEM;
e.g., [10, 11]) is well established, and a variety of commercial programs is at hand for different
engineering applications. In the multiscale context, the FE2 (FE Square; e.g., [12–14]) method can
be used if the length scales of the structural problem and of the microstructure are clearly sepa-
rated. The FE2 method associates each integration point on the structural scale with an FE model
of the microstructure. Because the microstructure itself contains usually hundreds of thousands of
unknowns and internal state variables and, at the same time, the structural problem contains hun-
dreds of thousands of integration points (and thus the same number of RVEs), the computational
cost (CPU time and storage requirements) of the FE2 technique is prohibitively large, particularly
for three-dimensional (3D) problems. Therefore, the FE2 method has not been applied to realistic
3D problems to the best of our knowledge.
   In order to alleviate the computational burden of two-scale simulations, Balzani, Brands,
Scheunemann and Schröder suggest the use of simplified representative volume elements: the so-
called statistically similar RVEs (SSRVEs; [15, 16]). The SSRVEs are constructed based on real
3D images of the microstructure by means of optimizing parameterized microstructures (e.g., con-
taining spheroidal inclusions) such that certain statistical measures of the original material are
preserved. The concept allows to construct periodic SSRVEs, which is an important advantage over
the direct use of 3D images in numerical simulations. Still the computational cost for the simula-
tion of SSRVEs is high, and applications to nested FE simulations can only be realized using a large
amount of resources (i.e., parallel computing with large hard disk storage requirements).
   A completely different approach for the rapid simulation of periodic 3D RVEs was proposed
approximately two decades ago by Moulinec, Michel, and Suquet [17, 18]. They suggested the use
of the elegant fast Fourier transform (FFT) in order to quickly solve the boundary value problem
using the Lippmann–Schwinger equation. The method has undergone a remarkable development
(e.g., [19–24]), and it has recently been applied in two-scale FE–FFT simulations [25]. While the
computing time (more precisely mainly the time required for the stiffness assembly and for the linear
solver in the FEM) can be reduced considerably by application of the FFT, the storage requirements
remain unaltered.
   In the past decade, the increasing demand for high-performance simulation methods has led to a
broad activity in the emerging field of model order reduction methods (e.g., [26–30]). Many appli-
cations treated in the mathematical community are designed for time-dependent linear problems or
problems with affine parameter dependence of the differential operator. Only recently, a introduc-
tory textbook on the topic was released [31]. The aim of model reduction is to find low-dimensional
parameterizations of approximate, yet accurate, solutions. Often this is performed by replacing
rather general solution spaces by problem-specific ones. An interpretation of these reduced solution
spaces for mechanical problems is, for example, that a linear combination of physically meaningful
displacement fields (for the investigated problem) is used to approximate the solution.
   Many of the existing model reduction techniques are specific to discrete systems, that is, they
require a discretization of the problem a priori, and the methods then operate directly on the discrete
system. For example, the nodal degrees of freedom in an FE discretization are assembled in a global
vector uh 2 RN , which is sought after in order to find the solution to partial differential equations

   A rather straightforward approach to exploit the model reduction is to use a Galerkin projection.
This approach was, for example, also considered by [32] in the Reduced Model Multiscale Method
(R3M), which provides a reduced order model for hyperelastic materials (two-dimensional porous
microstructures undergoing finite strains were considered). The same working group has later pro-
posed the use of Numerical Explicit Potentials (NEXP; [33]) and of Stochastic NEXP (S-NEXP
[34]) for the efficient simulation of hyperelastic solids. The key idea is to precompute the free energy
of the hyperelastic solid and, based on the preanalysis data, find suitable interpolants. The method
has two disadvantages with respect to its application in two-scale simulations: (i) the missing con-
sideration of dissipative processes and, thus, the missing path-dependency and (ii) the rather large
number of required precomputations in order to obtain the interpolation functions (for instance, in
[33], 100 samples are computed for a two-dimensional RVE covering deformations between 0 and
0.1% only). For the same class of hyper-elastic materials, Temizer and Zohdi [35] proposed a numer-
ical homogenization procedure in the sense of material maps. If applicable, the NEXP approach
or the concept of material maps can provide accurate approximations with an excellent computa-
tional efficiency of the resulting effective material model. However, many realistic problems require
the consideration of dissipative processes and for a broad range of loadings. Even for hyperelastic
materials, the latter can lead to a prohibitive amount of required precomputations.
   Other Galerkin projection-based approaches provide reduced models that involve a prohibitive
amount of algebraic operations. Despite the reduced number of degrees of freedom, the compu-
tational effort for the algebraic operations at runtime leads to a modest overall acceleration of
the numerical problem. This has long been recognized, and many authors propose to replace the
exact Galerkin projection by approximations. Thereby, additional computational gains are possible.
Many different methods have been proposed over the years, for example, the empirical interpola-
tion method (EIM; [26]), the discrete EIM (DEIM; [36]) or the missing point estimate (MPE; [27]),
to mention only a few. All of these approaches have in common that they replace the evaluation
of the high-dimensional residual of the discrete system by an evaluation of selected values of the
residual only. Obviously, this is not possible without assuming a certain correlation of the entries of
the discrete residual vector. It shall be emphasized that the discrete system serves as a basis for the
representation of the global residual in a reduced way. Moreover, projection-based model reduction
techniques usually require the storage of all history variables (i.e., of the internal state variables of
the material). Thereby, they fail at significantly reducing the storage requirements, which is a key
demand for the practical realization of two-scale simulations of 3D problems.
   A method that is suited for nonlinear mechanical problems involving internal variables is the A
Priori Hyperreduction (APHR; e.g., [28, 30]). While the method can allow for considerable speed-
ups (e.g., values of 4 to 30 are mentioned), it preserves a lot of freedom with respect to the modeling.
Unfortunately, the computational savings and the reduction in the memory requirements are limited
for the APHR.
   For the specific field of nonlinear homogenization, Michel and Suquet [37] proposed the Nonuni-
form Transformation Field Analysis (NTFA). In contrast to other model order reduction techniques,
the NTFA parameterizes not the displacements but the internal variables of the system using a
reduced basis spanned by so called plastic modes. By exploiting the micromechanical properties of
the system, it can be shown that the microscopic displacement field u.x/ is a linear function of the
actuation of the plastic modes and of the macroscopic strain applied to the representative volume

element (RVE). While being very efficient with respect to computation time and memory savings,
the NTFA has the limitation of being particularly sensitive to the microscopic material model, which
also determines the effective yield criterion for the reduced variables in a semi-heuristic way. Exten-
sions of the original proposal to other isotropic material laws and applications to 3D problems have
been proposed in the past decade [38–44]. Besides the complications related to the effective yield
criterion, a limitation of the NTFA is the recourse to constant hardening variables within the individ-
ual phases. Therefore, effects induced by local hardening or softening of the inelastic phase cannot
be accounted for.
   In order to derive a reduced-order model for a more general class of inelastic materials, the authors
recently proposed an extension of the NTFA that eliminates the semi-heuristic yield criterion and,
at the same time, most of the limitations with respect to the local constitutive model. The method
is referred to as potential based reduced basis model order reduction (pRBMOR; [45, 46]). The
generalizations with respect to the NTFA are achieved by allowing for a rather rich sub-class of GSM
[47–49]. A mixed variational representation of the constitutive behavior in the reduced framework
leads to an evolution law for the reduced variables of the effective material. The freedom with
respect to the modeling does not come for free: many algebraic operations are required in order to
compute the functionals defining the evolution law. The speed-up of the method was found to be on
the order of 50 to 500 with respect to full FE calculations, which is a good achievement, when being
compared with other reduced-basis model order reduction methods.
   Recently, the method was extended by the authors [46] to also account for a reduced basis of
non-uniform hardening states, and a massively parallel GPU implementation of the algorithm using
mixed precision floating point arithmetics was introduced. It provides additional gains with respect
to the computation time by a factor 100 and higher (one GPU vs one CPU core). Thereby, the
total speed-up of the GPU accelerated pRBMOR is on the order of 104 and higher. In the present
contribution, the GPU accelerated version [46] is used and extended in order to accelerate actual 3D
two-scale simulations of FE2 type.
   The paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, the two-scale problem is formally described. In
Section 3, the pRBMOR framework proposed in previous works [45, 46] is formulated. A discussion
of theoretical and algorithmic aspects of the method and a methodological comparison with the
APHR are presented. Section 4 is devoted to FE2 and the novel FE2R method. The conceptual idea
of computational homogenization (in the sense of the FE2 method) is detailed. Based on this, the
computational demands for a feasible two-scale simulation are formulated, and a comparison with
simulations using the rapid FFT is conducted. Then the pRBMOR approach is equipped with an
algorithmic stiffness operator. The implementation of the pRBMOR with GPU acceleration into
a FE code is briefly described. The resulting multiscale method is called FE2R . In Section 5, 3D
two-scale problems are computed using the newly proposed FE2R method. The results show that
the chosen reduced basis approach is able to provide speed-ups and memory savings that allow for
realistic two-scale simulations. The paper closes with a summary and a conclusion in Section 6,
where the necessity for some future developments is emphasized.

extended in [46] is used in the sequel (pRBMOR). The method originates from the NTFA initially

sis (TFA; [55, 56]). In contrast to classical model reduction methods that parameterize the discrete
nodal displacements, the pRBMOR and the NTFA parameterize only the internal variables.

3.3. Comparison with the A Priori Hyperreduction of Ryckelynck
The evolution law for the reduced coefficients is a key difference to the APHR [28, 30], which is a
successful model reduction technique for nonlinear solid mechanical problems. In the APHR, the
microscopic material state is stored as a local variable in a subset of the RVE. This confined region
is referred to as reduced integration domain (RID). The mechanical equilibrium is only enforced for

the nodes inside of the RID with the displacements being computed using a global basis of ansatz
functions. Formally, this can be seen as a Petrov–Galerkin procedure. The microscopic internal
variables inside of the RID are computed using classical implicit time integrators. Then a reduced
basis for the internal variables is used to extrapolate the internal state found inside of the RID to the
remainder of the RVE using a sort of minimum least square approximation. This projection of the
internal state onto the reduced basis is not physically motivated, but derived based on mathematical
aspects that are independent of the underlying constitutive model. Note also that in the APHR, the
memory savings are closely related to the size of the RID, that is, the bigger the RID, the smaller
the memory savings and vice versa. This point is controversial because the accuracy of the method
increases with increasing size of the RID and vice versa, which leads to an obvious trade-off between
accuracy and computational savings.
   The pRBMOR proposed in [45, 46] differs from the APHR in several points. First, the mem-
ory requirements in the pRBMOR are independent of the integration domain, but they depend only
on the number of reduced ansatz functions. Second, the evolution of the reduced internal variables
(here: O , O ) is driven by the local constitutive equations of the material and a penalization using the
dual dissipation potential. Thereby, the different material parameters and the degree of nonlinearity
of the constitutive model are incorporated in the projection, and the operator Pq .x; O / can depend on
the reduced hardening state O . Third, the displacements and strains are a priori linear functions of
the applied loading and of the reduced plastic strains. This is an essential advantage of the pRBMOR
because there is no need for an additional reduced basis (for the displacements/strains). Moreover,
mechanical equilibrium is guaranteed because of the superposition of self-equilibrated stress fields,
whereas in other model reduction techniques, this equilibrium needs to be determined iteratively.
Fourth – and maybe most importantly – the APHR and most if not all other model reduction tech-
niques for nonlinear solid mechanical problems require the computation of the local increments of
the microscopic internal state variables using implicit time integrators in order to allow for the sub-
sequent projection. Such local time integrators require the solution of low-dimensional, often highly
nonlinear problems. Each of the nonlinear constitutive problems involves a varying number of itera-
tions (in the following referred to as local or inner iterations). For each reduced displacement field,
the nonlinear problems have to be solved in order to compute the microscopic stress. The latter then
enters into the mechanical residual, which is then used to correct the reduced displacement field.
Each correction necessitates another integration of the local constitutive equations until the mechan-
ical residual is sufficiently small. The computational effort for the time integration of the constitutive
models depends multiplicatively on the number of iterations of the reduced displacements (referred
to as global or outer iterations) and the number of inner iterations.
   The authors would like to emphasize that the APHR is considered a very efficient and general
model reduction technique. It has the advantage of being suited for arbitrary constitutive mod-
els (i.e., not necessarily of GSM type) and for geometrically nonlinear or multi-physics problems.
However, this generality comes at the expense of increased computational cost, which is often
significantly greater than for the specialized pRBMOR. In contrast to the APHR or other model

3.4. Mode identification and data handling
An important step of the pRBMOR is the identification of the plastic modes and of the hardening
modes. Inspired by classical model reduction, we divide the pRBMOR into an offline or pre-analysis
stage and an online stage. During the pre-analysis, the full RVE problem needs to be solved for a set
of different loading paths. The output (i.e., the internal variables) of all discrete time steps during
the pre-analysis phase has to be stored. Once the pre-analysis data are computed, a snapshot proper
orthogonal decomposition (POD) is used to extract N plastic modes and M hardening modes from
the computed inputs. Therefore, the symmetric positive semi-definite matrices

any further after a certain level of accuracy is attained. Instead, the same number of modes identified
using more diverse input data can produce reduced order models with considerably smaller errors.

hoc choice for the generation of the input data was promoted in [40] in terms of six linearly indepen-
dent strain loadings, which should be applied using a problem-related load rate mimicking the one
of potential fields of application. A sophisticated kinematic approach for the automatic generation
of well-chosen loading directions was recently promoted in [58] with the key idea of finding training
directions that are homogeneously distributed on the hyper-sphere in R6 . In the current work, we
use a similar approach for the definition of the training directions. The number of pre-analysis com-
putations is provided in Section 4 for the considered examples. The authors would like to emphasize
that the computational effort for the offline phase is basically negligible if the pRBMOR is used in
order to accelerate actual two-scale simulations otherwise treated using computational homogeniza-
tion techniques. Even hundreds of training directions make up for only a fraction of the overall cost
that can later be economized during the solution of the two-scale problem (i.e., in the online phase).
More precisely, hundreds of years of saved computing time have to be related to a couple of days
required for the numerical training.

forced microstructures in their elastic regime. It was shown that this method is equivalent to a
steepest descent algorithm, which is known to converge rather slowly under certain conditions [23],
for example, if the phase contrast gets large. Hence, the basic scheme has nowadays been replaced
by more refined algorithms with improved computational efficiency especially for high-phase con-
trast, for example, involving Krylov subspace methods [23, 59] or Lagrangian multipliers [18]. The
FFT method has evolved rather rapidly, and it was extended to more involved problems, for exam-
ple, for the consideration of viscoplasticity and large strains [19–21, 60]. Thereby, the FFT proved
to be a valuable alternative to the FEM (e.g., [61]).
   Today, efficient software libraries for the FFT/inverse FFT exist off the shelf, for example,
in terms of the parallelized FFTW [62] or the GPU-accelerated version by Nvidia (cuFFT;
http://developer.nvidia.com/cufft). An operable FFT code for mechanical problems is also freely
available at http://www.sattse-technologies.com/projects/en/craft, which was initially developed
by A. Labé, H. Moulinec and P. Suquet at the Laboratoire de Mécanique et d’Acoustique,
Marseille, France.
   In the context of true multiscale simulations such as two-scale problems as described in Section 2,
the authors are aware of a single contribution [25], which uses a nested FE–FFT-based approach in
a setting that is similar to the one used in the present work (3D; nonlinear materials). However, the
authors of [25] used a rather simple constitutive model with a single internal variable denoting the
damage of the material. The CPU time reported in [25] was still on the order of several days although
the problem size was rather small (500 macro elements, whereof only 93 elements are coupled
to the FFT; FFT discretization: 643 250,000 voxels). Although being already rather efficient with
respect to the computing time, the main problem of the FE–FFT multiscale method remains the need
to store the local constitutive variables for each RVE problem. This induces tremendous memory
requirements, which are a general problem when solving nested PDEs involving internal variables.
Further, the FFT approach still requires numerous (i.e., prohibitively many) evaluations of the
constitutive model, which can produce an enormous computational cost in two-scale simulations.

4.3. Computational demands for two-scale simulations
In order to develop a practicable two-scale simulation method, the demands have to be well under-
stood. A hypothetic but realistic example of a two-scale problem, which is representative for many
applications, will be discussed in the following. Therefore, we assert N int D 300; 000 integration
points on the structural or macroscopic domain .    N The number of displacement degrees of free-
dom of the macroscopic problem is nearly negligible for the considered two-scale setting. On the
microscale, Nint D 200; 000 integration points are assumed although this number could easily be
higher. In addition, we assert 200,000 displacement degrees of freedom for the microscale problem.
Then a modern six core workstation can solve one nonlinear load increment of the FE problem on
the RVE level in approximately 5 min.
   First, we illustrate the computational demands of the FE2 method for the model problem.
Assuming four equilibrium iterations on the structural level for each load step and each RVE and a
total of 50 load increments, a total of 6 mio. RVE-level iterations are required to solve the two-scale
problem. This amounts to 500,000 h or 57 years of computation time on the assumed workstation. In
order to solve the problem within 2 or 3 days, a reduction of the computation time by a factor of 104
or higher is mandatory. In addition to the prohibitive computation time, the memory requirements of
the FE2 problem are excessive. If only the stress tensor, the total and plastic strain tensor and one
scalar hardening variable are stored, then approximately 8 TB of data would have to be stored in
order to save a single snapshot of all RVE problems, which is the mere minimum of information
that is required.
   With respect to three-dimensional two-scale simulations, three aspects are considered as crucial in
the following. First, a reduction of the computation time for the RVE in the order of 104 or (possibly)
higher is required in order to be able to solve the two-scale problem in an acceptable time (here: two
or three days of computation time). Second, the memory requirement has to be reduced by a factor
104 -105 (equivalent to less than 1 GB of data per load increment). These first two requirements are
approximately met by the pRBMOR in combination with a high-performance GPU implementation.
   Third, the accuracy of the prediction has to be sufficient. While the first two requirements are
easy to motivate and to verify, the accuracy is currently the most intricate to judge on. It is obvi-
ous that the massive reductions required because of the anticipated computation time and memory
savings cannot be achieved without some loss in accuracy, for example, with respect to the macro-
scopic stress response predicted by the reduced order model (ROM). The difficulty in answering
whether the ROM is sufficiently accurate is because of the nonlinearity and path-dependency of
the local and, thus, global material response. A priori, it is not possible to give an indication for
the accuracy in a mathematically rigorous way to the best of our knowledge. However, previous
investigations (e.g., [45, 46]) have shown that the accuracy of the ROM for loadings that are not
used in the pre-analysis stage is still acceptable. Here acceptable refers to the accuracy of the pre-
dicted macroscopic stress N . Deviations in the norm of N below 10% are assumed acceptable.
Errors in this order of magnitude can be considered sufficiently small when taking into account
the uncertainty of the microscopic material parameters or the fluctuation of the microstructural
geometry of real-world heterogeneous materials. In the current investigation, it is a priori assumed
that the preliminary testing using selected load cases is sufficient to approve the desired accuracy
of the ROM.

4.5. FE2R implementation
The implementation of the FE2R method involves two stages: the offline phase (Section 3.4) in
which the modes are determined and the online phase. The offline phase is mandatory and needs
to be performed before the two-scale simulations as it determines the reduced-order model. During
the subsequent online phase, the actual two-scale problem is solved by using the reduced model to
determine the effective stress N and the algorithmic tangent operator C  N a at each integration point
of the structural problem. While the steps of the pre-analysis (or offline) phase have been discussed
in previous works (e.g., [40, 45]), the online phase has not been discussed thus far. It consists of
the modeling of the structural problem involving the geometry, the boundary conditions, and so on.
Then a classical nonlinear FE implementation is used with the only difference of calling the reduced-
order model (ROM) described in Section 3.1 instead of standard constitutive model. All steps of the
offline and online phase are illustrated in Figure 4.
   Most importantly, the calls to the ROM are frequent, that is, for each (macroscopic) integration
point at each (macroscopic) equilibrium iteration. Hence, the algorithmic efficiency of the ROM is of
utmost importance. Therefore, we use our recent GPU-accelerated version of the pRBMOR scheme
for Nvidia graphic cards, which allows for rapid computations. In order to obtain further gains with
respect to computational efficiency, we have adopted the idea of a reduced integration domain (RID)
from the APHR method [28, 30]. More precisely, we select only a subdomain R ¨  for the
evaluation of the driving forces  and r, O of the hardening matrices HO and H  O and of the stationary
conditions (30), (31). In practice, only a subset of the integration points within the inelastic mate-
rial was chosen heuristically, for example, by taking only every n-th integration point. Then the
evaluation of the effective potential is pursued only on this reduced subset, and, thereby, an approx-
imate effective potential is obtained. Because the computational effort for the algebraic operations
(various matrix/vector operations) is linear in the number of integration points, a significant reduc-
tion of the overall computation time can thus be achieved. Of course, the accuracy of the reduced
system is not warranted by this heuristic reduced integration of the occurring functionals. How-
ever, the authors have verified that a reduced number of integration points have a surprisingly low
effect on the accuracy, if not too few integration points are chosen. For example, the RVE exam-
ined in [46] contains approximately 1 mio. integration points within the inelastic phase. By using
only every 128th integration point, computational savings on the order of 100 are found although
the computational accuracy is essentially preserved.

5.3. Two-scale FE2R simulation
In order to investigate the FE2R approach, a flat yet 3D specimen with variable geometry and multi-
ple circular holes is considered (Figure 7). The boundary conditions were applied using prescribed
displacements at the ends of the specimen. The total stretch of 1% is applied in 1 s. Although the
global stretch is only 1%, the strain field is found to be highly heterogeneous with local maxima of
up to 5% in norm. In order to investigate the sensitivity of the computational results with respect
to the macroscopic mesh resolution, three different mesh densities (all consisting of fully integrated
tri-linear hexahedral elements) were investigated: 832 elements (1900 nodes), 6504 elements (8965
nodes), and 53 790 elements (65 597 nodes). All three discretizations were used in combination
with both the fibrous material (microstructure A) and the porous material (microstructure B). The
fibrous material was oriented such that the mean fiber axis was perpendicular to the planar surface
of the specimen, while the coordinate system of the near isotropic porous material was aligned with
the global system. The authors would like to point out that by using local material orientations, arbi-
trary distributions of the orientation of the microstructure can be assigned to each integration point
on the macroscopic scale (see [64] for an example with local-material orientations using the NTFA).
Although the specimen geometry suggests the use of planar boundary conditions, the underlying
3D microstructure induces a nonlinear, rate-dependent, and anisotropic overall response. Thus, the
macroscopic problem is in fact 3D, which precludes the use of planar boundary conditions, and a
full 3D analysis is required.
   The effective force-displacement curve of the virtual two-scale experiment was extracted
(Figure 8). It is found that the tensile force shows only a small dependency with respect to the
mesh density on the macroscale (max. difference of 4% between 832 and 53 790 elements) for both
microstructures.
   In addition to the overall tensile force, the distribution of the local stress and strain fields is also
investigated. The distribution of the von Mises equivalent stress N eq and of the pressure pN at the
end of the simulation are shown for microstructures A and B in Figure 9. At first, the distribution
is qualitatively similar for both microstructures. However, quantitative discrepancies are found, for
example, because of the different hardening models and the different underlying microstructural
geometry.

                                   6. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

In this contribution, the theoretical and algorithmic developments of recent works [45, 46] have
been combined and extended in order to develop a novel two-scale simulation method called FE2R .

The pRBMOR approach is presented in Section 3.1 in detail. Several theoretical and algorithmic
aspects are discussed in Section 3.2. These include an interpretation of the stationarity conditions
in terms of state-dependent projections and a comparison of the pRBMOR to the APHR of David
Ryckelynck [28, 30]. While the APHR can allow for more general models on the RVE level (large
deformation, non-GSM-type materials), it provides significantly smaller computational gains than
the pRBMOR method. The concept of the reduced integration domain (RID) of the APHR is
identified as being (with some modifications) applicable to the pRBMOR approach.
   The core of the paper is Section 4. It first describes the FE2 method [13, 14]. Based on computa-
tional considerations for the FE2 method and a hypothetic two-scale problem, requirements for the
savings in computation time and memory are postulated. Current model reduction methods cannot
offer the required savings (>104 in computation time and >105 in memory) for the considered class
of nonlinear material models. FFT-based methods are also considered, but, in our opinion, their
storage requirements limit their applicability in nested two-scale simulations (see, e.g., [25] and the
comparison in Section 4.2). Therefore, the pRBMOR method is chosen in order to accelerate the
microstructural simulation: it offers a reduction in the memory requirements that is beyond the pos-
tulated requirements and a reduction with respect to the computation time that is sufficient to meet
the postulated demands. In order to be applicable to two-scale simulations, the pRBMOR scheme
is equipped with an algorithmic tangent stiffness operator in Section 4.4. Interestingly, the algorith-
mic stiffness can be computed basically without additional computational effort, which is another
advantage over the FE2 and multiscale FFT method, where the tangent stiffness operator requires a
substantial number of additional algebraic operations (Section 4.1).
   The steps of the offline and online phase of the pRBMOR algorithm are then summarized in
Section 4.5. The concept of a reduced-integration domain (RID) is included in the schematic rep-
resentation. More precisely, only a subset of the integration points is used in order to evaluate the
residual fO and the Jacobian JO . This further improves the computational efficiency of the pRBMOR
without leading to a notable loss in accuracy. Remarkably, only a rather small number of integration
points are required in order to provide accurate predictions, thus allowing for an acceleration of the
pRBMOR scheme in the order of up to 100 for rather fine discretizations of the RVE.
   The FE2R method is implemented into an in-house FE code. Only two changes are required: First,
an initialization is required in which the reduced model is read into the memory of the workstation
and GPU. Second, a specific material subroutine implementing the saddle-point problem for …         N  is
required. The latter makes excessive use of the remarkable performance of modern graphics cards
[46]. The resulting algorithm is used for the two-scale simulation of a 3D problem. Two different
microstructures and different microscopic constitutive models are used in order to show the effi-
ciency and generality of the chosen approach. The computational results show that the FE2R method
using the GPU accelerated pRBMOR allows for realistic two-scale simulations with complex 3D
microstructures at reasonable computing times. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first applica-
tion of a 3D-nested FE method to realistic problem settings without the excessive use of large-scale
computer clusters. Thereby, the new FE2R method offers new multiscale simulation capabilities to
both academia and industry.
   The pRBMOR algorithm appears rather complicated at first, and many individual parts need to
be implemented. However, the potential structure of the GSM, which is the basis for the pRB-
MOR method is the only requirement that has to be imposed on the constitutive modeling. Still the
implementation is independent of the actual formulation of the thermodynamic potentials. In partic-
ular, the pRBMOR algorithm and its implementation need to be written only once. Then all GSM
that belong to the specified sub-class can be considered by only altering the implementation of the
two potentials and   . This holds also true for the mode-identification procedure (Section 3.4),
which can be implemented as an automated, unattended procedure that is basically independent of
the considered RVE and constitutive model. Again, the identification procedure requires the GSM-
type definition of the material depending only on          and   . This is a significant generalization
with respect to the constitutive models over the NTFA [37, 38]. Additionally, the black-box charac-
ter of the method is a nice feature that allows for many interesting applications at a rather modest
investment once the initial coding is performed.

   With respect to future developments, several important questions remain unanswered thus far:
First and most importantly, online error estimators or indicators for the reduced model need to be
developed in order to judge on the accuracy of the computational results. Second, a better under-
standing of the effect of the reduced integration domain is required to exploit the algorithmic gains
systematically. Third, the current mode-identification procedure could be extended to allow for a bet-
ter reduced basis, possibly incorporating also the aforementioned error indicators. Lastly, extensions
towards kinematically nonlinear problems are ultimately of interest. The latter require a thorough
re-design of the approach and cannot be realized in terms of extensions of the current method but
only through completely novel developments.
   An interesting combination could be the use of FFT-based methods in the offline phase in order
to identify the reduced order model. Here, significant speed-ups could be attained which would,
for example, allow for more training computations and, thereby, for an improved accuracy due to
a refined selection of the reduced basis: recently, it was found that even at a constant dimension
of the reduced basis, significant accuracy improvements can be achieved when having more input
data [58]. The authors think that the development of sophisticated error indicators and of refined
algorithms for the offline phase are the most fundamental steps and should be addressed shortly.
