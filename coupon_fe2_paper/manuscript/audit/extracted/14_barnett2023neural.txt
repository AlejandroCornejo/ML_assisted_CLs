1. Introduction

   Projection-based model order reduction (PMOR) allows for the parsimonious representation of a parametric, semi-
discrete or discrete high-dimensional model (HDM), through the construction of an associated, lower-dimensional,
projection-based reduced-order model (PROM). A second-level approximation known as hyperreduction transforms a PROM
into a hyperreduced counterpart (HPROM) capable of operating in real-time, while preserving the desired level of accu-
racy. As such, a HPROM is essential for many time-critical applications such as simulation-driven design and optimization,
optimal control, uncertainty quantiﬁcation, and digital twinning, to name only a few.
   Traditionally, PMOR has been performed using best-approximations on aﬃne subspaces of dimension n  N, where
n and N denote the dimensions of the PROM/HPROM and underlying HDM, respectively. It has demonstrated signiﬁcant
success for a large number of parametric applications grounded in elliptic, parabolic, and second-order hyperbolic partial

differential equations (PDEs) – for example, problems in solid mechanics [2,3], heat conduction (or diffusion) [4], structural
dynamics [5], and wave propagation [6,7]. It has also demonstrated success for parametric, linearized computational ﬂuid
dynamic (CFD) and coupled aeroelastic computations [8,9]. For nonparametric RANS [10] and LES [11] CFD computations,
PMOR has delivered impressive speedup factors including for compressible ﬂow problems past complex three-dimensional
geometries [10]; and in the presence of shocks [12]. For parametric counterpart ﬂow computations – and for that matter,
for most ﬁrst-order hyperbolic PDEs describing transport (or advection) problems – traditional PMOR suffers from the slow
decay of the Kolmogorov n-width (for example, see [13]).
    The Kolmogorov n-width dn (M) describes the worst-case error with respect to a subset M of a normed space S arising
from a projection onto the best-possible linear subspace of S of dimension n [14], represented here by a right reduced-order
basis (ROB) V ∈ R N ×n . Hence, it sets a limit to any PROM-based approximation; for this reason, it is often referred to as a
“barrier” to reducibility. In particular, the Kolmogorov n-width affects most convection-dominated (ﬂow) models; it implies
a dimension n that is suﬃciently large to challenge the construction of a traditional PROM, or simply defeat the purpose of
PMOR.
    A variety of approaches has been formulated so far to attempt to mitigate the Kolmogorov barrier to PMOR. Typically,
these approaches involve altering the traditional aﬃne subspace approximation to incorporate some nonlinearity. The sim-
plest of them substitutes the aﬃne subspace approximation with a piecewise counterpart [15] constructed by partitioning
of the nonlinear high-dimensional solution manifold into regions and computing in each region a local right ROB associated
with a local aﬃne subspace approximation. This approach was shown in 2016 to perform in real-time on a laptop, for
steady-state RANS computations associated with the NASA Common Research Model [16] and a parametric HDM of dimen-
sion greater than N = 68 × 106 , in the transonic regime characterized by shocks, at a high Reynolds number (Re = 5 × 106 ),
and in a shape parameter domain D of dimension N D = 4 [17]. Nevertheless, due to the Kolmogorov barrier, one can rea-
sonably expect such a PMOR method to face increasing diﬃculties at delivering a similar performance for increasing values
of N D .
    Registration methods have also been proposed for addressing the Kolmogorov n-width issue, including in the context of
transport problems. From a mathematical viewpoint, these methods transform the traditional aﬃne subspace approximation
into a nonlinear approximation by applying a nonlinear parametrization to the aﬃne subspace [18–24] – usually, through
a transformation of the given spatial domain. As such, these methods involve the motion and/or deformation of the mesh
underlying the semi-discrete or discrete HDM. For this reason, they have been limited so far to simple geometries; or are
applicable to complex ones but are in this case vulnerable to mesh entanglements – particularly for large-scale meshes such
as those associated, for example, with high Reynolds number ﬂows. From an intuitive viewpoint, registration methods aim
at determining domain mappings that cause parametric features of the solution to align in a reference domain for a set
of training points sampled in the parameter domain D , which removes the convective nature of the solution, lowers the
Kolmogorov n-width, and improves the usefulness of the sampled solution snapshots when an unsampled parameter point
is queried in D .
    Quadratic approximation manifolds have also been proposed, in two different forms, to mitigate the Kolmogorov barrier:
a simulation-free form for structural dynamic applications [25]; and a more general data-driven form that was demonstrated
for convection-dominated turbulent ﬂow problems as well as other transport problems [1]. They have also been proposed
for performing nonintrusive PMOR using an operator inference method [26].
    Most relevant to this paper are approaches that have been proposed for constructing arbitrarily nonlinear approximation
manifolds for model order reduction (MOR) using some form of artiﬁcial neural network (ANN) representations of the
solution or some residual, without necessarily incorporating any projection. One such approach was introduced in [27],
in the context of structured meshes, and based on convolutional autoencoders. During training, such methods map the
high-dimensional solution to a latent space whose dimension approaches that of the intrinsic solution manifold. Extensions
to unstructured meshes were performed in [28], then in [29], using various means, including replacing the convolutional
autoencoder with a graph convolutional ANN [29]. The success of all these approaches however comes at a hefty price,
as convolutional networks are computationally expensive to train and evaluate, especially for large-dimensional problems
whose solutions act as input and output of an autoencoder. Alternative techniques based on conventional deep ANNs (simply
referred to in the remainder of this paper as ANNs) have been more recently proposed for mitigating the Kolmogorov
barrier. These include the techniques described in [30,31], which use ANNs to learn transformation maps such as those
used by registration methods that eliminate the convection nature of the solution, without deforming the underlying mesh
however. Another approach that is equally relevant to this paper is the uplifted reduced-order modeling (UROM) method
introduced in [32]. This method combines a standard PROM, a memory embedded long short-term memory (LSTM) closure
model, and a LSTM-based super-resolution model. Hence, it is a complex approximation method with three modeling layers:
a ﬁrst layer in which a standard, intrusive, PMOR approach is adopted to model the dynamics represented by the largest
modes of the system of interest; a closure layer where an LSTM model is introduced to account for the residuals beyond
the usual truncation; and a third, uplifting (or super-resolution) layer, which expands the span of the approximation in a
post-processing step using a second LSTM.
    All ANN-based MOR methods outlined above suffer from either or both of the following issues:

  • First, except for the UROM method [32], which qualiﬁes as a PMOR method as it incorporates a projection, the size
     of their network scales with the large dimension N of the HDM. Hence, for large-scale computational models such as

                                                              2

    those associated with ﬂows at high Reynolds numbers and past complex geometries, the training of such networks is
    computationally intractable. This explains why in most if not all cases, the aforementioned ANN-based MOR methods
    have been demonstrated on simple one-dimensional applications, occasionally on two-dimensional (2D) ones, in both
    cases on problems with N ≤ 9 000; and usually without emphasizing oﬄine (training) and/or online (execution) wall
    clock time. We also note that in the context of nonintrusive ANN-based MOR methods however,1 an approach was
    most recently presented in [33] for training such methods using projected quantities and therefore a computational
    complexity that scales with the reduced dimension n of the approximation. This training approach was demonstrated
    for an application associated with N = 257 528, in which case it required 50 mins on a Tesla V100 32 GB GPU. Methods
    where ANNs are introduced in the context of Galerkin PMOR to mimic the concept of closure modeling [34,35] also rely
    on the aforementioned concept of training with projected quantities to achieve computational tractability.
  • Second, the hyperreduction of nonlinear reduced-order models (ROMs) constructed using the aforementioned ANN-
    based MOR methods remains a challenge, although some progress in this area was recently reported in [36] for the
    case of sparse shallow auto-encoders. As for the case of UROM, which is a PMOR method with two different ANNs, how
    to incorporate in it a computationally eﬃcient hyperreduction process remains at best unclear.

    The present work is inspired by our previous work on mitigating the Kolmogorov barrier, which focused for this purpose
on a data-driven approach for constructing a quadratic approximation manifold [1]. It proposes a general, intrusive, ANN-
augmented PROM that we refer to here and throughout the remainder of this paper as a PROM-ANN. The main objective of
the PROM-ANN is, for a ﬁxed level of accuracy, to reduce the dimensionality of the online approximation of the solution
beyond what is possible using the quadratic approximation, and dramatically beyond what is achievable using the traditional
aﬃne subspace approximation. In contrast to the alternative intrusive and projection-free approaches outlined above for
constructing arbitrarily nonlinear approximation manifolds for MOR using some form of ANN, the training of the ANN
part of the PROM-ANN we propose in this paper does not involve data whose dimension scales with that of the HDM. In
contrast to a UROM [32], our PROM-ANN is simpler to construct, relies on a single ANN, and couples it strongly with the
aﬃne approximation; therefore, our PROM-ANN is deemed to be more practical and performant than a UROM. And unlike
all ROMs resulting from all aforementioned dimensionality reduction approaches, our PROM-ANN is eﬃciently hyperreducible
using any well-established hyperreduction method. Consequently, unlike most if not all of the aforementioned ANN-based
approaches, the PROM-ANN concept we propose in this paper should be practical for large-scale and industry-relevant
problems which, in the context of CFD for example, tend to have hundreds of millions of degrees of freedom (dofs).
    While the scope of the PROM-ANN concept we propose here is equally applicable to HDMs arising from the semi-
discretization or discretization of elliptic, parabolic, and both types of hyperbolic PDEs, we focus in this paper on the case
of ﬁrst-order hyperbolic PDEs, as the PMOR of the corresponding HDMs is most vulnerable to the Kolmogorov barrier.
Consequently, we ground our work in the nonlinear least-squares Petrov-Galerkin (LSPG) PMOR method [37,38] which
represents the state of the art of PMOR for convection-dominated turbulent ﬂow problems. However, we note that the
approach we propose in Section 3 for constructing an arbitrarily nonlinear approximation manifold using a right ROB and
an ANN is equally applicable to Galerkin PMOR methods.
    To achieve computational eﬃciency, we choose to hyperreduce our PROM-ANN using the energy-conserving sampling
and weighting (ECSW) [5] method. Although originally designed in the context of ﬁnite element (FE) approximations, solid
mechanics, and Galerkin PROMs, this hyperreduction method was recently extended in [10] to that of ﬁnite volume (FV) ap-
proximations, CFD, and LSPG PMOR [10]; and was shown to deliver impressive speedup factors while preserving a desirable
level of accuracy. Nevertheless, we note that any other hyperreduction method such as the empirical interpolation method
(EIM) [39] and its discrete version (DEIM) [40] can be considered to hyperreduce a PROM-ANN.
    The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we set the scope of this work and overview the LSPG
and ECSW methods. In Section 3, we present the main underpinning of the PROM-ANN concept – namely, a computationally
tractable approach for constructing an arbitrarily nonlinear approximation manifold using a right ROB and an ANN. In Sec-
tion 4, we adapt LSPG and ECSW to this concept and comment on the additional computational complexity and additional
storage requirement entailed by the adaptations. In Section 5, we apply our proposed PMOR technology to two instances
of dimension 125 000 and 1 125 000 of a popular, parametric, shock-dominated benchmark problem that exhibits the Kol-
mogorov n-width issue. We contrast the performance of our computational technology with that of the traditional PMOR
method. We highlight the computational tractability of its oﬄine stage and the superior performance of its online stage.
Speciﬁcally, for the same level of desirable accuracy, we report impressive wall clock time speedup factors with respect to
both the considered HDMs and their traditional HPROMs. In Section 6, we offer concluding remarks.

2. Background

    Throughout this paper, we consider the following parametric, nonlinear, semi-discrete, ﬁrst-order initial value problem

        M(μ)u̇ (t ; μ) + f (u (t ; μ) ; μ) − g (t ; μ) = 0
                                                                                                                                                      (1)
                                                      u (0; μ) = u0 (μ)

where t ∈]0, T f ] denotes time and ]0, T f ] denotes the time-interval of interest; μ ∈ D ⊂ R N D denotes a vector-valued
parameter of dimension N D and is often referred to as a “point” in the parameter domain D ; a dot denotes a time-
derivative; u ∈ R N denotes the semi-discrete solution vector; M ∈ R N × N is a mass-like matrix; f ∈ R N is a nonlinear vector
function representing a semi-discrete ﬂux or an internal force vector; g ∈ R N is a nonlinear vector function representing a
semi-discrete external force vector, source term, or the effect of some time-dependent boundary conditions; and u0 ∈ R N
denotes an initial condition for the semi-discrete solution vector. We refer to this problem as the HDM problem (or simply
HDM) and consider its solution using an implicit time-integration scheme. We refer to the computed solution as the high-
dimensional solution (or simply HDM solution). We note that this HDM encompasses counterparts associated with nonlinear,
semi-discrete, second-order dynamical systems characterized by arbitrarily complex dissipative (damping) forces, as such
problems can be rewritten in the ﬁrst-order form (1) using a change of variables and some mathematical transformations
[5].

3. Construction of a nonlinear approximation manifold using a ROB and an ANN

    We propose here a concept of an ANN-augmented PROM – or a PROM-ANN – that is grounded in that of an ANN-
augmented aﬃne approximation, which itself is inspired from our previous work on the development of a quadratic
approximation manifold [1]. We start from the same snapshot matrix S ∈ R N × N s used to construct a traditional PROM
based on the traditional aﬃne subspace approximation. However, we aim at building a right ROB V whose dimension n not
only veriﬁes n  N, but is also much smaller than the dimension ntra of a traditional PROM. In practice, this means that
we build V by using a much looser tolerance εS in the singular value energy truncation criterion (3) than traditionally. To
remedy the inaccuracy that can be expected from such a decision, we augment the corresponding aﬃne subspace approx-
imation with an arbitrarily nonlinear ANN approximation. Furthermore, we design the ANN approximation such that the
size of the corresponding network does not scale with the large dimension N of the parametric HDM of interest, but with
a much smaller dimension.
    Speciﬁcally, we construct an arbitrarily nonlinear approximation manifold by approximating the solution u ∈ R N ×n as
follows

        u(t ; μ) ≈ ũ(t ; μ) = uref + Vq(t ; μ) + VN (q(t ; μ))                                                                               (16)

  • We construct V ∈ R N ×n as usual, using the ﬁrst n  N columns of US – the matrix of left singular vectors of the

    – that is, n  ntra .

4.2. Adaptation of ECSW

    Next, we adapt ECSW to the concept of an ANN-augmented PROM. For this purpose, we ﬁrst point the attention of the
reader to (8) and the ﬁrst bullet below that equation, which emphasize that for a given PROM characterized by a ROB
V, ECSW trains the cubature approximation for PROM predictions deﬁned by generalized coordinates and not for HDM
counterparts. This means that in principle, one should exercise the constructed PROM to compute and collect generalized
coordinates solution snapshots, which unfortunately defeats the purpose of hyperreduction. In the case of the traditional
aﬃne subspace approximation, this issue can be elegantly resolved by simply projecting the high-dimensional solution
snapshots collected in the matrix S, or a subset of them, onto the right ROB V. This is highlighted in (11), which describes
the construction of the matrix C and vector d deﬁning the least squares problem (14) and its early termination criterion
(15).
    In the case of the proposed mixed ANN-augmented aﬃne approximation (16) however, the identiﬁcation of the vector of
generalized coordinates ql associated with a collected high-dimensional solution snapshot ul can no longer be obtained via
projection onto either of the right ROB V or auxiliary right ROB V. Instead, it requires the solution of a nonlinear problem
of the form

5.5. Performance comparisons for the case of the mesh M1

   Table 1 summarizes the various models and model parameters considered for the solution of problem (30) on the mesh
M1; and the oﬄine as well as online performances of these models on the single core described at the beginning of

6. Conclusions

    The concept of a projection-based reduced-order model (PROM) augmented by an artiﬁcial neural network (ANN) – or
a PROM-ANN – we propose in this paper distinguishes itself from many previous attempts at exploiting ANNs in model
order reduction in two major aspects: 1) the size of its neural network does not scale with the large dimension N of the
underlying high-dimensional model (HDM), but with a much lower dimension n̄  N related to the dimension n  N of the
PROM part of this concept; and 2) it is hyperreducible to a HPROM-ANN using readily available hyperreduction methods.
Consequently, our concept is computationally tractable oﬄine, including for very-large scale computational models. While it
is inspired from our previous work on polynomial approximation manifolds [1], it also distinguishes itself from that work
in two other aspects: a) it enriches the traditional aﬃne subspace approximation a priori rather than a posteriori; and
b) it explicitly computes the enrichment using n̄ left singular vectors truncated during the construction of the traditional
reduced-order basis via the singular value decomposition of the snapshot matrix.
    For a parametric, shock-dominated benchmark problem that exhibits the Kolmogorov n-width issue and characterized
by N = 1 125 000, and for the settings n = 10 and n̄ = 140, the oﬄine training of the ANN part of our PROM-ANN concept
requires 23.40 minutes wall clock time on a single core of a Linux cluster that is not a GPU. The oﬄine construction on the
same single core of a reduced mesh for the HPROM-ANN counterpart requires 26.40 minutes wall clock time.
    For the same benchmark problem, the constructed HPROM-ANN delivers a better level of accuracy than that delivered
by a traditional HPROM of dimension ntra = 95 – using an almost order of magnitude smaller dimension. Consequently, this
HPROM-ANN executes roughly 70 times faster online than its counterpart traditional HPROM.
    When compared to the HDM, the aforementioned HPROM-ANN, whose dimension n = 10 is ﬁve orders of magnitude
smaller than the dimension N = 1 125 000 of the HDM, reproduces the HDM solution almost 815 times faster and with
almost 3% accuracy.
    In summary, our proposed concept of a HPROM-ANN has the potential for mitigating the Kolmogorov reducibility barrier.
We are currently working on automating the setting of its parameters n and n̄, and on conﬁrming as well as quantifying its
potential for industry-relevant, parametric, convection-dominated, turbulent ﬂow problems and highly nonlinear structural
dynamics problems.
