  A multilevel projection-based model order reduction framework
   for nonlinear dynamic multiscale problems in structural and
                          solid mechanics

                                                   SUMMARY
A reduction/hyper reduction framework is presented for dramatically accelerating the solution of nonlin-
ear dynamic multiscale problems in structural and solid mechanics. At each scale, the dimensionality of the
governing equations is reduced using the method of snapshots for proper orthogonal decomposition, and
computational efficiency is achieved for the evaluation of the nonlinear reduced-order terms using a carefully
designed configuration of the energy conserving sampling and weighting method. Periodic boundary condi-
tions at the microscales are treated as linear multipoint constraints and reduced via projection onto the span
of a basis formed from the singular value decomposition of Lagrange multiplier snapshots. Most importantly,
information is efficiently transmitted between the scales without incurring high-dimensional operations. In
this proposed proper orthogonal decomposition – energy conserving sampling and weighting nonlinear model
reduction framework, training is performed in two steps. First, a microscale hyper reduced-order model is
constructed in situ, or using a mesh coarsening strategy, in order to achieve significant speedups even in
non-parametric settings. Next, a classical offline–online training approach is performed to build a paramet-
ric hyper reduced-order macroscale model, which completes the construction of a fully hyper reduced-order
parametric multiscale model capable of fast and accurate multiscale simulations. A notable feature of this
computational framework is the minimization, at the macroscale level, of the cost of the offline training using
the in situ or coarsely trained hyper reduced-order microscale model to accelerate snapshot acquisition. The
effectiveness of the proposed hyper reduction framework at accelerating the solution of nonlinear dynamic
multiscale problems is demonstrated for two problems in structural and solid mechanics. Speedup factors as
high as five orders of magnitude are shown to be achievable. Copyright © 2017 John Wiley & Sons, Ltd.

                                             1. INTRODUCTION

Nonlinear multiscale problems – defined here as those nonlinear problems that exhibit vastly dif-
ferent scale features that are significant to the macroscopic behavior – are ubiquitous in science and
engineering. They arise, for example, in the accurate and reliable modeling of bone structures where
the relevant scales range from nanometers to meters [1]. A sample important application is the non-
linear analysis of the harm that befalls a vehicle occupant when the vehicle is subject to an impact
load. Numerical methods that attempt to resolve all such scales lead to massive discretized problems.

duced to model highly heterogeneous solid and structural systems without requiring a monolithic
discretization. Despite these advances, the numerical solution of multiscale problems – at least those
formulated in a general analysis setting, that is, finite deformation and nonlinear, history-dependent
microscale constitutive laws – remains today computationally intensive if not simply unaffordable.
To this effect, this work introduces a nonlinear, projection-based, model order reduction (PMOR)
framework built on the recently developed energy conserving sampling and weighting (ECSW) [4,
5] hyper reduction method for significantly reducing the computational cost associated with solving
multiscale problems in general, and those characterized by large deformation, microscale damage,
plasticity, and viscoelasticity in particular.
   Here and throughout the remainder of this paper, hyper reduction refers to the approximation of
the projections onto the subspace of approximation underlying a PMOR approach of residual vectors
and, for implicit computations, their Jacobian with respect to the solution using a numerical method
whose computational complexity scales only with the size of the constructed projection-based
reduced-order model (PROM). Hence, whereas PMOR reduces the dimensionality of a nonlinear
computational model, it is hyper reduction that ensures its operability in real time.
   A multitude of multiscale methods have been proposed to circumvent the need to resolve all het-
erogeneities in a monolithic manner. Self-consistent methods [6, 7], which tend to deliver reasonable
accuracy if the structure possesses geometric regularity, derive equivalent material properties from
the solution of an analytical boundary value problem – usually, an ellipsoidal inclusion in an infinite
matrix problem. Other multiscale methods, such as those based on an asymptotic homogenization
theory [8], expand the displacement field on a length parameter to approximate the macroscopic dis-
tribution and use variational principles to bridge scales. The scope of these methods is usually limited
to small strains and simple microscopic geometries, although their extension to finite deformation
has been performed in some contexts (for example, see [9]).
   Another class of multiscale methods considers homogenized material properties by fitting the
results of a representative volume element (RVE) analysis under a prescribed loading history [10,
11]. The à priori selection of the loading history and the analysis of the RVE usually limit the range
of applications of these methods to problems with simple material behaviors and small deformations.
   The concept of a locally attached microstructure [12–15] leads perhaps to the most general
approach for solving problems exhibiting multiple, separable scales. It can be described as fol-
lows. At each macroscale material point, the stress –strain relationship is determined via a detailed
computational analysis of a microstructure boundary value problem. The corresponding bound-
ary conditions are determined from the localization of the pointwise deformation gradient at the
macroscale. The pointwise macroscale stress tensor is determined from the distribution of this ten-
sor over the microstructure and a homogenization method. An analytic constitutive law is prescribed
at the microscale where the length scale is sufficiently small so that a homogeneous medium can
be assumed, and existing material models can accurately capture the response. While this concept
defines perhaps the most general approach for multiscale modeling and leads to a more computation-
ally efficient analysis than a full microscale analysis, it is very computationally intensive. It requires
performing a microstructure analysis at each Gauss point of the macroscale model. Hence, several
PMOR methods have been recently developed to reduce the computational cost of this multiscale
approach to that of a single scale computation [16–20]. In [16], proper orthogonal decomposition
(POD) was used to construct a PROM of the microscale problem based on an á priori training strategy
that computes solution snapshots from predefined microstrain histories. However, this microscale
PROM was not hyper reduced, and consequently, the realized speedup was limited to about an order
of magnitude, even when the offline costs were not accounted for. The PMOR approach proposed in
[16] was subsequently extended in [17] to formulations with periodic boundary conditions for the
microstructure model using the Lagrange multiplier approach. It was also very recently equipped
in [20, 21] with the empirical interpolation method [22, 23] and related variants for hyper reduc-
tion – albeit at the microscale level only – which nevertheless enabled the speedup of multiscale
computations based on the concept of a locally attached microstructure by up to three orders of mag-
nitude, when ignoring the offline costs. A number of other methods based on the so-called reduced
basis method have also been designed for elliptic (static), multiscale, scalar problems with affine

and finite deformation, and their impact on the acceleration of the solution of such problems, have
yet to be determined.
   In this work, a nonlinear PMOR framework built on an N + 1-level locally attached microstructure
approach and incorporating hyper reduction is proposed for solving nonlinear dynamic multiscale
problems arising in solid and structural mechanics. In this computational framework, the dimen-
sionality of the semi-discrete governing equations is reduced at all N + 1 scales, in sharp contrast
with previous works on this topic where model reduction was performed only at the finest scale [21,
26–30]. Specifically, PMOR is performed using a Galerkin projection onto a well-chosen subspace
of approximation. POD is used to extract this subspace from relevant training data. Averaging-based
localization and homogenization schemes are used in the proposed framework to transmit strain
information to finer scales and stress information to coarser scales, respectively. The deformation
gradient at the k-th scale is used to define uniform or periodic essential boundary conditions at the
k + 1-th scale. The pointwise first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor at the k-th scale is computed as the
volumetric average of the corresponding stress tensor over the locally attached microstructure, that
is, the k + 1-th scale. To achieve a computational complexity that depends on the lowest dimensions
only, the ECSW method [4, 5] is used for hyper reducing the projected nonlinear internal forces.
Indeed, for nonlinear structural dynamics problems in general, ECSW is the hyper reduction method
of choice due to its simplicity, strong connection to the FE method, and most importantly, prov-
able structure-preserving and stability properties [5]. However, this method is first tailored here to
the context of the locally attached microstructure approach by redesigning its training algorithm to
ensure that the reaction forces associated with the essential boundary conditions at the microscales
are well approximated. This revisiting of the ECSW method constitutes another major contribution
of this paper. In the case where periodic boundary conditions are used for formulating the microstruc-
ture problems, the periodicity conditions are posed as linear multipoint constraints, enforced by the
Lagrange multiplier method, and reduced via projection onto a reduced-order subspace represented
by a dual reduced-order basis (ROB) [31].
   Additionally, this work contributes a two-step training strategy for the proposed multilevel PMOR
framework. The first step focuses on the microscale problems and is either performed in situ or using
a coarsened mesh. It generates snapshots of the state, unconstrained and constrained components
of the internal forces, history variables, and Lagrange multipliers (in the case of periodic boundary
conditions) at the N finest scales. Because at each level, a microstructure simulation is performed at
each Gauss point, and the first step of the training algorithm uses readily available high-dimensional
solution snapshots to construct a POD-based ROB and a corresponding hyper-reduced PROM for
the microstructure problems. For example, these may be sampled from the discrete solutions that
are computed anyway at the first several Gauss points encountered during the first few time-steps
of a dynamic computation of interest. Alternatively, the solutions computed at the Gauss points of a
coarsened mesh can be sampled; in which case, more than a few time-steps of the dynamic compu-
tation of interest may be utilized for this purpose. Therefore, because an entire dynamic multiscale
simulation is not required to generate solution snapshots for the microstructures, significant speedups
of the desired multiscale computations can be achieved even in a non-parametric setting.
   The second step of the training strategy focuses on the macroscale problem. It relies on the
traditional offline– online approach to generate solution snapshots at the macroscale level, where
high-dimensional, time-dependent, multiscale simulations are performed for various configurations
of the parametric multiscale problem of interest. Thus, to realize the additional benefit of reduction
and hyper reduction at the macroscale level, a parametric analysis setting, such as that encountered
in optimization, uncertainty quantification, or simply repeated analysis, is required in order to amor-
tize training costs. However, an important feature of the proposed multilevel PMOR framework is
that its in situ or coarsened training for microscale problems can be used to speedup the acquisition
of solution snapshots for macroscale problems.

       2. LOCALLY ATTACHED MICROSTRUCTURE MULTISCALE FORMULATION

In order to keep this paper as self-contained as possible, a multiscale continuum mechanics formula-
tion based on the concept of a locally attached microstructure is first overviewed here. The labeling
of this concept results from the fact that in this formulation, the stress –strain relationship for a het-
erogeneous material is not defined by a constitutive law but by the solution at each material point of
a boundary value problem governing its microstructure. For generality, this concept is applied here
recursively to address problems that exhibit N+1 separate scales. Specifically, the stress –strain rela-
tionship at the N coarsest scales is defined by the solution of the boundary value problem at the next
finest scale and an appropriate scale transition method. At the N + 1-th scale, that is, the finest scale,
where all heterogeneities can be adequately resolved and described by an available constitutive the-
ory, it is defined by an analytical constitutive law. All considered length scales are assumed to be
much larger than the molecular dimension so that the continuum assumption holds. Furthermore,
scale separation is assumed to loosely couple the various scales through localization from coarse to
fine scales and homogenization from fine to coarse scales.
   For further details, the reader is referred to [12–15] for the concept of a locally attached
microstructure and to [13] for the localization/homogenization scale bridging strategy adopted here.

                      3. MULTISCALE HYPER REDUCTION FRAMEWORK

Here, a reduction/hyper reduction framework is presented for dramatically accelerating the solution
of nonlinear dynamic multiscale problems modeled using the multiscale formulation based on the
concept of a locally attached microstructure overviewed above. Specifically, the POD method is used
to construct a PROM at each scale, and a computational approach based on the ECSW method [4,
5] is developed to hyper reduce it. Training at all scales k = 1, … , N is performed in situ (or on-
the-fly), or using an auxiliary, coarser, computational macroscale model designed for this purpose,
which leads to impressive speedups even for non-parametric multiscale problems. For a parametric
problem setting, training is performed at scale k = 0 using the traditional offline– online approach
in order to gain additional computational efficiency.

I of the two-step training strategy described in this section is to train a ROB for the solution of
microscale problems using the original microscale models, but an auxiliary, coarser computational
macroscale model. Although somewhat less convenient from a user perspective than the in situ strat-
egy, this approach is attractive for a class of simulations in which there is an insufficient diversity
of microscale snapshots available in the first few time-steps to admit the simplest variant of the in
situ strategy. In such cases, it furnishes a more implementationally straightforward alternative than
any of the refinements of the in situ training discussed in the previous section and can potentially
reduce training costs by eliminating the post-processing of solution snapshots required to update the
microscale PROM in the adaptive setting.

                                           4. APPLICATIONS

The nonlinear, multiscale, PMOR framework described in the previous sections of this paper was
implemented by the authors in the general-purpose FE software AERO-S [50, 51]. Here, the com-
putational efficiency of this framework is demonstrated using a number of nonlinear multiscale
simulations with N + 1 = 2 scales. Its accuracy is illustrated, and its versatility and potential for sig-
nificant wall-clock and CPU time reductions are highlighted. Specifically, this framework is applied
to the solution of two academic but nevertheless computationally intensive nonlinear, multiscale,
dynamic response problems. All ROBs are constructed using the POD method based on displace-
ment (and when applicable, Lagrange multiplier) solution snapshots, and all constructed PROMs
are applied to the same parametric configuration for which they have been trained. Wall-clock time
and CPU time reduction factors including all offline costs are reported in order to demonstrate the
ability to achieve true speedups even under such conditions which are unusual for model reduction.

                                           5. CONCLUSIONS

This paper describes a highly efficient computational framework for solving structural and solid
mechanics problems that exhibit multiple, separable scales. This framework uses the concept of a

locally attached microstructure at N + 1 scales, leverages PMOR techniques to reduce the dimen-
sionality of the governing equations at all scales, and relies on the ECSW hyper reduction method
to efficiently evaluate the resulting nonlinear reduced-order terms. It also enforces the scale trans-
mission conditions without any operation that scales with the size of a HDM. At each scale k =
1, … , N, the hyper reduced PROM is trained on-the-fly or using an auxiliary, coarser, computa-
tional macroscale model, which leads to significant speedup factors even in a non-parametric setting.
For parametric settings, an offline–online decomposition can be used to train the macroscale level
(k = 0) and deliver a near-real-time multiscale model.
   Performance results obtained for the two-level instance of this framework (N + 1 = 2) and two
academic but nevertheless computationally intensive nonlinear, multiscale, dynamic response prob-
lems show that the proposed nonlinear, multiscale framework for PMOR is capable of delivering
wall-clock time speedup factors of the order of tens of thousands and CPU time speedup factors of
the order of millions, while maintaining a desired level of accuracy.
