K Introduction


The theory of invariants plays a central role in many branches of
mathematics and physics, for instance, in projective geometry and in
quantum mechanics. The object of this chapter is to present an account
of algebraic invariant theory from the point of view of its application
to modern continuum mechanics.
   Numerous isolated instances of the application of invariance principles
to problems in continuum mechanics may be found in the literature,
but it is only comparatively recently that the significance of these
principles has come to be commonly recognized.
   The modern developments in the application of invariant theory in
continuum mechanics date largely from the work of Rivlin (1948a, and
many subsequent papers) on the elastic strain-energy function, and by
Reiner (1945) and Rivlin (1948b) on the constitutive equations of
non-Newtonian fluids. Oldroyd (1950) considered the formulation of
properly invariant constitutive equations for more general viscoelastic
materials. Rivlin and Ericksen (1955) began the study of nonlinear
viscoelastic materials from a rather different point of view, taking as
their starting point the proposition that the stress tensor components
can be expressed as (or approximated by) polynomials in the components
                                        III. THEORY OF INVARIANTS      241


of certain kinematic tensors with suitable invariance properties; material
symmetries then impose certain invariance requirements on the form of
the constitutive relation. This theory has been extensively developed
and is reviewed by Rivlin (1965a,b). Another form of the theory of
viscoelastic materials, in which the stress components are expressed as
functionals of the strain or strain-rate components, was formulated by
Green and Rivlin (1957) and by Noll (1958). In these theories, the forms
of the constitutive functionals are subject to invariance conditions in
the presence of material symmetries. This functional theory of visco-
elasticity has also been the subject of a great deal of study; the theory,
and its relation to the earlier theory mentioned above, is also reviewed
by Rivlin (1965a,b). The formulation of constitutive equations for more
general classes of problems in continuum physics, using methods similar
to those employed in the formulation of viscoelasticity theories, has
been described by Pipkin and Rivlin (1959) and Rivlin (1960).
   The papers mentioned above are concerned at least partly with the
applications of invariance principles to continuum mechanics and
physics (the list is in no way intended to be comprehensive). Work
which deals with specific problems in invariant theory which arise
from these applications will form a large portion of Part III, and
references will be given at appropriate points in the following sections.
Recent general accounts of continuum mechanics, written from various
points of view, include Truesdell (1952), Truesdell and Toupin (1960),
Truesdell and Noll (1965), Green and Adkins (1960), Eringen (1962),
and Sedov (1962). Some discussion of the role of invariant theory is
given in several of these texts.
   In Part III, it is not the intention to deal with applications of
invariant theory, but rather to present a fairly self-contained account of
the mathematical theory, although Part III is written with applications
to continuum mechanics in mind. Naturally, many of the problems to
be discussed also have applications in fields other than continuum
mechanics, and, in fact, use will be made of material drawn from the
classical theory of algebraic invariants (whose motivation is mainly
geometrical) and of results in the theory of group representations,
which has been extensively applied to quantum mechanics. However,
topics which do not at present appear to be readily applicable to
continuum mechanics have been excluded. For example, no account
will be given of invariance under the full linear group of transformations,
or under Lorenz transformations; nor will we discuss invariants of
vectors and tensors in more than three dimensions, although in some
cases the extension from two and three to n dimensions is straight­
forward.
242    A. J. M. SPENCER

   The field of application also dictates the terminology and to a large
extent the notation employed. Invariants throughout will be regarded
as invariants of vectors and tensors, which may be given physical
interpretations, rather than of algebraic forms as in the classical theory.
It is often found convenient to associate matrices with the vectors and
tensors and to employ matrix notation.
   The remainder of this section is concerned with notation and
definitions, an account of some results of the classical invariant theory,
and a description of the transformation groups which are of importance
in continuum mechanics. The main part of Part HI is contained in
Arts. 2-4, where the invariants of vectors and tensors under various
groups of orthogonal transformations are systematically discussed. The
theory of form-invariant tensor polynomial functions is given in Art. 5,
and a brief account of invariant and form-invariant tensor functionals
follows in Art. 6. Article 7 is concerned with methods of establishing
that a set of invariants of a given set of vectors and tensors under a given
transformation group is minimal. In Art. 8, some recent results about
invariants and form-invariant tensors which are not polynomial functions
are stated.
   Since the manuscript of this article was completed, a number of new
results have been published. In particular, attention is drawn to a paper
by Smith (1968) which describes an alternative and very elegant
method of deriving many of the results of Arts. 2-4.

1.2.   REDUCIBLE AND IRREDUCIBLE INVARIANTS; INTEGRITY BASES


  The central problem in the theory of invariants is: For a given set of
variables (in our case, vectors and tensors) and a given group of trans-
246    A. J. M. SPENCER

formations, determine a set of basic invariants (assuming it to exist)
from which all other invariants can be generated and which contains no
redundant members. For the present, attention will be confined to
invariants which are polynomials; the justification for this approach
will be given in Arts. 1.3 and 8.
   The following definitions apply for a given set of variables and a
given group of transformations. A polynomial invariant is said to be
reducible if it can be expressed as a polynomial in other invariants;
otherwise, it is said to be irreducible. A set of polynomial invariants which
has the property that any polynomial invariant can be expressed as a
polynomial in members of the given set is called an integrity basis;
an integrity basis is minimal if it contains the smallest possible number
of members. Clearly, all members of a minimal integrity basis are
irreducible.
   It frequently happens that polynomial relations exist between
invariants which do not permit any one invariant to be expressed as a
polynomial in the remainder. Such relations are called syzygies.
   The main problem, therefore, is to determine minimal integrity bases
and the syzygies for given sets of variables under various groups of
transformations. The solution of this problem (in so far as it can be
given at present) for the case in which the variables are components of
vectors and second-order tensors in three dimensions, and the trans­
formation groups are the orthogonal groups and certain of their sub­
groups, will form the main substance of Part III.


1.3.   RESULTS FROM CLASSICAL THEORY



   The theory of algebraic invariants was extensively studied by nine­
teenth century mathematicians, and a large body of theory and results
exists as a result of these studies. This theory is described in, for example,
the books by Grace and Young (1903), Elliott (1913), Turnbull (1960),
Weitzenböck (1923), and Gurevich (1964). This work, however, is not
as useful for applications to modern continuum mechanics as might
appear at first sight. The main reason for this is that the motivation
for most of the classical work was geometrical, and its exponents were
concerned mainly with projective geometry, which is in effect the study of
invariance of vectors and tensors under the full linear group of trans­
formations. Consequently, most of the results of classical theory concern
invariants under general linear transformations. It is true that, since
for a given number of dimensions the orthogonal groups and their
                                         III. THEORY OF INVARIANTS       247


subgroups are all subgroups of the linear group, it is possible to deduce
certain results concerning, say, the full orthogonal group from related
results for the full linear group; for example, it is quite easy to prove
that an integrity basis for a given set of vectors and tensors under the
full orthogonal group is given by an integrity basis for the same set of
vectors and tensors, together with one further symmetric second-order
tensor, under the linear group, when the further adjoined tensor is set
equal to the Kronecker delta S^ . However, an integrity basis for
orthogonal transformations which is obtained in this way is not, in
general, minimal, and in practice it is more convenient to obtain results
for the orthogonal groups directly rather than to deduce them from
results for the full linear group.
   A more modern approach to invariant theory is contained in the book
by Weyl (1939). This is mainly concerned with general theory, rather
than with special problems.
   In spite of its drawbacks from the present standpoint, the classical
theory includes a number of essential general results which should be
stated. The principal one of these is Hilberths Theorem, which may be
stated as follows:
  For any finite system of vectors and tensors, there exists an integrity
basis which consists of a finite number of invariants.
The proof of this theorem is rather lengthy, and is given, for example,
in Gurevich (1964). The theorem is of great importance in that it
asserts the existence of a finite integrity basis for any finite system of
vectors and tensors, and so justifies the search for such a basis.
   Another theorem of general applicability is Peano's Theorem. Consider

2.7.   FULL ORTHOGONAL G R O U P ; INVARIANTS OF VECTORS
       AND SECOND-ORDER TENSORS



   The invariants obtained in the previous article, which are absolute
invariants under the proper orthogonal group, are either absolute or
relative invariants under the full orthogonal group; the relative invariants
change sign under an orthogonal transformation whose determinant is
equal to — 1 . As in Art. 2.1, we use the term odd invariant to describe
an invariant which does change sign, and even invariant for an invariant
which does not change sign, under an orthogonal transformation with
determinant equal to — 1 . Then both odd and even invariants are
absolute invariants under the proper orthogonal group, but only even
invariants are absolute invariants under the full group. We note that
since the full group includes the proper group, any invariant under
the full group is also invariant under the proper group; however, if it is
irreducible under the full group it is not necessarily irreducible under
the proper group.
   In considering the full orthogonal group, it is necessary to distinguish
between absolute and axial vectors, whose transformation laws are
defined by Eqs. (1.1.7) and (1.1.8). It is easily verified that any invariant
which is of odd degree in the components of absolute vectors is odd and
any invariant which is of even degree in the components of absolute
290   A. J. M. SPENCER

vectors is even; the degree in the components of second-order tensors
and axial vectors does not affect whether the invariant is odd or even.
   It is therefore possible, for a given set of vectors and tensors, to
generate all polynomial invariants under the full group from the
invariants obtained from Table II for the given set. For, suppose that
for the given vectors and tensors, Table II yields a set S of invariants,
and that / is a polynomial invariant under the full group. Then / is also
a polynomial invariant under the proper group and so expressible as a
polynomial in the members of 5 . In order for / to be invariant under
the full group, this polynomial must be of even degree in those members
of S which are odd invariants, and every such polynomial is invariant
under the full group. Consequently every polynomial invariant under
the full group is a polynomial in the members of the appropriate integrity
basis for the proper group, which is of even degree in the odd invariants
of the basis.
   It does not follow that an integrity basis for the full group can be
derived by deleting odd invariants from the corresponding integrity
basis for the proper group, for certain even invariants which are
irreducible when regarded as invariants under the full group can be
expressed in terms of odd invariants, and so do not appear in the
integrity basis for the proper group. It is possible to obtain a finite,
but not in general a minimal, integrity basis for the full group, by
replacing odd invariants of the corresponding basis for the proper group
by their squares and products in pairs; we shall, however, proceed
otherwise.
   Before proceeding to the integrity bases for the full group, it is worth
noting that if we are concerned with algebraic or rational, as distinct
from polynomial, invariants, it may well be necessary to take odd
invariants into consideration since, for example, the ratio of two odd
invariants is an even invariant.

4* The Crystal Classes


   The known results concerning invariants of vectors and tensors
under the groups of transformations corresponding to the crystal classes
are mainly due to Smith and Rivlin. References to their work are given
in the following articles. The cases which have been dealt with are
the invariants of a single symmetric second-order tensor, of a vector
and a symmetric second-order tensor, and of an arbitrary number of
vectors, and irreducible integrity bases have been obtained for these
cases. The syzygies between these invariants have also received a certain
amount of study.
   Since there are thirty-two crystal classes to be considered, and the
results are in many cases of considerable complexity, we will not attempt
to reproduce all the known results but will proceed rather by illustrative
examples and give appropriate references to fuller results which are
available elsewhere.
   The transformation groups to be considered were described in
Art. 1.4d and are tabulated in Table I of that article. For brevity,
we will describe each class by the number assigned to it in Table I;
thus Class 1 denotes the pedial class of the triclinic system, Class 9 the
tetragonal-disphenoidal class of the tetragonal system, and so on. It

should be noted that this terminology is adopted solely for convenience
in the present work and is not a standard notation.

4.5.   INVARIANTS OF AN ARBITRARY NUMBER OF VECTORS



   Integrity bases for an arbitrary number of vectors have been obtained

The results are again quite complex and again only a few of the simple
cases will be described. For a given class it is necessary to consider

Peano's Theorem (Art. 1.3). Results for an arbitrary finite number of
vectors are obtained by making appropriate substitutions in the integrity
bases for N vectors.
   The methods employed by Smith and Rivlin to determine the integrity
bases for the triclinic, monoclinic, rhombic, and tetragonal systems are
essentially the same as those used in Arts. 4.2 and 4.4. These methods
were modified somewhat for certain classes of the hexagonal system,
and a different procedure was used for the classes of the cubic system.
These modified procedures will not be described here; they depend on

discussed in Art. 7.1 to determine the number of linearly independent
invariants of given degrees in the components of the vectors, and the
method employed is to construct sufficient independent invariants of
successively higher degrees. Peano's theorem indicates when the process
may be terminated. In the case of the cubic system, the algebraic
difficulties are considerable.

  The procedure which has been followed so far in constructing integrity
bases for vectors and tensors under various transformation groups has

been to begin by constructing in each case an integrity basis which

7.3.   MINIMALITY OF INTEGRITY BASES. T H E CRYSTAL CLASSES


   The integrity bases for invariants of a single tensor a^ for all the
crystal classes, which were described in Arts. 4.2 and 4.3 were shown
to be minimal by Smith (1962b). Smith, in fact, proved a stronger
result, namely that any polynomial invariant can be expressed uniquely
in the form (4.2.4). This was done by computing the number of distinct
invariants of the form (4.2.4) of each degree in the components of α^ ,
and showing that this number is equal to the number of linearly inde­
pendent invariants of the same degree, by an extension of the methods
described in Art. 7.1.
   The integrity bases for invariants of a single symmetric tensor and
a vector (cf. Art. 4.4) were shown to be minimal by Smith et al. (1963).
For the triclinic, monoclinic, and rhombic systems minimality can be
established by inspection. For the remaining systems the method
described in Arts. 7.1 and 7.2 (with some modifications) was employed.
In each case tables were given of the numbers of linearly independent
invariants, basis invariants, and invariants constructed from invariants of
lower partial degrees, and sufficient syzygies constructed to show that
the integrity bases derived in the same paper are minimal.
   Similar remarks apply to the integrity bases for an arbitrary number of
vectors (Art. 4.5), except in the case of the cubic system, where the
method of construction of the basis in itself assures minimality. Smith
and Rivlin (1964) derived the integrity bases and proved them to be
minimal.
