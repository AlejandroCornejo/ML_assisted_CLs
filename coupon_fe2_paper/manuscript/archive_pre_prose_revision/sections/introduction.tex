\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Representing the effective hyperelastic response of a heterogeneous material requires capturing how its constituents and geometry accommodate deformation. At finite strains, their interaction can produce anisotropy and coupling between loading modes that are difficult to describe with a predetermined macroscopic energy function. Computational homogenization provides a numerical route to this response: a deformation is imposed on a representative volume element (RVE), a microscopic boundary-value problem is solved, and the resulting fields yield effective stresses and constitutive derivatives~\cite{Miehe1999}. The RVE thus provides both a means of characterizing the material and a source of data for constructing a less expensive constitutive representation.

Obtaining this response over a range of deformation states can require many nonlinear finite element solves. Repeating these calculations during material characterization or a subsequent structural analysis increases both execution time and the resources devoted to microscopic states and operators. Surrogate models seek to reuse representative RVE solutions computed offline so that new constitutive evaluations can be performed at substantially lower online cost. The difficulty is not only computational: a surrogate must represent the response accurately while respecting the constitutive structure required by its intended use.

Two broad approaches are available. Non-intrusive constitutive surrogates learn the relation between macroscopic deformation and effective response from RVE data, without access to the microscopic residual and tangent operators. Intrusive projection-based reduced-order models (PROMs) instead use these operators to approximate the microscopic equilibrium problem in a low-dimensional space; hyperreduction limits the local contributions needed to evaluate the reduced equations. This is not a distinction between models with and without physics. A non-intrusive model can embed constitutive restrictions through physics-augmented neural networks (PANNs), while an intrusive PROM can employ an artificial neural network (ANN) to enrich its displacement representation.

The present work develops a polyconvex PANN construction for the effective anisotropic hyperelastic response of an RVE. Its central question is how to increase the flexibility of the constitutive representation while preserving the selected mechanical conditions, including an exactly stress-free reference state. PROM-based surrogates provide a complementary assessment: they approximate the same RVE through its displacement field and governing equations rather than through a learned macroscopic energy. A structural tensile example subsequently illustrates the use of the resulting models beyond isolated constitutive evaluations. The development of the PANN construction, its assessment alongside reduced-order alternatives, and this application form the three parts of the study.

\subsection{Physics-augmented neural networks}
The non-intrusive route begins with a constitutive approximation: given the macroscopic deformation, predict the response that would otherwise require an RVE solve. Neural models of homogenized material behavior~\cite{Aldakheel2023} and physics-constrained networks coupled to automated microscopic data generation~\cite{kalina2023} illustrate this approach. Its attraction is that the online model no longer needs a microscopic mesh or equilibrium iterations. Its central difficulty is ensuring that approximation of the sampled response is compatible with the constitutive restrictions imposed on the learned law. The required state description also depends on the material: learning microscopic histories~\cite{Liu2022}, directional polymer models with history variables~\cite{Ghaderi2020}, and staggered training across interacting scales in woven materials~\cite{AsadFarhat2026} address path-dependent behavior. The present work concerns history-independent hyperelasticity, for which the current deformation describes the constitutive state.

Even in this setting, fitting stress values is not sufficient to define a mechanically consistent constitutive law. For a hyperelastic material, the stress must derive from a stored-energy function. Direct regression of its components does not enforce that relation: an accurate fit at sampled states can coexist with nonzero net work around a closed deformation cycle. Learning a scalar energy and obtaining stress by differentiation enforces the potential structure, as emphasized by As'ad et al.~\cite{asad2022}, and supplies the corresponding major symmetry of the tangent. Objectivity can also be imposed through deformation measures that leave the energy unchanged under superposed rigid rotations. PANNs incorporate such constitutive structure into the learned representation~\cite{klein2022,linden2023}. The term identifies this modeling strategy, not a single architecture or a guarantee that every PANN satisfies the same set of conditions. Which properties hold must be established for the particular construction.

Curvature introduces a further distinction. An energy potential need not satisfy a convexity condition, and a convexity condition must specify its variables. As'ad et al.~\cite{asad2022} use an input-convex neural network (ICNN) in Green--Lagrange strain. Convexity in that strain measure has a tensile-state stability implication, but the second Piola stress enters the relation between strain-space and deformation-gradient curvature~\cite{GaoNeffRoventaThiel2017}. Similarly, a positive-matrix factorization in an incremental stress update~\cite{Xu2021} is not, by itself, an energy Hessian. These formulations therefore address different constraints.

Polyconvexity~\cite{ball1977} provides a tractable sufficient condition for rank-one convexity by representing the energy as a convex function of deformation minors. In two dimensions, a convex representation in the deformation gradient and its determinant is sufficient. Together with appropriate growth, coercivity, and admissibility hypotheses, this structure also supports existence results in nonlinear elasticity~\cite{Ciarlet1988}. It does not guarantee uniqueness or convergence of a particular Newton algorithm. For anisotropic hyperelasticity, the challenge is to impose the selected conditions without making the representation too restrictive to fit the homogenized response. This is the constitutive question pursued here. Evaluating such a construction alongside PROMs also places its accuracy and computational cost in the context of an alternative that retains a reduced approximation of the RVE equations.

\subsection{Projection-based reduced-order models}
The intrusive route retains the governing equilibrium equations instead of learning only their homogenized constitutive output. Projection-based model order reduction (PMOR) seeks their solution in a low-dimensional approximation space. For a microscopic displacement problem, a conventional PROM represents the displacement field in an affine space spanned by a reduced-order basis, usually constructed by proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) of solution snapshots. The basis coefficients are the generalized coordinates solved for online. Reduced finite-strain homogenization~\cite{yvonnet2007,Hernandez2014} and potential-based two-scale calculations with GPU acceleration~\cite{Fritzen2016} establish this route to reducing microscopic cost. Reduced periodic-substructure methods also operate in domain-decomposition settings~\cite{Hernandez2020}. Here, PROMs are used to approximate the RVE boundary-value problem and its effective response, providing an equation-based counterpart to the PANN approximation.

There are two distinct costs to reduce. The first is the number of independent unknowns. A small affine space may represent a curved family of microscopic solutions poorly, even when that family depends on few loading parameters. Local reduced bases~\cite{Amsallem2012}, autoencoder-based manifolds~\cite{Lee2020}, and quadratic approximation manifolds~\cite{Barnett2022} address this representational difficulty in different ways. The PROM--ANN approach of Barnett et al.~\cite{barnett2023} and the latent-space closure framework of Ares De Parga et al.~\cite{aresdeparga2026nonlinear} instead partition the reduced coordinates into primary and secondary groups. The primary generalized coordinates remain independent unknowns, while a learned closure predicts the secondary generalized coordinates from them. A rich displacement representation can therefore be reconstructed while solving for only a few independent coordinates. We adopt this primary/secondary nomenclature.

The second cost is the evaluation of the reduced equations. Reducing the number of coordinates is insufficient if every residual evaluation still requires constitutive calculations and assembly over the full microscopic mesh. Sampling-and-weighting hyperreduction approximates these evaluations through a selected subset of local contributions and associated weights. Optimized cubature~\cite{An2008}, energy-conserving sampling and weighting (ECSW)~\cite{Farhat2014,farhat2015}, and the empirical cubature method (ECM)~\cite{hernandez2017} provide established constructions, including extensions to local bases and Petrov--Galerkin projection~\cite{Grimberg2021,AresDeParga2023}. A hyperreduced projection-based reduced-order model (HPROM) thus combines an approximation of the microscopic state with an approximation of the integrals entering its reduced equations.

The integration rule can itself exploit the state representation. Continuous empirical cubature adjusts point locations and weights~\cite{Hernandez2024}; subspace-adaptive cubature uses common locations with weights specific to each reduced subspace~\cite{Bravo2024}. Manifold-adaptive weights extend the latter idea to weights varying continuously with the reduced coordinates~\cite{hernandez2026}. We use fixed-weight ECM for the affine HPROM and manifold-adaptive cubature for HPROM--ANN, both on actual reduced meshes. These models retain a reduced equilibrium solve. A direct operating mode, D--HPROM--ANN, additionally uses the imposed strain to prescribe strain-informed coordinates and reconstruct the microscopic response without equilibrium iterations. Although this mode also avoids an online equilibrium solve, it is derived from the intrusive reduced-order construction and still evaluates the microscopic material law on selected elements. It therefore differs from a PANN that directly represents the homogenized energy.

\subsection{Feature representation and position relative to prior work}
\label{sec:discussion}
For the PANN construction, representational flexibility depends on more than the size of the network. Unlike a PROM, which reconstructs microscopic displacements and evaluates the given constituent law, an energy-based PANN must parameterize the effective anisotropic energy itself. Its constitutive inputs are therefore part of the approximation, not merely a preprocessing choice. Structural tensors encode material directions, and invariants formed from these tensors provide objective measures of deformation. Polyconvex invariant formulations have been developed for anisotropic elasticity~\cite{SchroderNeff2003}, while models of distributed fiber orientations illustrate why the directional description can affect predicted deformation mechanisms~\cite{Gasser2006}. In a neural constitutive model, these scalar measures act as the network's features.

A useful sufficient construction combines polyconvex features with an energy core that is convex and nondecreasing in every feature. ICNNs provide the convex core~\cite{amos2017}; anisotropic invariant formulations and compatible growth and reference-state corrections are developed by Klein et al.~\cite{klein2022} and Linden et al.~\cite{linden2023}. Other structured parameterizations include constitutive artificial neural networks~\cite{Linka2023} and polyconvex neural ordinary differential equations~\cite{Tac2022}. For spline-based Kolmogorov--Arnold networks (KANs), constitutive KANs combine energy modeling, partial monotonicity, and symbolic interpretation~\cite{Abdolazizi2025}, whereas monotonic input-convex KANs (ICKANs) add the convexity constraints used in their polyconvex invariant composition~\cite{thakolkaran2025}.

The features determine which responses this constrained core can represent. Let $\bm z$ be the feature vector and $H$ a nondecreasing core. For two deformation states,
\[
 \bm z(\F_a)\leq\bm z(\F_b)
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 H\!\left(\bm z(\F_a)\right)\leq H\!\left(\bm z(\F_b)\right),
\]
where the feature inequality is componentwise. A target core response with the opposite ordering cannot be fitted by increasing the network width. Additional reference and volumetric terms must be included before applying this reasoning to the complete energy, but they do not remove the core's ordering restriction. This simple observation makes feature design consequential before optimization begins.

Klein et al.~\cite{klein2022} provide a pertinent example: their invariant model struggles with shear in a cubic lattice, while a deformation-gradient-based polyconvex model fits that response more accurately. The invariant approach performs well on their transversely isotropic example. The contrast identifies a representation issue, rather than a general failure of polyconvexity. More recent generalized-structure-tensor models learn aspects of anisotropy~\cite{Kalina2024}, and objective triclinic polyconvex formulations can be constructed without prescribing a nontrivial material symmetry group~\cite{klein2026}. Accordingly, the question addressed here is not whether anisotropy can be learned at all, but how a particular admissible feature family can gain flexibility while retaining a compatible stress-free reference.

\subsection{Present contribution}
The main contribution is a paired directional feature family with trainable orientations, stretch powers, and determinant exponents subject to explicit joint-convexity bounds. Pairing is chosen so that each feature's strain derivative at the undeformed state is proportional to the identity tensor. The resulting reference stress can then be cancelled by a single scalar correction affine in the determinant, while the positive volumetric stiffness terms remain independently adjustable. Away from the reference state, the paired terms can still produce an anisotropic response. The construction thus links representational flexibility to a normalization that preserves polyconvexity.

The same feature family is coupled to a smooth ICNN and an integrated-hat spline ICKAN. Both realizations enforce objectivity, an exactly stress-free reference, two-dimensional polyconvexity, and the specified growth behavior. For the spline realization, the tails have analytically matched derivatives. Nonnegative energy is treated separately through a sufficient lower bound evaluated for the selected trained parameters. The construction builds on existing ICNN and ICKAN principles and affine-determinant normalization; the specific contribution is their realization with this paired anisotropic family and the accompanying analytical conditions.

To assess this construction, the study brings together neural constitutive models and PROM-based approximations of the same RVE. Direct stress regression and an unrestricted energy model distinguish fitting accuracy from the properties imposed by the polyconvex architectures. The affine HPROM, iterative HPROM--ANN, and direct decoder mode supply complementary accuracy--cost comparisons based on reduced displacement representations and different levels of online equilibrium work. POD, latent-space closure, ECM, and manifold-adaptive cubature are established ingredients used for this assessment, not new reduction algorithms proposed here. Their role is to examine the consequences of approximating the constitutive response directly or retaining an approximation of the RVE state and equations. \subsection{Assessment strategy and scope}
The numerical assessment first addresses the constitutive construction on a periodic cell. It asks whether the constrained feature family can represent the effective anisotropic response and what its imposed properties add beyond pointwise stress accuracy. Four neural tiers are considered: direct stress Regression, an unrestricted scalar-energy model termed Free, and the ICNN and ICKAN energies. Closed-cycle work tests integrability, while rank-one curvature addresses a condition not implied by potential structure alone. This attention to constitutively relevant derivatives is also motivated by the benchmarking study of Tac et al.~\cite{Tac2024}.

The tensile-coupon example then illustrates a structural use of the models. In a first-order two-level finite element calculation, denoted FE$^2$, the deformation at each macroscopic integration point drives an RVE problem whose effective stress and tangent enter macroscopic equilibrium. Replacing those repeated RVE solves with the surrogates allows their errors to propagate through a common structural calculation. A nested full-order calculation (FOM--FE$^2$) provides the computational reference for displacement and stress fields, convergence behavior, and online wall time; field comparisons complement aggregate errors~\cite{Chmiel2024}. The purpose is to demonstrate deployment and assess numerical behavior on a controlled tensile problem, not to introduce a new FE$^2$ scheme or establish general robustness.

The assessment concerns one periodic material under plane-strain kinematics, a prescribed training domain, and one structural loading path. The coupon uses the same RVE and is not a second material validation. Agreement with FOM--FE$^2$ measures surrogate error within the adopted first-order homogenization model; it does not independently validate that model against a structure resolving every microscopic cell. No such cell-resolved comparison is performed here, and the study does not establish applicability to regimes with microscopic buckling or localization. Within the investigated scope, the results demonstrate compatibility between accurate fitting and the proposed polyconvex construction, not universality across microstructures, three-dimensional constitutive completeness, or history-dependent behavior. The effects of feature learning, reference normalization, and network parameterization are assessed jointly in the proposed construction. Attributing the gain separately to feature learning, determinant exponents, normalization, or spline curvature requires matched-budget ablations.

Constitutive counterexamples are interpreted within their reference coverage. A nonzero closed-cycle work exposes nonconservativity in Regression, whereas sampled curvature tests complement, but do not replace, an analytical guarantee. The present extrapolative Free-model curvature witness lacks a converged FOM reference and is reported as a model-level observation, not as an FOM-validated spurious instability. Successful coupon convergence likewise does not certify a constitutive property. Finally, the online cost comparison excludes offline training and data-generation costs and reports the actual process/thread configurations and repetition counts. It compares the implemented online workflows under the reported configurations; it does not constitute a matched-resource statistical ranking or an offline-inclusive break-even analysis. Memory savings are a motivation for model reduction, but are not quantified in this assessment.
