\begin{abstract}
Physics-augmented neural networks (PANNs) for hyperelastic constitutive
modelling are commonly held to a six-point admissibility checklist:
(1) thermodynamic consistency, (2) objectivity, (3) tensor symmetry,
(4) normalization at the reference configuration, (5) polyconvexity, and
(6) a growth/coercivity condition~\cite{Klein2022,Ball1977}.  This
paper works through each point in full mathematical detail for a
single certified model of the two-dimensional nonlinear hyperelastic
representative volume element (RVE) treated in this project, an
energy-based PANN built from an input-convex neural network (ICNN) acting
on balanced directional invariants of $\bm F$ and $\Cof \bm F$.  Four of the six
points -- (1)--(4) -- are satisfied exactly, by construction, with a
closed-form proof given below for each, and the growth condition (6) is
supplied architecturally by construction as well, short of the
Sobolev-exponent matching a fully rigorous existence proof would
additionally require.  The remaining point, polyconvexity in the sense of
Ball~\cite{Ball1977}, is proved by an explicit construction rather than a
numerical penalty; we also verify numerically the weaker
rank-one/Legendre--Hadamard condition it implies, and separately show
that a strictly stronger, globally SPD-tangent condition -- used
elsewhere in the literature (e.g.\ As'ad, Avery and
Farhat~\cite{Asad2022}) for a different anisotropic RVE, for its own
well-documented reasons -- is not implied by polyconvexity and is, for
this RVE's own full-order model (FOM), directly contradicted by the
measured ground-truth tangent.  The model is trained on the training
trajectories only, evaluated once on an untouched, held-out test
trajectory, and compared against a
projection-based hyper-reduced-order model (HPROM--ANN, in both its
iterative-corrector and direct-evaluation operating modes) on the same
path, and against a direct strain-to-stress regression baseline with no
energy potential at all, which is shown to violate thermodynamic
consistency directly (a nonzero cyclic-work integral around closed strain
loops) despite matching or exceeding every certified model's held-out
accuracy.  A state-of-the-art survey of
eight directly relevant PANN
constructions is given, precisely comparing which admissibility
properties each achieves exactly, approximately, or not at all; it
identifies, in the surveyed authors' own words, an explicit open problem
-- combining exact objectivity, an exact stress-free reference state, and
exact polyconvexity for an anisotropic material with no assumed symmetry
group -- that the construction here resolves for this RVE.  The same
proofs are shown to transfer verbatim to a second convex core, an
input-convex Kolmogorov--Arnold network, at comparable accuracy with far
fewer parameters; and a matched, otherwise identical, uncertified
flexible PANN is used to show directly that held-out accuracy cannot
detect the absence of a polyconvexity certificate -- it matches or beats
the certified models on every accuracy metric while failing a rank-one
convexity check on 7\% of sampled, ordinary deformation states.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Multiscale (FE$^2$-type) computational homogenization
resolves each macroscopic material point by solving a nonlinear boundary
value problem on a representative volume element (RVE), so that the
macroscopic constitutive response is not postulated but computed from the
microstructure directly. This is expensive: every macroscopic Gauss point,
at every load step and every Newton iteration, requires its own converged
microscale solve. Two largely separate lines of work have grown up around
reducing this cost, and they sit on opposite sides of the classic
\emph{intrusive}/\emph{non-intrusive} divide in reduced-order and
surrogate modelling.

The intrusive route keeps the RVE's finite element structure and
governing equations explicit, and instead compresses the linear algebra:
a projection-based reduced-order model (PROM) restricts the unknown
displacement field to a low-dimensional subspace identified by proper
orthogonal decomposition (POD) of precomputed snapshots, tracing back to
the reduced-model multiscale method of Yvonnet and
He~\cite{Yvonnet2007} and developed since for nonlinear multiscale
structural problems~\cite{Zahr2017,He2020,Fritzen2016,Hernandez2014,Hernandez2020}.
A single linear subspace, however, is generally not enough to reach high
accuracy for a genuinely nonlinear problem (the Kolmogorov $n$-width
barrier), which more recent work addresses by correcting the subspace
with a trained nonlinear-manifold map (the PROM-ANN/HPROM-ANN framework
of Barnett, Farhat and Maday~\cite{Barnett2022,Barnett2023}) and by
replacing the full Gauss-point residual assembly with a sparse, learned
cubature rule via the empirical cubature method (ECM) and its
manifold-adaptive-weight extension
(MAW--ECM)~\cite{Hernandez2017,HernandezMAWECM}. Throughout this route,
the true microscale equilibrium equations are still being solved, just
in compressed and hyperreduced form: the RVE's own physics is never
bypassed.

The non-intrusive route instead treats the microscale solve as a black
box: precompute enough RVE solutions offline, then learn a direct
surrogate for the homogenized constitutive law itself, so that no
microscale solve is needed online at all. Early versions of this idea
learn a bare regression map from strain to stress; the physics-augmented
neural network (PANN) literature that has grown from it
(surveyed in full in Section~\ref{sec:related-work})~\cite{Klein2022,Linden2023,Asad2022}
instead builds physical structure into the learned energy potential
itself: thermodynamic consistency, objectivity, material-frame
symmetry, a stress-free reference state, polyconvexity, and a growth
condition, so that the surrogate is trustworthy by construction, not
merely accurate where it happens to have been trained. Both routes are
active research areas in their own right, and both are used in this
paper, not as competitors but as two independent, non-interchangeable
sources of ground truth and comparison for the same RVE, evaluated on the
same held-out load path: a rare case of the intrusive and non-intrusive
philosophies being confronted with exactly the same problem rather than
argued for in isolation.

For the non-intrusive, PANN route specifically, a recurring difficulty
motivates the deeper focus of this paper. Physics-augmented neural constitutive models are commonly held to an
admissibility checklist: (C1) thermodynamic consistency, (C2)
objectivity, (C3) tensor symmetry, (C4) a stress-free reference state, (C5)
polyconvexity, and (C6) a growth condition. The current literature
(surveyed in full in Section~\ref{sec:related-work}) shows a clear,
recurring trade-off: models that enforce every one of these properties
\emph{exactly}, by construction, do so only for a material with a
\emph{named} symmetry class (isotropic, transversely isotropic, cubic,
\dots); models that relax the symmetry assumption to a genuinely general
anisotropic material give up polyconvexity, the reference state, or exact
objectivity to do so. Linden et al.\ state explicitly that a complete,
polyconvexity-compatible invariant set may not exist for some symmetry
groups, and flag a general, symmetry-agnostic construction as an open
problem. Whether this trade-off is fundamental, or an artifact of how the
anisotropy has been encoded so far, is the central question this
paper answers for a concrete, fully worked $2$D RVE: a Neo-Hookean
solid with a hole, admitting no assumed material symmetry.

Answering that question carefully also requires knowing, precisely, what
``accurate'' does and does not certify. A model can match or exceed a
certified model's held-out accuracy while silently violating thermodynamic
consistency or losing rank-one convexity at ordinary deformation states;
this is not a hypothetical concern but something demonstrated directly on
this project's own data in Sections~\ref{sec:c1-regression}
and~\ref{sec:non-polyconvex}. This is also why the paper evaluates six
model variants side by side on the identical held-out test path,
spanning a genuine spectrum of embedded physical structure: a pure
regression surrogate with no potential at all, a free (uncertified) energy
surrogate, two independently certified polyconvex cores (ICNN and ICKAN),
and the two operating modes of HPROM--ANN, a projection-based model that
satisfies every admissibility property automatically because it solves
(a hyperreduced form of) the true microscale equilibrium equations rather
than learning a constitutive law at all.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper's own contributions are
specifically:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item A closed-form construction of a PANN for a general, group-free
 anisotropic hyperelastic material that satisfies (C1)--(C6) exactly, by
 construction, including exact polyconvexity in Ball's sense
 (Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction}), to our knowledge the first
 construction to combine exact objectivity, an exact stress-free reference
 state, and exact polyconvexity without assuming or exploiting any named
 material symmetry class (Section~\ref{sec:related-gap}). We also verify
 the correct necessary consequence of polyconvexity (rank-one convexity)
 numerically, and show that a strictly stronger, globally SPD-tangent
 condition used elsewhere in the literature for a different material is
 not required by polyconvexity and is, for this RVE's own ground truth,
 false (Section~\ref{sec:c5-wrong}).
 \item A direct, quantitative demonstration that held-out accuracy alone
 cannot certify admissibility: a pure-regression baseline with no energy
 potential is the \emph{most} accurate of all six model variants while
 directly violating (C1) (Section~\ref{sec:c1-regression}), and an
 otherwise-identical uncertified free model matches the certified models'
 accuracy while failing a rank-one convexity check on $7\%$ of sampled
 states (Section~\ref{sec:non-polyconvex}).
 \item A reaction-force-based procedure for recovering the exact,
 energy-conjugate homogenized stress from a converged HPROM--ANN state

 \item Verification that the same certificate transfers, proof-for-proof,

\end{enumerate}

\section{Background: the representative volume element and its full-order model}
\label{sec:background}
This section fixes notation and gives a self-contained, high-level account
of the RVE and its full-order model (FOM),
which every model in this paper (the PANN as well as the projection-based
and hyper-reduced comparison models of Section~\ref{sec:rom-comparison})
is ultimately trained on, evaluated against, or built from. None of the
constructions in this section are a contribution of this work; they are
established tools, cited to their sources, applied here to a specific RVE.

\subsection{The representative volume element and its full-order model}
\label{sec:background-fom}
The RVE is a square unit cell with a central circular hole, Fig.~\ref{fig:rve-geometry},
discretized by a standard displacement-based finite element mesh and
governed by a single homogeneous, isotropic, compressible Neo-Hookean solid
($E=1.628\,\mathrm{GPa}$, $\nu=0.4$). A macroscopic strain
$\bm\varepsilon=(E_{11},E_{22},\gamma_{12})$ is imposed through an affine Dirichlet
condition on the outer boundary nodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:rve-geometry}, red),
while the remaining boundary and interior nodes, including the nodes on
the hole, are left free. We write $\bm u$ for the full nodal
displacement field over every node of the mesh (this is what is plotted
in Fig.~\ref{fig:displacement-field}), partitioned into its Dirichlet and
free parts as $\bm u=(\bm u_d,\bm u_f)$: on the Dirichlet boundary,
$\bm u_d$ is prescribed exactly, node by node, by the affine
macro-strain map determined by $\bm\varepsilon$: affine in the
reference-configuration position $\bm X$ of each Dirichlet node for a
\emph{fixed} $\bm\varepsilon$, but \emph{not} a linear function of
$\bm\varepsilon$ itself, since (made explicit below) it is built from
the deformation gradient $\bm F(\bm\varepsilon)$, a nonlinear
(matrix-square-root) function of the three components
$E_{11},E_{22},\gamma_{12}$, while $\bm u_f\in\mathbb R^{n_L}$, the
free-node values, are unknown. The
finite element (Galerkin) discretization of
the governing balance of linear momentum reduces, in the quasi-static
setting used here (no inertia, no explicit time dependence: the
held-out test load path is a pseudo-time sequence of equilibrium states,
not a genuine initial-value problem), to the nodal equilibrium residual
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fom-residual}
 \bm r(\bm u_f;\bm\varepsilon) := \sum_{e=1}^{n_{\rm el}}\bm L_e^T\left(\sum_{g=1}^{n_{\rm gp}^{(e)}}\bm B_{e,g}^T\,\bm S_{e,g}(\bm u_f;\bm\varepsilon)\,w_{e,g}\right)=\bm 0,
\end{equation}
where $\bm L_e$ is the Boolean element-assembly operator, $\bm B_{e,g}$ is
the element strain-displacement operator, $\bm S_{e,g}$ is the second
Piola--Kirchhoff stress at Gauss point $g$ of element $e$ (evaluated from
the true microscopic Neo-Hookean law, not a surrogate, and depending on
$\bm u_f$ and the fixed, prescribed $\bm u_d(\bm\varepsilon)$ together), and
$w_{e,g}$ is the
corresponding FE quadrature weight (parent-domain weight times the
isoparametric Jacobian determinant). Equation~\eqref{eq:fom-residual} is
solved by Newton--Raphson for $\bm u_f$ at every prescribed $\bm\varepsilon$ along a
load path; this is the FOM, and it is what generates every training
snapshot and every held-out ground-truth number (energy, stress, and their
components) used elsewhere in this paper. Throughout this paper,
$\bm\varepsilon$ denotes this prescribed, exact boundary strain: the
deformation gradient $\bm F=\bm U=\sqrt{2\bm E(\bm\varepsilon)+\bm I}$
(the unique symmetric, rotation-free square root) is used to build the
affine map $\bm u_d(\bm X)=(\bm F-\bm I)\bm X$ directly, so the outer
boundary's own Green-Lagrange strain equals $\bm\varepsilon$ exactly, by
construction, unlike the homogenized macro-\emph{stress}, which is a
computed output with more than one admissible definition. For $\bm
u_d$ to be well-defined at all, the prescribed $\bm\varepsilon$ must
satisfy $\bm C(\bm\varepsilon):=\bm I+2\bm E(\bm\varepsilon)\succ0$, so
that a real, symmetric square root $\bm F$ exists; the implementation
enforces this explicitly (an out-of-range $\bm\varepsilon$ raises an
error rather than silently returning a spurious $\bm F$), and every
trajectory actually used for training or testing (Fig.~\ref{fig:training-trajectories})
stays at moderate strain magnitudes that satisfy it comfortably, far
from this boundary. The
homogenized macro-stress
reported throughout is \emph{not} the naive volume average of
$\{\bm S_{e,g}\}$ (see the distinction drawn in
Section~\ref{sec:results}), but the reaction-force-based, exactly
energy-conjugate quantity described there; a volume-averaged
homogenized \emph{strain} of the converged solution can likewise be
computed and differs measurably from $\bm\varepsilon$ for this
mixed Dirichlet/free RVE (the free hole boundary is not affine-constrained,
so the classical boundary-average identity that would equate the two
does not apply), but this paper does not use that quantity anywhere:
$\bm\varepsilon$, being the independent (prescribed) variable rather
than a computed output, requires no such choice.

The outer-boundary condition used here is affine Dirichlet, not
periodic. Periodic boundary conditions are the more common convention
in idealized unit-cell homogenization precisely because, under them,
the Hill--Mandel macro-homogeneity condition holds automatically at the
level of the deformation gradient, with no further argument needed
(Appendix~\ref{app:periodicity} states the exact identity, and why it
does not extend to the strain $\bm E$ without an added qualification).
Neither this identity nor its stress counterpart is automatic for a
mixed Dirichlet/free RVE like this one, which is exactly why the
applied $\bm\varepsilon$ and the volume-averaged, computed strain/stress
quantities are kept explicitly distinct above and in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. The reaction-force
procedure used there does not rely on periodicity anywhere in its
derivation: it follows from the envelope theorem at any converged
equilibrium state, for any boundary-condition type, so the same
machinery applies unchanged to RVEs for which a periodic idealization
is not assumed or not available, such as a finite specimen, an RVE
adjacent to a free edge, or a boundary condition dictated by an
experimental protocol rather than by periodic tiling.

\section{The admissibility checklist}
\label{sec:checklist}
This paper organizes six requirements for an admissible hyperelastic
surrogate $W$, with $\bm\sigma=\partial W/\partial\bm\varepsilon$, into a
single checklist for clarity of exposition. Each requirement is
individually well established and is discussed with its own citation in
the section devoted to it below; the six-item grouping itself is this
paper's own organizing device, not a claim that this exact
enumeration is issued by some external authority:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item[\textbf{(C1)}] \textbf{Thermodynamic consistency} (reduced
 Clausius--Duhem inequality): $\bm\sigma^T\dot{\bm\varepsilon}-\dot W\geq0$.
 \item[\textbf{(C2)}] \textbf{Objectivity}: $W(\bm Q\bm F)=W(\bm F)$ for
 every $\bm Q\in\SO$, i.e.\ every rigid rotation superposed on the
 current configuration.
 \item[\textbf{(C3)}] \textbf{Symmetry}: the second Piola--Kirchhoff
 stress tensor satisfies $\bm S=\bm S^T$ (equivalently, in the Voigt
 notation~\eqref{eq:stress} used throughout, there is no independently
 parameterized $S_{21}\neq S_{12}$).
 \item[\textbf{(C4)}] \textbf{Normalization}: $\bm\sigma(\bm 0)=\bm0$ and
 $W(\bm0)=0$.
 \item[\textbf{(C5)}] \textbf{Polyconvexity}, in the sense of
 Ball~\cite{Ball1977}: see Section~\ref{sec:c5-definition} for its
 precise statement and Section~\ref{sec:c5-wrong} for its relation to a
 strictly stronger alternative condition used elsewhere in the
 literature.
 \item[\textbf{(C6)}] \textbf{Growth condition}: $W\to\infty$ as
 $\bm\varepsilon\to\infty$ (with extra terms if needed).
\end{enumerate}
Sections~\ref{sec:c1}--\ref{sec:c6} (within Section~\ref{sec:theory})
take each point in turn.  (C1)--(C4) are satisfied exactly, by
construction, independent of training.  (C6)'s qualitative coercivity
structure (a barrier as $J\to0^+$ and super-quadratic growth as any
structural stretch diverges) is likewise architectural rather than a
trained or fitted behavior, though, as Section~\ref{sec:c6} makes
precise, this stops short of the Sobolev-exponent matching a fully
rigorous existence proof would additionally require.
(C5) requires the most care: Section~\ref{sec:c5} gives Ball's precise
definition, the correct (weaker) necessary condition we actually
falsify-test, and the explicit construction that proves our model
polyconvex in Ball's sense; it also shows that a strictly stronger
alternative used elsewhere in the literature is not required by
polyconvexity and is, for this RVE's own ground-truth response, false.
Before doing so, Section~\ref{sec:related-work}
places this checklist against the current PANN literature: which parts
are already correctly and rigorously solved
elsewhere, which published claims are imprecise or documentedly incorrect,
and which combination of requirements (specifically, a \emph{general}
anisotropic material with no assumed symmetry group) remains an open
problem that this work targets.

\subsection{Models that enforce a strictly stronger condition than polyconvexity}
\label{sec:related-asad}
As'ad, Avery and Farhat~\cite{Asad2022} build a hyperelastic PANN for an
anisotropic woven-fabric RVE homogenized with the same kind of FE$^2$
pipeline used in this project, and validate it inside an actual coupled
fluid--structure simulation (the supersonic inflation of a parachute
canopy), the only work surveyed here that demonstrates a downstream,
large-scale numerical-robustness benefit, not just held-out accuracy.
Their own Section~3.3 states the reason plainly: ``enforcing polyconvexity
in $\bm F$ is difficult as $\bm E$ is not in general convex in $\bm F$. On the other
hand, consider the convexity of $W$ in $\bm E$, which is equivalent to...
$\mathbb C=\partial\bm S/\partial\bm E\succeq0$.'' This is, verbatim, the
SPD-tangent condition discussed in Section~\ref{sec:c5-wrong}: a global convex
ICNN is applied directly to the raw Green--Lagrange strain, which by
elementary calculus forces $\partial\bm S/\partial\bm E\succeq0$
\emph{everywhere}, not merely at the reference state. The authors are
explicit that this is a deliberate substitute for polyconvexity, not an
oversight, and they give a real justification for it: their own Eq.~(13)
decomposes the $\bm F$-Hessian of $W$ into a material-stiffness term (made
non-negative by convexity in $\bm E$) and a geometric-stiffness term built
from the ambient stress $\bm S$ itself, which can be negative in
compression, so ellipticity in $\bm F$ (and hence buckling-type
non-uniqueness) can still be lost even though $\partial\bm S/\partial\bm E
\succeq0$ holds globally. This is a legitimate and carefully reasoned
engineering trade-off, and their finite-element examples (Section~4.4)
show it buys real numerical robustness: Newton's method diverges for a
non-convex ANN under load, and converges for their convex-in-$\bm E$ one.

It is nonetheless exactly the condition Section~\ref{sec:c5-wrong} argues
against for a material like the one in this project. Unlike convexity in
$\bm F$ itself, which As'ad et al.'s own Section~3.3 correctly notes is
incompatible with objectivity, citing Coleman and Noll's classical
counterexample, convexity in $\bm E$ raises no such contradiction,
since $\bm E$ (equivalently $\bm C$) is already an objective strain measure;
Gao, Neff, Roventa and Thiel~\cite{GaoNeffRoventaThiel2017} build a valid
existence theory on exactly this (stronger-than-polyconvex) hypothesis,
so it is a coherent, usable condition in general, not an impossible one.
The reason to avoid it for this RVE is not a general impossibility but a
specific, measured fact: the FOM tangent audit gives a negative
eigenvalue, $\FomStageTenTangentMinimum$, at a held-out test-trajectory state, so
$\partial\bm S/\partial\bm E\succeq0$ is simply false for this material's
true response, and a convex-in-$\bm E$ ICNN applied to this RVE would be
structurally unable to reproduce that state.  Two further, more mechanical
differences follow directly from this choice.  First, As'ad et al.'s
stress-free-reference correction is a fully general linear term $\bm
h\cdot\bm E$ with $\bm h=-\partial\theta/\partial\bm E(\bm 0)$ (their
Eqs.~15--16): adding \emph{any} linear function of $\bm E$ preserves
convexity in $\bm E$, so no special structure among the three strain
components is required.  A linear-in-$\bm E$ correction is a
\emph{quadratic}, generally non-convex-in-$\bm F$ form once expressed in $\bm F$,
so this shortcut is not available to a polyconvex-in-$\bm F$ construction;
this is precisely why Proposition~\ref{prop:cancellation} needs the
balanced-direction identity~\eqref{eq:balanced-directions} instead of a
free 3-parameter correction.  Second, As'ad et al.'s ICNN needs only the
classical Amos et al.\ convexity-only construction (unconstrained
input-to-hidden weights), because it is applied directly to the raw
strain; our construction needs the strictly stronger \emph{monotone} ICNN
(non-negative weights at every layer, Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction})
because the ICNN here is composed with an intermediate layer of engineered
convex features of $\bm F$, and lifting convexity through that composition
requires the outer function to be coordinate-wise non-decreasing.  Finally,
their default loss normalizes every stress component by its own
per-component standard deviation (their Eq.~2), i.e.\ always at the
extreme $\gamma=1$ of the componentwise blend~\eqref{eq:loss-blend}
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:loss}, which
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows costs a factor of roughly two in von Mises
accuracy for an RVE whose components differ in magnitude by two orders of
magnitude; this trade-off is not discussed in~\cite{Asad2022}, most likely
because their fabric material's stress components do not differ as
severely.

The same global-SPD-tangent condition, by a different mechanism, underlies
Xu, Huang and Darve~\cite{Xu2021}: rather than an ICNN, their SPD-NN
predicts the Cholesky factor of the tangent stiffness directly, so that
the stiffness matrix is symmetric positive definite by construction and
stress is integrated from it incrementally. This is applied to anisotropic
fiber-reinforced plates and to path-dependent (elasto-plastic) response,
which the ICNN constructions surveyed here do not attempt, at the cost of
the same strictly-stronger-than-polyconvex commitment as
Section~\ref{sec:related-asad}, for the same reason: a positive-definite
tangent stiffness \emph{is} $\bm D\succeq0$, restated in rate form.

\subsection{Models that enforce Ball's polyconvexity, but only for a named symmetry group}
\label{sec:related-klein-linden}
Klein et al.~\cite{Klein2022} (already used throughout
Section~\ref{sec:c5} as the primary reference for the correct definition)
give two ICNN constructions, both restricted to a material symmetry group
$\mathcal G\subset SO(3)$ specified a priori (cubic, transversely
isotropic) via a structural tensor.  Their invariant-based model $W^I$
gets objectivity, material symmetry, and polyconvexity exactly, but not
the reference state: ``the model (16) is not stress-free by construction
$\dots$ this may thus lead to a loss of ellipticity.'' Their own
explanation for why is worth quoting in full, because it is the state of
the art this project improves on: ``In Fern\'andez et al.\ (2021b), a
projection approach is proposed which fulfills the stress-free reference
configuration in an exact way. While this approach preserves polyconvexity
when formulated in terms of $\bm F$ instead of $\bm C$, it is not compatible with
the methods of incorporating objectivity and material symmetry which are
used in this work, and therefore cannot be applied.'' Their
deformation-gradient model $W^F$ makes the opposite trade: exact
polyconvexity and material symmetry, but objectivity is only
\emph{approximated} by data augmentation over $1024$ random rotations
(their Eq.~22), which they candidly report requires $64$ observers before
the shear response becomes acceptably tight (their Table~1, Fig.~4).

Linden et al.~\cite{Linden2023} close this specific gap for isotropic and
transversely isotropic materials: they construct an additive stress- and
energy-normalization term, restricted to $J$ and the symmetry-respecting
anisotropic invariants (never the raw off-diagonal components of $\bm C$), and
show this achieves exact objectivity, exact reference state, exact
polyconvexity, and exact material symmetry \emph{simultaneously}, solving
the open problem Klein et al.\ identified.  Critically, however, they do
this only within a symmetry class fixed by a structural tensor, and they
are explicit that this does not extend to a general material: ``there are
symmetry groups for which no complete set can be found that does not
violate polyconvexity $\dots$ it might be possible that there does not
exist a set of invariants to describe the anisotropy of materials with
arbitrary microstructures, e.g., 3D-printed composites.'' This is, in the
words of the researchers who solved the harder half of this problem, an
open question for exactly the case this paper addresses: an RVE with
no known or assumed symmetry class.  The mechanism used here answers it
for that case: the balanced, non-$D_4$-closed direction systems of
Table~\ref{tab:direction-systems} are not tied to any structural tensor or
symmetry group, and Proposition~\ref{prop:isotropic-features} shows they
still produce the isotropic reference-derivative structure that an exact,
polyconvexity-preserving correction requires.

A very recent paper by an overlapping set of authors, Klein et
al.~\cite{Klein2026}, extends Linden et al.'s exact-triple result (exact
objectivity, exact reference state, exact polyconvexity, all
simultaneous) from isotropic/transversely-isotropic materials to a
substantially wider set of named finite symmetry groups (cubic,
tetragonal, and others), using a triclinic (fully general) integrity
basis of $\bm C$, $\Cof(\bm C)$, and $J$ that is subsequently restricted to a
chosen group $\mathcal G$ by an explicit group-symmetrization operator:
real, recent progress on the specific gap this paper also targets.
Every one of their worked numerical examples (their Section~5: a BCC
lattice and a matrix-with-spherical-inclusion metamaterial) uses the
cubic group, and their abstract, Section~1.3, Section~4.3.2, and
conclusion state the ``fulfills all common mechanical constitutive
conditions by construction'' result for finite symmetry groups. They
also raise, as a brief forward-looking remark (Section~4.3.2 and
footnote~16), the possibility that the same model ``could also be
employed without incorporating material symmetry at all, which could be
beneficial in scenarios where no information about the underlying
symmetry of the material is available,'' an appealing idea that their
paper does not develop into a proof or a numerical example.
Appendix~\ref{app:klein-trivial} looks closely at what their own
machinery would already imply in this limit, and why we still do not
credit it as an achieved result.

What this paper adds is the part not yet carried out anywhere in the
literature, including Klein et al.'s own paper: an explicit
statement of the general-material case, a proof against (C1)--(C6), and
a worked, non-symmetric numerical demonstration
(Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction} onward). In that sense, the gap
Linden et al.\ identified for a material with no known or assumed
symmetry class is closed here by direct construction and verification,
complementing rather than competing with Klein et al.'s own,
differently-scoped result for named symmetry groups.

\subsection{A model that widens the anisotropy class by giving up polyconvexity}
\label{sec:related-kalina}
Kalina et al.~\cite{Kalina2024} take the opposite trade from
Section~\ref{sec:related-klein-linden}: they still require a user-specified
symmetry class (one of $14$ crystallographic groups), but the structure
tensor's own parameters (e.g.\ fiber directions) within that class are
learned jointly with the network from data, which is a substantially more
flexible anisotropy treatment than a fixed structural tensor. In exchange,
they explicitly decline polyconvexity: ``polyconvexity can be too
restrictive, especially for multiscale modeling,'' relying instead on
physics-augmented correction terms in the loss rather than an
architecturally convex core. Together with
Section~\ref{sec:related-klein-linden}, this is the clearest evidence in
the current literature for a real trade-off frontier: the more general the
anisotropy representation gets, the harder (and, so far, unachieved outside
this work) it has been to keep an exact, architectural polyconvexity
certificate.

\subsection{Isotropic and fiber-restricted polyconvex CANNs, and the growth condition in practice}
\label{sec:related-cann}
Linka \& Kuhl~\cite{Linka2023}, Tac et al.~\cite{Tac2023}, and
Thakolkaran et al.~\cite{Thakolkaran2025} all correctly use the additive,
block-decoupled sufficient condition for polyconvexity (convex,
non-decreasing scalar functions of $I_1$, of a polyconvex modification of
$I_2$, and of $J$, summed), and Tac et al.\ are unusually careful to state
that this is strictly stronger than what is needed and to distinguish it
explicitly from rank-one convexity: ``[r]ank-one convexity [is] weaker
than polyconvexity and not sufficient for the existence of global
minimizers.'' None of the three, however, is anisotropic beyond at most two
fixed fiber directions (transverse isotropy), and the growth condition
(C6) is uniformly \emph{stated but not exercised}: Linka \& Kuhl and Tac et
al.\ both work with perfectly or nearly incompressible materials, so $J=1$
is enforced by a Lagrange-multiplier pressure and the stated $J\to0^+$
divergence is never actually active in a trained model. Thakolkaran et
al.'s ICKAN paper is, on the polyconvexity definition itself, the most
careful of the three: it states Ball's condition precisely and does not
conflate it with anything stronger, but it uses $K_3=(J-1)^2$ as its sole
volumetric term, which is finite (equal to $1$) at $J=0$: there is no
divergent barrier at all, and (C6) is not satisfied by their model as
published. Our own volumetric term is exercised, not merely stated: the
audit in Table~\ref{tab:coefficients}--\ref{tab:direction-systems} and
Figure~\ref{fig:volumetric} samples $W_{\rm pc}$ down to $J=10^{-6}$ and up
to $J=100$ for the trained model, for a compressible, general anisotropic
material, which is a more demanding setting than any of the three
incompressible or bounded-volumetric examples above. Section~\ref{sec:ickan-construction}
shows this project's own construction transfers, proof-for-proof, to
Thakolkaran et al.'s own architecture: replacing the ICNN core with an
input-convex Kolmogorov--Arnold network, following their reference
implementation, while keeping the balanced direction systems and every
other part of the construction identical. Thakolkaran et al.\ apply
their architecture only to isotropic compressible hyperelasticity
(three isotropic invariants, above); no other work surveyed in this
section combines an ICKAN core with a general, group-free anisotropic
feature set, so the model of Section~\ref{sec:ickan-construction} is,
to our knowledge, the first anisotropic, group-free,
exactly-reference-state, exactly-polyconvex ICKAN.

Two further isotropic-only works round out this picture, at opposite ends
of the convexity-vs-flexibility trade-off already seen in
Section~\ref{sec:related-kalina}. Tac, Sahli-Costabal and Buganza
Tepole~\cite{Tac2022} originate the neural-ODE route to the same
additive, monotone-in-the-invariants sufficient condition used
in~\cite{Linka2023,Tac2023}, demonstrated on anisotropic (two-fixed-fiber)
skin data but, like the others in this subsection, without an exercised
$J\to0^+$ barrier. Abdolazizi, Aydin, Cyron and
Linka~\cite{Abdolazizi2025}, by contrast, apply a plain, unconstrained
Kolmogorov--Arnold network to the same isotropic invariants and explicitly
decline any convexity requirement at all: objectivity, material symmetry,
and the reference state are enforced by construction, but polyconvexity,
rank-one convexity, and growth are not discussed as design goals anywhere
in the paper. Their examples (rubber, brain tissue, silicone) show good
fits with no reported instability, which is consistent with, not evidence
against, the point made throughout this section: losing the polyconvexity
certificate does not necessarily show up as a visible failure on
in-distribution calibration data, which is precisely why it needs to be
guaranteed by construction rather than checked post hoc.

\subsection{Two previously identified errors, and the lesson they teach}
\label{sec:related-wrong}
Beyond the SPD/polyconvexity distinction drawn in Section~\ref{sec:c5-wrong},
the literature itself documents two concrete, checkable errors of exactly this
kind, which is useful context for how easy this is to get wrong even with
a stated intent to be rigorous.  Klein et al.~\cite{Klein2022} report that
Ghaderi et al.~\cite{Ghaderi2020} claim a polyconvex ANN potential but use
hyperbolic-tangent activations, which are not convex, so ``the potential
proposed in Ghaderi et al.\ (2020) violates the polyconvexity condition.''
We cross-checked this against the original: Ghaderi et al.\ do state, in
their own Appendix~B.1, that ``[i]f
weights, which connect the input of $\lambda_j$ to other neurons, are
positive, the proposed model holds the condition of polyconvexity,'' and
their Section~4 specifies the network core uses ``soft plus $\dots$,
sinusoid $\dots$ and hyperbolic tangent'' activations together. Weight
non-negativity alone does not make a composition convex if the activation
itself is not convex and non-decreasing (Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction});
sine is neither, and $\tanh$ is concave, not convex, on the positive
axis. Klein et al.'s report is accurate, and the error is exactly the
kind Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction} is built to avoid by construction:
stating a sufficient condition on the weights while leaving out that it
also requires convex, non-decreasing activations. They report the same
failure mode for Tac et al.\ (2021), whose model uses
the invariant $\bar I_2=\tfrac12(\tr\bar C)^2-\tfrac12\tr\bar C^2$ as a
direct network input: this particular invariant is \emph{not} polyconvex
(Hartmann and Neff, cited by Klein et al.\ as their Lemma~2.4), so despite
the stated intent, ``the polyconvexity condition is violated by the choice
of input arguments.'' Both are examples of the same lesson emphasized in
Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction}: polyconvexity is a property of the
specific functional form all the way down to the choice of activation
function and input invariant, not a label that can be attached to an
architecture in general, and a single non-convex activation or a single
non-polyconvex invariant among otherwise-correct ones is enough to void
the entire certificate.

\subsection{What remains open, and what this work contributes}
\label{sec:related-gap}
Reading Sections~\ref{sec:related-asad}--\ref{sec:related-wrong} together,
the state of the art as of this writing has, to our knowledge, not
produced a model that is simultaneously (i) exactly objective, (ii)
exactly stress-free and energy-free at the reference state, (iii) exactly
polyconvex in Ball's sense, and (iv) anisotropic without committing to a
named symmetry group, the combination Linden et al.~\cite{Linden2023}
explicitly flag as possibly requiring an invariant set that does not exist
for a general microstructure.  This paper's contribution is a
specific, checkable answer for the case of a $2$D RVE: replace the
symmetry-group structural tensor with several \emph{generic}, non-$D_4$-
closed direction systems, each individually balanced
(Table~\ref{tab:direction-systems}), so that Proposition~\ref{prop:isotropic-features}'s
isotropic-reference-derivative argument goes through without ever
assuming or checking which symmetry class, if any, the material belongs
to.  Section~\ref{sec:direction-selection} gives the explicit,
reproducible selection procedure and states plainly what is, and is
not, claimed about it: injectivity of the resulting feature map and
optimality of using exactly three direction systems are open questions
here, not results of this paper (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).  A
secondary, smaller contribution is the loss-design result of
Section~\ref{sec:loss}: none of the eight works surveyed above discusses
the possibility that per-component loss normalization (used, in its most
extreme form, by~\cite{Asad2022}, and in a milder blended form by
$\ComponentStressWeightOld{}$ of the previously certified version of this
model) can measurably \emph{hurt} accuracy on the physically relevant
equivalent stress when the material's stress components differ by orders
of magnitude, purely because it forces capacity to be spent chasing the
smallest, noisiest component's relative error.  Finally, a limitation this
work shares with every polyconvex-PANN paper surveyed except
As'ad et al.: none of Sections~\ref{sec:c1}--\ref{sec:results} demonstrate
that the polyconvexity certificate translates into the kind of downstream
finite-element robustness (guaranteed Newton convergence, bounded
transient kinetic energy) that~\cite{Asad2022} demonstrates for their
strictly stronger, $\bm E$-convex construction.  Embedding this certified
polyconvex PANN in an actual structural or multiscale solve, and checking
whether the same robustness benefits appear without requiring the
stronger and, for this material, false SPD-tangent assumption, is the
natural next step to make this defensible end-to-end rather than only at
the level of the standalone constitutive surrogate.

\section{Admissibility of the construction: theoretical properties}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Kinematics and stress convention}
\label{sec:kinematics}
Let $\bm F$ be the deformation gradient, and
\begin{equation}
 \bm C=\bm F^T\bm F,\qquad \bm E=\tfrac12(\bm C-\bm I),\qquad J=\det \bm F=\sqrt{\det \bm C}>0.
 \label{eq:kinematics}
\end{equation}
The database and the model use the engineering-shear coordinate
\begin{equation}
 \bm\varepsilon=\begin{bmatrix}E_{11}\\E_{22}\\\gamma_{12}\end{bmatrix},
 \qquad \gamma_{12}=2E_{12},
 \label{eq:e-coordinate}
\end{equation}
so that the work identity
\begin{equation}
 dW=S_{11}\,dE_{11}+S_{22}\,dE_{22}+S_{12}\,d\gamma_{12},\qquad
 \bm\sigma:=\frac{\partial W}{\partial\bm\varepsilon}
 =\begin{bmatrix}S_{11}\\S_{22}\\S_{12}\end{bmatrix}
 \label{eq:stress}
\end{equation}
returns exactly the second Piola--Kirchhoff components stored in the
direct FOM data, with no factor-two ambiguity in the shear term and no
independently trained stress network: every stress reported in this
document is $\partial W/\partial\bm\varepsilon$ of the same scalar $W$.

\subsection{(C1) Thermodynamic consistency}
\label{sec:c1}
For a purely elastic RVE with no internal (dissipative) state variables,
the reduced isothermal Clausius--Duhem inequality is
$\mathcal D:=\bm\sigma^T\dot{\bm\varepsilon}-\dot W\geq0$.  Since
$W=W(\bm\varepsilon(t))$ and $W$ is differentiable,
\begin{equation}
 \dot W=\frac{\partial W}{\partial\bm\varepsilon}\cdot\dot{\bm\varepsilon}
 =\bm\sigma^T\dot{\bm\varepsilon}
\end{equation}
identically, for \emph{any} smooth scalar potential $W$ and \emph{any}
strain history $\bm\varepsilon(t)$, by the chain rule alone.  Hence
$\mathcal D\equiv0\geq0$: the inequality holds with equality, and the
model is thermodynamically consistent (fully reversible, zero
dissipation) for the same reason every hyperelastic (Green-elastic) model
is -- because the stress is defined as $\partial W/\partial\bm\varepsilon$
in \eqref{eq:stress} and not as an independently parameterized function.
This is not a property that training can violate or that needs to be
checked numerically; it is a property of the functional form
\eqref{eq:stress} itself.

\subsection{A pure-regression baseline: violating (C1) directly}
\label{sec:c1-regression}
Section~\ref{sec:c1} shows (C1) holds for \emph{any} model of the form
$\bm S=\partial W/\partial\bm\varepsilon$, regardless of which $W$ is
learned. It says nothing about a model that is not of this form. As'ad et
al.~\cite{Asad2022} motivate their own hyperelastic-ANN construction by
first exhibiting exactly such a model, a plain regression network that
maps strain directly to stress with no potential anywhere, as their
baseline ``regression ANN'', the bottom tier of a three-tier ablation
(regression ANN, hyperelastic ANN, convex hyperelastic ANN) that
motivates each of their architectural commitments in turn. This section
repeats that ablation for this project's own RVE and its own admissibility
checklist, adding a fourth tier below the two certified polyconvex cores
and the uncertified free baseline of Section~\ref{sec:non-polyconvex}:

\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Tier 1 (this section)}: a pure-regression MLP, widths
 $(\RegressionWidths{})$, mapping normalized $[E_{11},E_{22},\gamma_{12}]$
 straight to normalized $[S_{11},S_{22},S_{12}]$: the stress output is a
 direct forward pass, never a gradient of anything. No energy functional
 exists in this model at all.
 \item \textbf{Tier 2}: the free hyperelastic PANN of
 Section~\ref{sec:non-polyconvex}, an unconstrained energy $W_\theta$,
 so (C1) holds, but nothing else in (C4)--(C6) is guaranteed.
 \item \textbf{Tiers 3a/3b}: the certified polyconvex ICNN and ICKAN of
 Sections~\ref{sec:c5-construction} and~\ref{sec:ickan}, satisfying all
 six checklist items exactly.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A direct, quantitative test of (C1).} For a genuine hyperelastic
model, integrating $\dot W=\bm S\cdot\dot{\bm\varepsilon}$
(Section~\ref{sec:c1}) along any closed strain loop $\mathcal L$ -- a path
in strain space with the same start and end point -- gives
\begin{equation}
 \oint_{\mathcal L}\bm S\cdot d\bm\varepsilon
 =\oint_{\mathcal L}dW
 =W(\bm\varepsilon_{\rm end})-W(\bm\varepsilon_{\rm start})=0,
 \label{eq:cyclic}
\end{equation}
exactly, for \emph{every} closed loop, independently of which admissible
$W$ produced $\bm S$: this is a direct corollary of (C1), not a new
physical assumption. For the tier-1 pure-regression model, $\bm S$ is not the
gradient of anything, so nothing in its construction forces
\eqref{eq:cyclic}, and there is no reason to expect it numerically. We
evaluate \eqref{eq:cyclic} by trapezoidal quadrature ($\CyclicPointsPerEdge{}$
points per edge) on four closed polygonal loops entirely inside the
training range (two axis-aligned normal-strain squares of
different size, a shear loop, and an irregular triangle mixing normal and
shear strain), for all four tiers, using each model's own stress output
(a direct forward pass for tier 1, $\partial W_\theta/\partial\bm
\varepsilon$ by automatic differentiation for tiers 2--3b).

\paragraph{Distinguishing a real violation from quadrature error.} The
mixed-triangle residuals of tiers 2--3b in Table~\ref{tab:cyclic} are not
exactly zero at $\CyclicPointsPerEdge{}$ points per edge, because the
trapezoidal rule only approximates a line integral of a nonlinear
gradient field; but \eqref{eq:cyclic} holds exactly in the continuum
limit, so this residual must vanish as the quadrature is refined,
regardless of which admissible $W$ produced $\bm S$. The tier-1 residual
has no such reason to vanish, because it is not quadrature error in the
first place -- it is the trapezoidal estimate of a line integral of a
non-conservative vector field, which is generally path-dependent and
nonzero even in the continuum limit. Refining the same $\CyclicRefinementLoop{}$
loop by $4\times$ and $16\times$ ($\CyclicRefinementLevels{}$ points per edge)
confirms exactly this:
\begin{itemize}
 \item Pure regression (tier 1): $\CyclicRefinementRegression{}$ -- stable, non-vanishing.
 \item Free (tier 2): $\CyclicRefinementFree{}$ -- shrinking toward zero.
 \item ICNN (tier 3a): $\CyclicRefinementIcnn{}$ -- shrinking toward zero.
 \item ICKAN (tier 3b): $\CyclicRefinementIckan{}$ -- shrinking toward zero.
\end{itemize}
The three energy-based tiers converge to zero at the rate expected of
trapezoidal quadrature error for a smooth integrand ($\mathcal
O(h^2)$, i.e.\ roughly a $16\times$ reduction per $4\times$ refinement);
the tier-1 residual instead converges to a fixed nonzero constant. This is
not a difference of degree: it is the signature of a genuine curl in the
tier-1 stress field, i.e.\ a direct, measured violation of (C1), as
distinct from finite-sample noise.

\paragraph{Accuracy does not reveal this violation -- if anything, it hides
it.} Table~\ref{tab:master} reports the tier-1 model's own held-out
held-out test-trajectory accuracy: $\RegressionStressError{}$ overall stress error and
$\RegressionVonMisesError{}$ von Mises error, \emph{better than every other
tier in this study}, including both certified polyconvex cores. This is
the sharpest version yet of the point made in Section~\ref{sec:non-polyconvex}
with the free baseline: an aggregate accuracy metric on a held-out
trajectory not only fails to detect the absence of a physical constraint,
it can reward the model that discards the most physics, because a direct
regression has no architectural constraint competing with the stress fit
at all. The cyclic-work test of Table~\ref{tab:cyclic} is not an
alternative way of measuring the same thing accuracy already measures; it
is the only test in this paper that checks (C1) itself; direct
observation, not a metric to be simultaneously optimized.

\subsection{(C2) Objectivity}
\label{sec:c2}
Objectivity concerns a superposed rigid rotation $\bm Q\in\SO$ of the
\emph{current} configuration, $\bm F\mapsto\bm Q\bm F$.  Since
\begin{equation}
 (\bm Q\bm F)^T(\bm Q\bm F)=\bm F^T\bm Q^T\bm Q\bm F=\bm F^T\bm F=\bm C,
\end{equation}
any scalar built only from $\bm C$ (equivalently, from $\bm F^T\bm F$, $\Cof(\bm F)^T\Cof(\bm F)=\Cof(\bm C)$,
and $\det \bm F$, as in Section~\ref{sec:c5}) automatically satisfies
$W(\bm Q\bm F)=W(\bm F)$ for every $\bm Q\in\SO$.  Every feature used by the model in
Section~\ref{sec:c5} is built exclusively from $\bm C$ and $\Cof(\bm C)$, so
objectivity holds identically, not approximately, and not because it was
included as a training penalty.  This is different from \emph{material}
symmetry, which concerns rotating the RVE relative to its own fixed
material axes and is discussed next.

\subsection{(C3) Symmetry: two different meanings}
\label{sec:c3}
The word ``symmetry'' is used for two unrelated properties in continuum
mechanics, and the checklist item (C3) refers to the first, not the
second:
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Tensor symmetry}, $\bm\sigma=\bm\sigma^T$: a basic
 consequence of balance of angular momentum in the absence of body
 couples.  It must hold for \emph{any} admissible constitutive model,
 including anisotropic ones.
 \item \textbf{Material symmetry}: invariance of $W$ under a
 \emph{material} symmetry group (e.g.\ the $D_4$ group of a square
 lattice), $W(C_{11},C_{22},C_{12})=W(C_{22},C_{11},-C_{12})$.  This is a
 \emph{modelling choice}, not a conservation law, and this project
 deliberately does \emph{not} impose it: the RVE is treated as a general
 anisotropic material, because a square-shaped numerical cell does not by
 itself make the effective homogenized material isotropic or $D_4$
 symmetric.
\end{itemize}
Tensor symmetry (the actual content of C3) holds identically in this
model: the second Piola stress is parameterized by exactly the three
independent numbers $(S_{11},S_{22},S_{12})$ of \eqref{eq:stress}, which
are then placed into the symmetric matrix
$\begin{pmatrix}S_{11}&S_{12}\\S_{12}&S_{22}\end{pmatrix}$.
There is no separate, independently learned $S_{21}$ that could disagree
with $S_{12}$: $\bm\sigma=\bm\sigma^T$ by the structure of the
parameterization, not as an approximate or penalized identity. Material
symmetry ($D_4$) is a different, optional property that this model does
not claim.

\subsection{(C4) Normalization: the stress-free reference state}
\label{sec:c4}
\subsubsection{The energy value: trivial by construction}
Let $\Phi_\theta$ be a scalar network of a feature vector $\bm
z_{\rm pc}(\bm F)$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:c5}, and let $\bm
z_{\rm pc}(\bm I)$ denote the features evaluated at the reference state
$\bm F=\bm I$.  The energy is defined as
\begin{equation}
 W_{\rm pc}(\bm F)=\Phi_\theta\big(\bm z_{\rm pc}(\bm F)\big)
 -\Phi_\theta\big(\bm z_{\rm pc}(\bm I)\big)-r\log J+\frac\beta2(J-1)^2 .
 \label{eq:pc-energy}
\end{equation}
At $\bm F=\bm I$: $\bm z_{\rm pc}(\bm F)=\bm z_{\rm pc}(\bm I)$ so the first two terms
cancel exactly; $J=1$ so $\log J=0$ and $(J-1)^2=0$.  Hence
$W_{\rm pc}(\bm I)=0$ \emph{exactly}, for \emph{any} network $\Phi_\theta$ and
\emph{any} parameters $\theta$, before any training happens.  This part
of (C4) requires no special argument about the structural directions.

\subsubsection{The stress value: not trivial, and this is where the balanced directions matter}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:pc-energy} and evaluating at $\bm\varepsilon=\bm0$
does \emph{not} automatically give zero: it requires the gradient of
the $\Phi_\theta$ term to be exactly cancelled by the gradient of the
$-r\log J$ term, and the latter only ever points in one fixed,
isotropic direction (equal normal parts, zero shear). The argument that
this cancellation actually happens has three steps: (i) show that at
the reference state, every one of the fifteen features feeding
$\Phi_\theta$ has a gradient that is a positive multiple of the
identity matrix $\bm I$ -- an \emph{isotropic} direction, regardless of how
anisotropic the feature itself is away from the reference state
(Lemma~\ref{lem:isotropic} and Proposition~\ref{prop:isotropic-features});
(ii) conclude, by the chain rule alone, that $\Phi_\theta$'s own
gradient at the reference state must then also be a non-negative
multiple of $\bm I$, however $\Phi_\theta$ was trained; (iii) show that a
single scalar $r$, matched to that multiple, exactly cancels it
against $-r\log J$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:cancellation}). We take these
in turn.

\paragraph{Step (i): every feature's reference gradient is isotropic.}
The fact this step rests on is an elementary property of the
$2\times2$ cofactor applied to a rank-one, unit-norm outer product: it
turns the direction into its own orthogonal complement.

\begin{lemma}[Isotropic reference derivative]
\label{lem:isotropic}
Let $\bm d$ be a unit vector in $\R^2$.  Then $\Cof(\bm d\otimes\bm d)=\bm I-\bm d\otimes\bm d$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $\bm d=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, so that
\begin{equation}
 \bm d\otimes\bm d=
 \begin{pmatrix}\cos^2\theta&\cos\theta\sin\theta\\[2pt]
 \cos\theta\sin\theta&\sin^2\theta\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The $2\times2$ cofactor formula (swap the diagonal entries, negate the
off-diagonal ones) gives
\begin{equation}
 \Cof(\bm d\otimes\bm d)=
 \begin{pmatrix}\sin^2\theta&-\cos\theta\sin\theta\\[2pt]
 -\cos\theta\sin\theta&\cos^2\theta\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
 -\begin{pmatrix}\cos^2\theta&\cos\theta\sin\theta\\[2pt]
 \cos\theta\sin\theta&\sin^2\theta\end{pmatrix}
 =\bm I-\bm d\otimes\bm d,
\end{equation}
using $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$ on the diagonal entries.
\end{proof}

With this in hand, the same conclusion extends from a single direction
to every one of the fifteen features in~\eqref{eq:features}, provided
the directions within each system are balanced:

\begin{proposition}[Every feature has an isotropic $\bm C$-gradient at $\bm C=\bm I$]
\label{prop:isotropic-features}
Let $\bm d_{k1},\bm d_{k2},\bm d_{k3}$ be unit vectors and $w_{k1},w_{k2},w_{k3}>0$
satisfy the balance condition $\sum_iw_{ki}\,\bm d_{ki}\otimes \bm d_{ki}=\bm I$ for
each system $k=1,2,3$, and recall the fifteen features of
Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction}: $I_1=\tr \bm C$, and, for $p=2,3$,
\begin{equation}
 Q_{k,p}^F=\sum_iw_{ki}(\bm d_{ki}\cdot \bm C\bm d_{ki})^p,\qquad
 Q_{k,p}^H=\sum_iw_{ki}(\bm d_{ki}\cdot\Cof(\bm C)\bm d_{ki})^p .
\end{equation}
Then at $\bm C=\bm I$, every one of these thirteen scalars ($I_1$ and twelve
$Q$'s -- three systems, two powers, two blocks), together with $J$ and
$J^2$, has a $\bm C$-gradient proportional to the identity: $dz_m/dC|_{\bm C=\bm I}
=c_m\,\bm I$ for every feature $z_m$, with the explicit coefficients $c_m$
collected in Table~\ref{tab:coefficients}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Taking the trace of the balance identity and using $|\bm d_{ki}|=1$ gives
$\sum_iw_{ki}\,|\bm d_{ki}|^2=\tr(\bm I)=2$, i.e.\ $\sum_iw_{ki}=2$ for every
system $k$ (consistent with the numerical weights of
Table~\ref{tab:direction-systems}). This one identity, together with
Lemma~\ref{lem:isotropic}, is all the next three cases need.

\emph{The invariant $I_1$.} Trivially, $dI_1/dC=\bm I$ for every $\bm C$, so in
particular at $\bm C=\bm I$.

\emph{The $\bm F$-block features $Q_{k,p}^F$.} By the product/chain rule,
$dQ_{k,p}^F/dC=\sum_ip\,w_{ki}(\bm d_{ki}\cdot \bm C\bm d_{ki})^{p-1}\,\bm d_{ki}\otimes
\bm d_{ki}$. At $\bm C=\bm I$, each factor $\bm d_{ki}\cdot Id_{ki}=|\bm d_{ki}|^2$ equals
$1$, so the $(p-1)$-power drops out and only the balance sum survives:
$dQ_{k,p}^F/dC|_{\bm C=\bm I}=p\sum_iw_{ki}\,\bm d_{ki}\otimes \bm d_{ki}=p\,\bm I$.

\emph{The $\Cof(\bm C)$-block features $Q_{k,p}^H$.} This case needs one
extra observation before the same trick applies: $\bm d\cdot\Cof(\bm C)\bm d$ is
itself \emph{linear} in $\bm C$, because $\bm d\cdot\Cof(\bm C)\bm d=\Cof(\bm C):(\bm d\otimes
\bm d)$ and the cofactor operation is self-adjoint under the Frobenius
inner product for $2\times2$ matrices ($A:\Cof(B)=B:\Cof(A)$, checked
by direct expansion), so $\bm d\cdot\Cof(\bm C)\bm d=\bm C:\Cof(\bm d\otimes\bm d)$ -- linear
in $\bm C$, with the \emph{constant} gradient $\Cof(\bm d\otimes\bm d)=\bm I-\bm d\otimes
\bm d$ supplied by Lemma~\ref{lem:isotropic}. Differentiating as before,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{dQ_{k,p}^H}{dC}=\sum_ip\,w_{ki}\big(\bm d_{ki}\cdot\Cof(\bm C)\bm d_{ki}\big)^{p-1}
 \big(\bm I-\bm d_{ki}\otimes \bm d_{ki}\big).
\end{equation}
At $\bm C=\bm I$, $\Cof(\bm I)=\bm I$, so $\bm d_{ki}\cdot\Cof(\bm I)\bm d_{ki}=1$ and the
$(p-1)$-power again drops out, leaving
\begin{equation}
 \left.\frac{dQ_{k,p}^H}{dC}\right|_{\bm C=\bm I}
 =p\Big[\Big(\textstyle\sum_iw_{ki}\Big)\bm I-\sum_iw_{ki}\,\bm d_{ki}\otimes \bm d_{ki}\Big]
 =p\,(2\bm I-\bm I)=p\,\bm I,
\end{equation}
using $\sum_iw_{ki}=2$ and the balance identity a second time.

\emph{The volumetric terms $J,J^2$.} The standard determinant-derivative
identity gives $dJ/dC=\tfrac1{2J}\Cof(\bm C)$, so $dJ/dC|_{\bm C=\bm I}=\tfrac12\bm I$;
then $dJ^2/dC=2J\,dJ/dC=\Cof(\bm C)$, so $dJ^2/dC|_{\bm C=\bm I}=\bm I$.
\end{proof}

for each of the $15$ features, from Proposition~\ref{prop:isotropic-features}.}
\label{tab:coefficients}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Feature & $I_1$ & $Q_{k,2}^F,\,Q_{k,2}^H$ (each of 3 systems) & $Q_{k,3}^F,\,Q_{k,3}^H$ (each of 3 systems) & $J$ & $J^2$\\

In words: every feature's gradient at the reference state points in the
same isotropic direction $\bm I$; only the multiple $c_m$ changes from
feature to feature. This is exactly the fact Step (ii) needs.

\paragraph{Steps (ii)-(iii): the multiple cancels exactly against the volumetric barrier.}
By the chain rule, $\Phi_\theta$'s own reference gradient is a weighted
sum of the fifteen feature gradients just computed, each of which is a
multiple of $\bm I$ -- so the sum is too, automatically, for \emph{any}
trained $\Phi_\theta$. Matching that multiple against the barrier
term's own (also isotropic) reference gradient is what
Proposition~\ref{prop:cancellation} makes precise.

\begin{proposition}[Exact cancellation of the reference stress]
\label{prop:cancellation}
Define
\begin{equation}
 r:=\frac12\left.\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_\theta}{\partial E_{11}}+
 \frac{\partial\Phi_\theta}{\partial E_{22}}\right)\right|_{\bm\varepsilon=\bm0}.
 \label{eq:pressure-cancellation}
\end{equation}
Then $\bm\sigma(\bm0)=\bm0$ exactly.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: $\Phi_\theta$'s reference gradient is $2\lambda \bm I$ for
some $\lambda\geq0$.} By the chain rule and
Proposition~\ref{prop:isotropic-features},
\begin{equation}
 \left.\frac{d\Phi_\theta}{dC}\right|_{\bm C=\bm I}
 =\sum_m\left.\frac{\partial\Phi_\theta}{\partial z_m}\right|_{\bm z(\bm I)}
 \left.\frac{dz_m}{dC}\right|_{\bm C=\bm I}
 =\Big(\sum_mc_m\left.\frac{\partial\Phi_\theta}{\partial z_m}\right|_{\bm z(\bm I)}\Big)\bm I
 =:\lambda \bm I .
 \label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
Because $\Phi_\theta$ is an ICNN with non-negative weights at every
layer (Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction}), every partial derivative
$\partial\Phi_\theta/\partial z_m$ is non-negative; combined with
$c_m>0$ from Table~\ref{tab:coefficients}, $\lambda$ is a non-negative
combination of non-negative terms, so $\lambda\geq0$ automatically --
independent of training, for any $\theta$. Converting from $\bm C$ to $\bm E$
via $\bm C=\bm I+2\bm E$ doubles the gradient, $d\Phi_\theta/dE|_{\bm E=0}=2\lambda \bm I$,
which in the engineering-strain coordinate~\eqref{eq:e-coordinate} reads
\begin{equation}
 \left.\frac{\partial\Phi_\theta}{\partial\bm\varepsilon}\right|_{\bm0}
 =(2\lambda,\,2\lambda,\,0)^T
\end{equation}
(the shear entry vanishes because it is the off-diagonal entry of the
isotropic matrix $2\lambda \bm I$).

\emph{Step 2: the barrier term's reference gradient has the same
isotropic shape.} Since $\log J=\tfrac12\log\det \bm C$,
$d(\log J)/dC=\tfrac1{2\det \bm C}\Cof(\bm C)$, so $d(\log
J)/dC|_{\bm C=\bm I}=\tfrac12\bm I$; converting to $\bm E$ the same way as in Step~1
gives $\partial(\log J)/\partial\bm\varepsilon|_{\bm0}=(1,1,0)^T$ -- the
same isotropic direction as Step~1's result, by construction.

\emph{Step 3: match the two and cancel.} By
definition~\eqref{eq:pressure-cancellation} and Step~1,
$r=\tfrac12(2\lambda+2\lambda)=2\lambda$: $r$ is defined precisely so
that $r\,(1,1,0)^T$ equals $\Phi_\theta$'s own reference gradient
$(2\lambda,2\lambda,0)^T$ from Step~1. Summing all three terms
of~\eqref{eq:pc-energy} at $\bm\varepsilon=\bm0$ then gives
\begin{equation}
 \bm\sigma(\bm0)=\underbrace{(2\lambda,2\lambda,0)^T}_{\text{from }\Phi_\theta}
 \;-\;r\,\underbrace{(1,1,0)^T}_{\text{from }-r\log J}
 \;+\;\underbrace{\beta(J-1)\nabla J|_{J=1}}_{=\,0\text{, since }J=1}
 =\bm0. \qedhere
\end{equation}
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
The balance condition $\sum_iw_{ki}\bm d_{ki}\otimes \bm d_{ki}=\bm I$ is what makes
this possible with a \emph{single} scalar $r$: without it, the reference
gradient of $\Phi_\theta$ would generally be an anisotropic $3$-vector
(unequal normal components, nonzero shear), and no scalar multiple of the
purely isotropic direction $(1,1,0)^T$ could cancel it exactly. The
construction would then leave a spurious residual stress at the
undeformed configuration -- a real defect, unlike the SPD-tangent
distinction drawn in Section~\ref{sec:c5}, which does not indicate any
defect of the FOM at all.
\end{remark}

\subsection{(C5) Polyconvexity}
\label{sec:c5}

\subsubsection{Definition}
\label{sec:c5-definition}
Polyconvexity (Ball~\cite{Ball1977}) is a condition on the
\emph{functional form} of $W$ as a function of $\bm F$, namely that there
exists a jointly convex function $G$ of three \emph{independent} block
arguments such that
\begin{equation}
 W(\bm F)=G(\bm F,\Cof \bm F,\det \bm F),\qquad G\text{ convex on }\R^{2\times2}
 \times\R^{2\times2}\times\R.
 \label{eq:poly-def}
\end{equation}
This is an existence-theory condition: Ball used it (together with
suitable growth, Section~\ref{sec:c6}) to prove, via the direct method of
the calculus of variations, that minimizers of the total elastic energy
exist. It says nothing by itself about the sign of $\partial\bm\sigma/
\partial\bm\varepsilon$ at a given deformation state. The correct
hierarchy (Ball~\cite{Ball1977}; see also Dacorogna's standard reference
on the calculus of variations) is
\begin{equation}
 \text{convex}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{polyconvex}\ \Longrightarrow\
 \text{quasiconvex}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{rank-one convex},
 \label{eq:hierarchy}
\end{equation}
with each arrow strict in general; Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction}
proves our construction satisfies~\eqref{eq:poly-def} directly, and
Section~\ref{sec:c5-implies} gives the weakest, pointwise-checkable
necessary consequence of it that we verify numerically.

\subsubsection{A strictly stronger alternative used elsewhere, and why it is not adopted here}
\label{sec:c5-wrong}
Polyconvexity~\eqref{eq:poly-def} is not the only architectural route to
a well-posed hyperelastic model, and it is worth being precise about how
it relates to a different, strictly stronger condition used elsewhere in
the literature: a globally symmetric positive definite (SPD) tangent,
$\bm D=\partial\bm\sigma/\partial\bm\varepsilon\succ0$ everywhere. As
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related-asad}, As'ad, Avery and
Farhat~\cite{Asad2022} adopt exactly this stronger condition for their
own anisotropic fabric RVE, for a specific, well-documented reason
(Newton-solver robustness under load, verified in their own coupled
fluid--structure simulations); Gao, Neff, Roventa and
Thiel~\cite{GaoNeffRoventaThiel2017} likewise build a valid
global-minimizer existence theorem directly on convexity of $W$ in $\bm C$,
which the same reparametrization $\bm C=\bm I+2\bm E$ shows is equivalent to this
SPD-tangent condition. Both are legitimate, coherent design choices, not
errors -- but neither is polyconvexity: Ball's existence theorem needs
only~\eqref{eq:poly-def} together with growth, not a globally SPD
tangent, so the two conditions are logically independent, and the
SPD-tangent route remains available only because, unlike plain convexity
of $W$ in $\bm F$ itself, convexity in $\bm C=\bm F^T\bm F$ raises no contradiction with
objectivity (objectivity forces $W$ to be constant along the curved,
non-convex orbit $\{\bm Q\bm F:\bm Q\in\SO\}$, which for $n=2$ includes $\bm Q=-\bm I$ and
would force convexity in $\bm F$ to control $W$ along a segment through the
singular matrix $\tfrac12(\bm F+(-\bm F))=0$; convexity in the already-objective
$\bm C$ has no such obstruction).

For this RVE specifically, however, the SPD-tangent route is not
available: the independent FOM tangent audit of this project measures
$\bm D=\partial\bm S/\partial\bm\varepsilon$ by finite differences at
several deformation states, including a held-out test-trajectory state
where the \emph{smallest eigenvalue of $\bm D$ is $\FomStageTenTangentMinimum$},
i.e.\ negative. The true, homogenized RVE response is therefore not
SPD-tangent everywhere -- a signature of an incremental-stability
transition in the underlying microstructure, not a modelling artifact --
and a surrogate forced to satisfy $\bm D\succ0$ globally would
necessarily \emph{contradict} the FOM at that state. We therefore target
the correct, weaker, and for this RVE actually achievable condition,
polyconvexity, and deliberately do not impose
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial\bm S}{\partial\bm\varepsilon}\succ0 \qquad\text{(not imposed, and not implied by (C5))}.
 \label{eq:not-spd}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The necessary condition we verify: rank-one convexity}
\label{sec:c5-implies}
The weakest property in the hierarchy~\eqref{eq:hierarchy}, rank-one
convexity, equivalent to the Legendre--Hadamard (ellipticity)
condition, is the correct \emph{necessary} consequence of
polyconvexity that can be checked pointwise:
\begin{equation}
 \left.\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\right|_{t=0} W\big(\bm F+t\,\bm a\otimes\bm b\big)\;\geq\;0
 \qquad\text{for all }\bm F\text{ in the domain and all }\bm a,\bm b\in\R^2 .
 \label{eq:rank-one}
\end{equation}
This is a condition on the curvature of $W$ \emph{restricted to
rank-one lines in $\bm F$-space}, which is a small, highly structured subset
of directions, not on the full $3\times3$ Hessian $\bm D$ in strain
space required (and false) in Section~\ref{sec:c5-wrong}. Rank-one
convexity failing is the classical mathematical signature of loss of
ellipticity (shear banding, microstructure buckling): a real, physically
meaningful phenomenon that a good model should be able to reproduce, not
one it should be forced to exclude. \eqref{eq:rank-one} is what the
evaluator in this project samples: $64$ random pairs $(\bm F,\bm a,\bm b)$ with $\det
\bm F>0$, giving a smallest observed value of $\RankOneCurvature$. Being
positive is consistent with (necessary for) polyconvexity; it is a
numerical falsification attempt, not the proof, which is given next.

\subsubsection{The construction, and the proof that it is polyconvex}
\label{sec:c5-construction}
For each $k=1,2,3$, fix three unit structural directions $\bm d_{ki}$ and
positive weights $w_{ki}$ satisfying the balance condition
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{i=1}^3w_{ki}\,\bm d_{ki}\otimes \bm d_{ki}=\bm I,
 \label{eq:balanced-directions}
\end{equation}
with the three direction systems (angles in degrees) and weights recorded
in Table~\ref{tab:direction-systems}, chosen without reference to any
material symmetry group: Section~\ref{sec:direction-selection} gives the
explicit, reproducible procedure that produced them.

\paragraph{How the direction systems are chosen: an explicit,
reproducible procedure.}
\label{sec:direction-selection}
Fixing three angles $\theta_{k1},\theta_{k2},\theta_{k3}\in[0^\circ,180^\circ)$
for a candidate system $k$ determines the unit directions
$\bm d_{ki}=(\cos\theta_{ki},\sin\theta_{ki})$; the balance
condition~\eqref{eq:balanced-directions} is then three scalar equations
for the three unknown weights $w_{k1},w_{k2},w_{k3}$, obtained by
equating each of the three independent entries of the symmetric
$2\times2$ matrix $\sum_iw_{ki}\bm d_{ki}\otimes \bm d_{ki}$ to the
corresponding entry of $\bm I$:
\begin{equation}
 \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
 \cos^2\theta_{k1}&\cos^2\theta_{k2}&\cos^2\theta_{k3}\\
 \sin^2\theta_{k1}&\sin^2\theta_{k2}&\sin^2\theta_{k3}\\
 \sin\theta_{k1}\cos\theta_{k1}&\sin\theta_{k2}\cos\theta_{k2}&\sin\theta_{k3}\cos\theta_{k3}
 \end{pmatrix}}_{=:A(\theta_{k1},\theta_{k2},\theta_{k3})}
 \begin{pmatrix}w_{k1}\\w_{k2}\\w_{k3}\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:weight-system}
\end{equation}
For a generic angle triple, $A$ is invertible and
\eqref{eq:weight-system} has a unique solution $\bm w_k$ from a single
$3\times3$ linear solve -- but that solution is not guaranteed to be
admissible: the construction additionally needs $w_{ki}>0$ (used
explicitly in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:polyconvex}, where
positivity of the weights is what lets a sum of convex terms stay
convex), and nothing in \eqref{eq:weight-system} forbids a negative or
zero entry for a poorly chosen triple. The systems of
Table~\ref{tab:direction-systems} were produced by the direct,
mechanical procedure this makes possible: sample a candidate angle
triple, solve \eqref{eq:weight-system} for $\bm w_k$, and keep the
triple only if $A$ is well-conditioned and every solved weight is
strictly positive; repeat until three sufficiently distinct systems are
found. The three reported systems are well-conditioned in this sense
($\mathrm{cond}(A)\approx1.9$, $2.3$, $2.2$ for systems $1$--$3$
respectively) and their solved weights agree with
Table~\ref{tab:direction-systems} to the reported precision.

Two honest limits of this procedure are stated plainly here rather than
left implicit. First, none of the proofs in this section use any
property of these specific nine angles beyond
\eqref{eq:balanced-directions} and $w_{ki}>0$: Propositions~\ref{prop:isotropic-features}
and~\ref{prop:polyconvex} hold verbatim for \emph{any} three systems
produced by the same accept/reject procedure, so the particular numbers
in Table~\ref{tab:direction-systems} are a convenient, verified choice,
not a load-bearing one. Second, and more fundamentally, this paper does
\emph{not} prove that the resulting map from $\bm C$ to the
$15$-dimensional feature vector~\eqref{eq:features} is injective (that
two different admissible $\bm C$ can never produce identical features),
nor that three direction systems are the minimum or optimal number for
this construction: a single system already supplies an admissible,
polyconvex model, and more than three would remain admissible by the
identical argument. Three systems were adopted because, empirically,
they gave the anisotropic expressivity needed to fit this RVE's own
held-out response (Section~\ref{sec:results}) with a single fixed
feature set, not because of a representational-completeness argument
for a general anisotropic material. This is a reasonable, structured
construction consistent with every proof and every numerical result in
this paper, not a theorem of representational optimality
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to this point).

From these we form
\begin{align}
 I_1&=\tr \bm C=|\bm F|^2,\nonumber\\
 Q_{k,p}^F&=\sum_{i=1}^3 w_{ki}\,(\bm d_{ki}\cdot \bm C\bm d_{ki})^p
       =\sum_{i=1}^3 w_{ki}|\bm F\bm d_{ki}|^{2p},\qquad p=2,3,\nonumber\\
 Q_{k,p}^H&=\sum_{i=1}^3 w_{ki}\,(\bm d_{ki}\cdot\Cof(\bm C)\bm d_{ki})^p
       =\sum_{i=1}^3 w_{ki}|\Cof(\bm F)\bm d_{ki}|^{2p},\qquad p=2,3,
 \label{eq:qfeatures}
\end{align}
using $\bm C:\Cof(A\otimes A)$ identities as in Lemma~\ref{lem:isotropic} to
recognize $\bm d\cdot\Cof(\bm C)\bm d=|\Cof(\bm F)\bm d|^2$. The evaluated feature vector is
\begin{equation}
 \bm z_{\rm pc}=(I_1,\{Q_{k,2}^F,Q_{k,3}^F,Q_{k,2}^H,Q_{k,3}^H\}_{k=1}^3,J,J^2)
 \in\R^{15}.
 \label{eq:features}
\end{equation}
The sixth-power terms are not cosmetic. In two dimensions a balanced
quartic aggregate alone obeys an accidental $90^\circ$ identity: writing
$t_i=\bm d_i\cdot \bm C\bm d_i$, the $90^\circ$-rotated directions have $t_i'=\tr(\bm C)-t_i$
(true for \emph{any} unit vector and its orthogonal complement, by trace
decomposition in an orthonormal basis), and the balance identity gives
$\sum_iw_i(\tr(\bm C)-t_i)^2=\sum_iw_it_i^2$ exactly (expand the square and
use $\sum_iw_it_i=\tr(\bm C)$ and $\sum_iw_i=2$). The cubic aggregate
$\sum_iw_it_i^3$ does not obey this identity in general, so it supplies
genuine anisotropic information while (Proposition~\ref{prop:isotropic-features})
its first derivative at $\bm C=\bm I$ remains isotropic.

\begin{remark}[The fifteen features are not algebraically independent]
\label{rem:redundancy}
The $Q^H$ features are, in fact, exact algebraic functions of $I_1$ and
the $Q^F$ features, so the fifteen entries of $\bm z_{\rm pc}$
in~\eqref{eq:features} are not fifteen independent scalars. This does
not weaken the construction (Propositions~\ref{prop:isotropic-features}
and~\ref{prop:polyconvex} hold regardless), and there is a concrete
architectural reason the redundant features are still worth including
as separate network inputs. Appendix~\ref{app:redundancy} gives the
exact identity and the reason in full.
\end{remark}

Let $\Phi_\theta:\R^{15}\to\R$ be an input-convex neural network (ICNN,
Amos et al.~\cite{Amos2017}) with Softplus activations and \emph{every}
weight matrix -- input-to-hidden at every layer, hidden-to-hidden, and
hidden-to-output -- reparameterized as $\mathrm{softplus}(\cdot)+
\varepsilon_0>0$. Non-negativity at every layer (not just hidden-to-hidden,
as in the original ICNN convexity-only construction) is what additionally
makes $\Phi_\theta$ \emph{coordinate-wise non-decreasing} in $\bm
z_{\rm pc}$, a property used explicitly below. The energy is
\eqref{eq:pc-energy}, repeated here with $r,\beta>0$:
\begin{equation}
 W_{\rm pc}(\bm F)=\Phi_\theta(\bm z_{\rm pc})-\Phi_\theta(\bm z_{\rm pc}(\bm I))
 -r\log J+\frac\beta2(J-1)^2.
 \tag{\ref*{eq:pc-energy}}
\end{equation}

end: the deformation gradient, the 15-dimensional feature map, the
input-convex architecture itself (widths $15$-$64$-$64$-$32$-$1$,
Softplus activations, every weight non-negative, with the
characteristic ICNN skip connections feeding $\bm z_{\rm pc}$ directly
into every layer rather than only the first), the resulting polyconvex
energy, and the stress obtained from it by automatic differentiation.

\begin{proposition}[Polyconvexity of $W_{\rm pc}$]
\label{prop:polyconvex}
$W_{\rm pc}(\bm F)=G(\bm F,\Cof \bm F,J)$ for a function $G$ that is jointly convex
on $\R^{2\times2}\times\R^{2\times2}\times(0,\infty)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For a fixed unit vector $d$, $\bm F\mapsto|\bm F\bm d|^2$ is a convex quadratic form
in $\bm F$ (a composition of the linear map $\bm F\mapsto \bm F\bm d$ with the convex
norm-squared). For $p\in\{2,3\}$, $x\mapsto x^p$ is convex and
non-decreasing on $x\geq0$, and $|\bm F\bm d|^2\geq0$; by the scalar
convex-composition rule ($g$ convex non-decreasing, $f$ convex
$\Rightarrow g\circ f$ convex), $|\bm F\bm d|^{2p}$ is convex in $\bm F$. Summing
with positive weights preserves convexity, so every $Q_{k,p}^F$ is convex
in $\bm F$. The identical argument with $\bm F$ replaced by $\Cof \bm F$ shows every
$Q_{k,p}^H$ is convex in $\Cof \bm F$. $I_1=|\bm F|^2$ is convex in $\bm F$. $J$ and
$J^2$ are (trivially, being affine and quadratic) convex functions of the
third block variable. Each of these $15$ functions, viewed as a function
of the joint variable $(\bm F,\Cof \bm F,J)\in\R^{2\times2}\times\R^{2\times2}
\times\R$, depends on only one block and is convex in that block, hence
is convex in the joint variable (a function of a linear projection of a
convex function is convex). $\Phi_\theta$ is convex in $\bm z_{\rm pc}$
and non-decreasing in each coordinate (weight non-negativity at every
layer, standard ICNN induction: each Softplus pre-activation is an affine,
non-negative-weighted combination of convex non-decreasing quantities of
the input, hence convex and non-decreasing, and Softplus itself is convex
and non-decreasing, so the composition is too, by induction on layers).
By the \emph{vector} convex-composition rule (Boyd \& Vandenberghe,
\S3.2.4: $h$ convex and non-decreasing in each argument, each $g_i$ convex
$\Rightarrow$ $h(g_1,\dots,g_n)$ convex), $\Phi_\theta(\bm z_{\rm pc}(\bm F,
\Cof \bm F,J))$ is convex in $(\bm F,\Cof \bm F,J)$. The additive constant
$-\Phi_\theta(\bm z_{\rm pc}(\bm I))$ does not affect convexity. $-r\log J$
and $\tfrac\beta2(J-1)^2$ are convex functions of $J>0$ for $r,\beta>0$.
A sum of functions each convex in $(\bm F,\Cof \bm F,J)$ is convex in
$(\bm F,\Cof \bm F,J)$. Setting $G$ equal to this sum proves the proposition;
note that this establishes convexity for $(\bm F,\Cof \bm F,J)$ ranging over the
whole product space, i.e.\ genuinely as three \emph{independent} block
arguments, which is exactly \eqref{eq:poly-def}, not merely along the
constrained manifold $\{(\bm F,\Cof \bm F,\det \bm F):\bm F\in\GLp\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
This is a construction proof: polyconvexity is a property of the
functional form \eqref{eq:pc-energy}--\eqref{eq:features}, holding for
\emph{every} value of $\theta$, not a property enforced by a training
penalty that could be violated between checkpoints or degraded by future
retraining with a different loss (Section~\ref{sec:loss}). By
Proposition~\ref{prop:polyconvex} and \eqref{eq:hierarchy}, $W_{\rm pc}$
is automatically also rank-one convex, and this is what the numerical
sample in Section~\ref{sec:c5-implies} attempts to falsify (and fails
to).
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Why $r\geq0$ automatically]
By \eqref{eq:lambda}, $r=2\lambda$ with $\lambda$ a non-negative
combination (positive coefficients $c_m$, non-negative $\partial_{z_m}
\Phi_\theta$) of non-negative terms, so $r\geq0$ for \emph{any} trained
$\theta$, not only the reported one.
\end{proposition}

\subsubsection{A second convex core: input-convex Kolmogorov--Arnold network (ICKAN)}
\label{sec:ickan-construction}
Every proposition above is stated for ``any $\Phi_\theta$ that is convex
and coordinate-wise non-decreasing in $\bm z_{\rm pc}$'' -- none of them
use any property specific to the Softplus ICNN core. This subsection
replaces $\Phi_\theta$ with an input-convex Kolmogorov--Arnold network
(ICKAN), following Thakolkaran et al.~\cite{Thakolkaran2025} and using
their reference implementation
(\url{https://github.com/mmc-group/ICKANs}), while keeping every other
part of the construction -- the balanced direction systems, the
$15$-dimensional feature vector $\bm z_{\rm pc}$, the reference-state
cancellation of Proposition~\ref{prop:cancellation}, and the volumetric
barrier of Section~\ref{sec:c6} -- identical to
Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction}.

A Kolmogorov--Arnold network replaces each scalar weight of a standard
layer with a learned univariate spline. In the reference implementation,
every such spline is reparameterized so that its control points $c_i$
satisfy $c_{i+1}-c_i\geq0$ and $c_{i+2}-2c_{i+1}+c_i\geq0$ (a double
cumulative sum of non-negative increments): the first condition makes
the spline non-decreasing, the second makes it convex, for \emph{any}
value of the underlying trainable parameters. Disabling the
(non-convex) residual base function and freezing the inter-layer affine
maps to the identity -- exactly the configuration used by Thakolkaran
et al.\ and reused here -- means every edge of the network is
simultaneously convex and non-decreasing in its scalar input, and a sum
of such edges is again convex and non-decreasing by the same rule used
for the ICNN. We verified this numerically before training: for a
randomly initialized ICKAN core, increasing one input strictly
increases the output, and the output is convex along an arbitrary line
through feature space (see the reproducibility scripts). Consequently,
Propositions~\ref{prop:cancellation}--\ref{prop:polyconvex} apply to
this model without modification, confirmed directly by measurement in
Section~\ref{sec:ickan}, where the reference stress norm for the
trained ICKAN model matches the ICNN model's at machine precision.

\subsection{(C6) Growth condition}
\label{sec:c6}
Ball's existence theorem pairs polyconvexity with a coercivity/growth
condition on $G$, schematically
\begin{equation}
 G(\bm F,H,\delta)\geq c_1|\bm F|^p+c_2|H|^q+h(\delta),\qquad p,q\geq2,
\end{equation}
with $h$ convex, $h(\delta)\to\infty$ as $\delta\to0^+$ and superlinearly
as $\delta\to\infty$, needed so that minimizing sequences of the total
energy do not escape to infinity and a minimizer exists. Our construction
supplies this structure explicitly:
\begin{itemize}
\item The sixth-power terms $Q_{k,3}^F,Q_{k,3}^H\sim|\bm F\bm d|^6,|\Cof(\bm F)d|^6$
give super-quadratic polynomial growth in $\bm F$ and $\Cof \bm F$ as any
structural stretch diverges; Proposition~\ref{prop:sixth-power-bound}
below makes this precise with an explicit, direction-independent lower
bound, rather than a qualitative appeal to direction coverage.
\item The volumetric term $h(J)=-r\log J+\tfrac\beta2(J-1)^2$ diverges as
$J\to0^+$ (impenetrability of matter: infinite energy to crush the RVE to
zero volume) and grows quadratically as $J\to\infty$, exactly the
qualitative shape used in classical polyconvex volumetric penalties
(e.g.\ Ciarlet--Geymonat-type terms), confirmed numerically in
Figure~\ref{fig:volumetric} for the trained model.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proposition}[A direction-independent lower bound on the sixth-power terms]
\label{prop:sixth-power-bound}
For any single balanced system ($\sum_iw_i\,\bm d_i\otimes \bm d_i=\bm I$, $w_i>0$)
and any $\bm F$,
\begin{equation}
 Q_{k,3}^F=\sum_{i=1}^3w_i\,(\bm d_i\cdot \bm C\bm d_i)^3\;\geq\;\tfrac14\,|\bm F|^6,
 \qquad
 Q_{k,3}^H\;\geq\;\tfrac14\,|\bm F|^6,
 \label{eq:sixth-power-bound}
\end{equation}
where $|\bm F|^2=\tr \bm C$ is the Frobenius norm. In particular
$Q_{k,3}^F,Q_{k,3}^H\to\infty$ as $|\bm F|\to\infty$ along \emph{every}
direction, not merely the three structural ones -- with no appeal
needed to how many orientations the balanced system happens to cover.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $t_i=\bm d_i\cdot \bm C\bm d_i\geq0$. Two consequences of the balance
condition, both already used in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:isotropic-features}, are all this needs:
$\sum_iw_i=2$, and $\sum_iw_it_i=\tr(\bm C)=|\bm F|^2$ (contract the balance
identity with $\bm C$: $\sum_iw_i\,\bm d_i\cdot \bm C\bm d_i=\bm C:\sum_iw_id_i\otimes
\bm d_i=\bm C:\bm I=\tr \bm C$). Since $x\mapsto x^3$ is convex on $x\geq0$, Jensen's
inequality applied with weights $w_i/2$ (which sum to $1$) gives
\begin{equation}
 \Big(\sum_i\frac{w_i}2\,t_i\Big)^{\!3}\;\leq\;\sum_i\frac{w_i}2\,t_i^3,
 \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
 \Big(\frac{|\bm F|^2}2\Big)^{\!3}\leq\frac12\,Q_{k,3}^F,
\end{equation}
which rearranges to $Q_{k,3}^F\geq\tfrac14|\bm F|^6$. The identical
argument with $\bm C$ replaced by $\Cof(\bm C)$ gives $Q_{k,3}^H\geq\tfrac14
|\Cof \bm F|^6$; in two dimensions $|\Cof \bm F|=|\bm F|$ exactly (the cofactor of
a $2\times2$ matrix permutes and sign-flips entries without changing
the sum of their squares), so $Q_{k,3}^H\geq\tfrac14|\bm F|^6$ as well.
\end{proof}

We do not claim to have verified the precise Sobolev-exponent matching
that a fully rigorous existence proof for the associated boundary-value
problem would require; that is a mathematical refinement beyond the scope
of this paper. What Proposition~\ref{prop:sixth-power-bound} and the
volumetric barrier above establish, together, is that the
\emph{qualitative} coercivity structure Ball-type theorems require is
present by construction and provably direction-independent, not added
as an afterthought penalty or asserted by appeal to intuition.

\section{Comparison models: projection-based and hyper-reduced surrogates}
\label{sec:rom-comparison}
This section fixes notation for the PROM and the hyper-reduced-order
model (HPROM) used in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} as an independent, non-ANN-constitutive-law
comparison point for the PANN of Section~\ref{sec:theory}. None of the
constructions in this section are a contribution of this work; they are
established tools, cited to their sources, applied here to the RVE and
FOM of Section~\ref{sec:background-fom}.

\subsection{Projection-based reduced-order model (PROM)}
\label{sec:background-prom}
A standard linear PROM replaces $\bm u_f$ by its projection onto a low-dimensional
subspace. Snapshots $\bm u_f(\bm\varepsilon_1),\dots,\bm u_f(\bm\varepsilon_{n_{\rm s}})$ from FOM
solves along the training trajectories
(Fig.~\ref{fig:training-trajectories}) are assembled into a snapshot matrix
$\bm D\in\mathbb R^{n_L\times n_{\rm s}}$, and a thin, truncated singular
value decomposition gives an orthonormal reduced-order basis (ROB)
$\mathbf V_{\rm tot}\in\mathbb R^{n_L\times n_{\rm tot}}$ spanning the
dominant left singular subspace of $\bm D$. The displacement field is then
approximated by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prom-affine}
 \bm u_f\approx \bm u_f^{\rm aff}(\bm\varepsilon)+\mathbf V_{\rm tot}\,\bm q_{\rm tot}(\bm\varepsilon),
\end{equation}
where $\bm u_f^{\rm aff}(\bm\varepsilon)$ is the affine baseline consistent with the
prescribed macroscopic Dirichlet data $\bm u_d(\bm\varepsilon)$ (so that
$\mathbf V_{\rm tot}\bm
q_{\rm tot}$ carries only the correction beyond that affine guess) and
$\bm q_{\rm tot}\in\mathbb R^{n_{\rm tot}}$ are the generalized (POD)
coordinates.

Substituting~\eqref{eq:prom-affine} into~\eqref{eq:fom-residual}
and testing with the same basis (Galerkin projection -- the natural choice
here since the tangent operator of a hyperelastic problem is symmetric,
unlike the non-self-adjoint operators that motivate a least-squares
Petrov--Galerkin, LSPG, test space in the compressible-flow PROM
literature~\cite{Barnett2023,AresDeParga2026}) gives the reduced equilibrium
equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prom-residual}
 \mathbf V_{\rm tot}^T\,\bm r\big(\bm u_f^{\rm aff}(\bm\varepsilon)+\mathbf V_{\rm tot}\bm q_{\rm tot};\bm\varepsilon\big)=\bm 0,
\end{equation}
a system of $n_{\rm tot}\ll n_L$ nonlinear equations, again solved by
Newton--Raphson. As documented for this exact class of problem
(propagating features, evolving nonlinear regions) by
Barnett and Farhat~\cite{Barnett2022} and by Hern\'andez's own metamaterial and
damage examples~\cite{HernandezMAWECM}, a single linear subspace
of dimension small enough to be worth reducing to is generally \emph{not}
enough to reach high accuracy for a nonlinear problem (the Kolmogorov
$n$-width barrier): the number of retained modes needed for good accuracy
can remain uncomfortably large. The nonlinear-manifold construction used by
HPROM--ANN in this paper addresses exactly this point.

\subsection{Nonlinear-manifold correction: the HPROM--ANN decoder}
\label{sec:background-decoder}
Following the general nonlinear-PMOR strategy of decomposing the ROB into a
small \emph{primary} (retained) part and a larger \emph{secondary}
(discarded) part whose amplitude is reconstructed by a trained nonlinear map,
introduced for fluid-dynamics PMOR as the PROM-ANN/HPROM-ANN framework
by Barnett, Farhat and Maday~\cite{Barnett2023} and generalized to
interpretable Gaussian-process and radial-basis-function closures by Ares De
Parga et al.~\cite{AresDeParga2026}. The total basis is partitioned as
$\mathbf V_{\rm tot}=[\mathbf V\ \bar{\mathbf V}]$, $\mathbf V\in\mathbb
R^{n_L\times n}$, $\bar{\mathbf V}\in\mathbb R^{n_L\times\bar n}$,
$n\ll\bar n$, and the displacement field is approximated as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:decoder}
 \bm u_f\approx \bm u_f^{\rm aff}(\bm\varepsilon)+\mathbf V\,\bm q+\bar{\mathbf V}\,\mathcal N(\bm q),
\end{equation}
with $\bm q\in\mathbb R^n$ the primary (retained) generalized coordinates
and $\mathcal N:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^{\bar n}$ a trained neural network
that reconstructs the secondary (discarded) amplitudes ($n=3$, $\bar
n=36$ for the trained model used throughout this paper).

\paragraph{Why the primary coordinates are not simply the first three POD coordinates.}
The raw POD coordinates $\mathbf Q_{\rm tot}=\mathbf V_{\rm tot}^T\mathbf S$
of a snapshot matrix $\mathbf S$ are optimal, by construction, for
linear reconstruction accuracy: no other rank-$n$ subspace captures more
snapshot energy. They are not, however, optimized for what the decoder
in~\eqref{eq:decoder} actually needs downstream: a coordinate that a
regression map $\bm\varepsilon\mapsto\bm q$ can learn accurately.
Figure~\ref{fig:pod-coordinates} shows why this matters concretely for
this RVE: the first three raw POD coordinates of the training
trajectories (Fig.~\ref{fig:training-trajectories}) trace out a
curved, self-intersecting ribbon in $\mathbb R^3$ (left panel) that
bears little resemblance to the simple, boustrophedon sweep of the
macro-strain domain itself (right panel): a nonlinear, non-monotonic
relationship that is unnecessarily hard for a regression network to
learn well. We therefore do not use $\mathbf Q_{\rm tot}$ directly;
instead, $\mathbf V$ and $\bm q$ are identified via the same
least-squares primary/secondary basis-identification procedure used by
Hern\'andez's MAW--ECM paper~\cite{HernandezMAWECM} for a different
homogenization RVE: a linear transformation of the raw POD coordinates,
fitted so that the resulting \emph{structured} coordinates track the
macro-strain path as closely as possible in a least-squares sense,
while remaining an exact orthonormal change of basis within the
primary subspace (so~\eqref{eq:decoder}'s reconstruction accuracy is
unaffected). The middle panel of Figure~\ref{fig:pod-coordinates} shows
the effect on this RVE's own data: the same ten training trajectories,
expressed in the structured coordinates, recover the simple,
near-affine sweep pattern of the macro-strain domain itself, at a
verified affine-fit accuracy of $6.9\times10^{-4}$ relative
$L^2$ error ($R^2\geq0.99999$ componentwise) and a
primary/secondary orthogonality residual of
$2.3\times10^{-15}$ (machine precision). This is exactly
what makes the direct macro-strain-to-coordinate regression
in~\eqref{eq:decoder} tractable: $\mathcal N$ only has to learn the
much smaller residual nonlinearity between this already near-affine
structured coordinate and the discarded secondary amplitudes, not the
raw POD manifold's own curvature.

In this project, $\mathbf V$ and $\bm q$ are identified with the
macro-strain $\bm\varepsilon$ itself, an \emph{input-driven} manifold, valid here
because the problem is static and hyperelastic (no history dependence), so
the equilibrium state is uniquely parameterized by $\bm\varepsilon$. Consequently
$\mathcal N$ is trained once, offline, on the training trajectories to map
macro-strain directly to the reconstructed secondary coordinates, and no
reduced equilibrium equation of the form~\eqref{eq:prom-residual} needs to
be solved online at all if $\mathcal N$'s prediction is trusted directly;
alternatively, \eqref{eq:decoder} can still be substituted into a
Galerkin-projected residual and corrected by Newton iterations, exactly as
in~\eqref{eq:prom-residual}. These are precisely the two HPROM--ANN
operating modes evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:results}: the
\emph{iterative} mode (Newton correction to convergence) and the
\emph{direct} mode, D-HPROM--ANN (zero Newton iterations, $\mathcal N$'s
output used as-is).

\subsection{Hyperreduction: the empirical cubature method and MAW--ECM}
\label{sec:background-ecm}
Even after~\eqref{eq:decoder}, evaluating the residual~\eqref{eq:fom-residual}
(or the reduced residual~\eqref{eq:prom-residual}) still requires a full
loop over all $n_{\rm gp}=\sum_e n_{\rm gp}^{(e)}$ Gauss points of the
original FOM mesh, since the stress $\bm S_{e,g}$ must be evaluated from
the true microscopic constitutive law at every one of them. Reindexing the
element/Gauss-point pairs $(e,g)$ of~\eqref{eq:fom-residual} by a single
global index $g\in\{1,\dots,n_{\rm gp}\}$ (as is standard in the ECM
literature), so that $\bm r_g:=\bm L_e^T\bm B_{e,g}^T\bm S_{e,g}$ and
$\bm S_g:=\bm S_{e,g}$ denote the residual and stress contribution of
global Gauss point $g$, hyperreduction replaces the full Gauss-point sum
by a sparse cubature rule over a small subset $\mathcal
Z\subset\{1,\dots,n_{\rm gp}\}$ of retained points with positive weights
$\{\omega_g\}_{g\in\mathcal Z}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ecm-residual}
 \sum_{g\in\mathcal Z}\big(\mathbf V^T\bm r_g\big)(\bm u_f;\bm\varepsilon)\,\omega_g=\bm 0,
 \qquad
 \bm S_{\rm macro}(\bm\varepsilon)\approx\frac{1}{|\Omega_0|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal
 Z_\sigma}\bm S_g(\bm u_f;\bm\varepsilon)\,\omega_{\sigma,g},
\end{equation}
one rule ($\mathcal Z$, weights $\omega_g$) tailored to integrate the
projected \emph{equilibrium} residual accurately, and, in general, a second,
independently constructed rule ($\mathcal Z_\sigma$, weights
$\omega_{\sigma,g}$) tailored to the homogenized-\emph{stress} output
functional -- the two point sets and weight sets are not required to
coincide, and Fig.~\ref{fig:ecm-supports} shows both retained element sets
for this RVE side by side.

The base weights, for a single fixed macro-strain state, are found
offline by the ECM of Hern\'andez, Caicedo
and Ferrer~\cite{Hernandez2017}, using the greedy active-set
sparse non-negative least-squares algorithm of Hern\'andez's own later
formulation~\cite{Hernandez2020}: starting from an empty point set, the
element whose residual-basis column best explains the current
integration-error vector is added at each step, the weights on the
active set are recomputed by a constrained least-squares solve, and any
element whose weight has gone negative is purged and re-solved, until
the truncated-SVD basis of the training-snapshot integrand space is
integrated to within a prescribed tolerance. This is in the same spirit
as (and predates) the energy-conserving-sampling-and-weighting (ECSW)
family used for Petrov--Galerkin hyperreduction in the fluids PMOR
literature~\cite{Farhat2014,Farhat2015,Grimberg2021} and the empirical
quadrature procedure~\cite{An2008,PateraYano2017,Yano2019}. A model
that uses a single such \emph{fixed} weight set for every macro-strain
state is an ECM-hyperreduced HPROM.

The manifold-adaptive-weight idea behind MAW--ECM, introduced by
Hern\'andez~\cite{HernandezMAWECM}, instead lets $\omega_g$ (and
$\omega_{\sigma,g}$) vary smoothly with the reduced state, here the
macro-strain, so that the same support set $\mathcal Z$ stays accurate
over a wider region of the strain path than a fixed rule could.
Hern\'andez's own construction achieves this smoothness through a
graph-Laplacian-regularized pruning-and-regression scheme over a
discretized manifold of candidate states, and the construction deployed
for this RVE follows the same two-stage idea -- in the same spirit,
though not a line-for-line reproduction of the published implementation.

\emph{Stage one: graph-regularized support pruning.} Starting from a
larger candidate support already produced by the fixed-weight ECM
procedure above (one candidate weight vector per training state), a
two-phase backward-elimination procedure prunes this candidate set down
to the final, fixed support $\mathcal Z$ (or $\mathcal Z_\sigma$)
reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:ecm-supports}. In phase one, candidates are
removed one at a time, independently at each training state, whenever
the remaining active set can still satisfy that state's linear
moment-matching constraint by a least-squares reweighting with no
weight going negative, until an intermediate support size of $40$
points is reached. In phase two, elimination continues jointly across
states: a $k$-nearest-neighbor graph ($k=8$) is built over a uniform
subsample of $500$ training states (over the reduced coordinate $\bm
q$ for the equilibrium-residual rule, or over the macro-strain
$\bm\varepsilon$ itself for the two homogenized-stress rules), and, at
each step, every remaining low-activity candidate is trial-removed by
solving a constrained quadratic program that keeps every sampled
state's weight vector close to its previous value while penalizing the
graph-Laplacian roughness of the weight field across neighboring states
(smoothing weight $\alpha=10^2$); the removal that leaves the smoothest
resulting weight field is kept, subject to the same non-negativity and
moment-matching constraints as phase one, until the final target size
of $10$ points is reached. This graph-regularized second phase is what
actually supplies the manifold-adaptive smoothness Hern\'andez's
construction is designed to give: it is a genuine, non-trivial
regularization step acting jointly across training states, not a
relabeling of independent per-state ECM solves.

\emph{Stage two: continuous weight-field regression.} Phase two's
output is the final fixed support together with a table of
graph-smoothed weight vectors, one per sampled training state -- still
a discrete object that must be evaluated at arbitrary, unseen states
online. This table is fit by one of two regressors depending on the
rule: a Gaussian radial-basis-function model with a polynomial trend
for the equilibrium-residual rule, and a softmax-output neural network
for the two homogenized-stress rules (non-negativity and the fixed row
sum $\sum_g\omega_g$ guaranteed by the softmax itself), trained with a
loss combining a Kullback--Leibler term and a mean-squared-error term
against the pruned weight table, plus a physics-exactness penalty that
enforces the linear integration constraint over the \emph{full}
training set, not only the $500$-state subsample used for pruning. We
state this precisely here, rather than either describing our own
implementation as an unmodified reproduction of the published
algorithm or, as an earlier version of this section incorrectly did,
describing it as skipping the graph-regularization step entirely. The
offline scripts that produced the specific support and weights deployed
for this RVE predate, and are not part of, this project's own
repository reorganization; the trained weights themselves are, and are
exactly what every HPROM--ANN number in this paper is computed from.

\paragraph{Broader provenance.} Related work on reduced-order modeling

homogenization~\cite{Hernandez2014,Hernandez2020,Miehe1999} traces back

\section{Numerical experiments and results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Loss design: an equivalent-stress-oriented training choice}
\label{sec:loss}
Sections~\ref{sec:c1}--\ref{sec:c6} are properties of the energy
functional \eqref{eq:pc-energy} and hold regardless of how the model is
trained. This section is different in kind: a purely statistical choice
about the training loss, made because it measurably improves accuracy on
the quantity of engineering interest, and explicitly does not touch
\eqref{eq:pc-energy}.

The final loss, used to train every reported model, is the plain
two-term sum actually used throughout this paper:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L=\EnergyWeight\,\mathcal L_W+\StressWeight\,\mathcal L_{S,\rm agg},
 \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal L_W$ normalizes the mean squared energy error by its mean
squared target, and $\mathcal L_{S,\rm agg}$ normalizes the mean squared
error of the full stress vector $(S_{11},S_{22},S_{12})$ by the mean
squared norm of the full target vector (so it is dominated by whichever
components carry the most physical stress magnitude). In both terms the
predicted stress is $\partial W_{\rm pc}/\partial\bm\varepsilon$ of the
predicted energy, as in \eqref{eq:stress}.

\paragraph{An alternative that was tried and is deliberately not used: a componentwise-weighted blend.}
In this RVE, $S_{12}$ is roughly two orders of magnitude smaller than
$S_{11}$, $S_{22}$ along both the training trajectories and the
held-out test trajectory alike. A natural alternative to
$\mathcal L_{S,\rm agg}$ alone, used by the previously certified
version of this model and, in its most extreme form, by As'ad et
al.~\cite{Asad2022} -- blends in a second, \emph{componentwise} stress
term,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_S(\gamma)=(1-\gamma)\,\mathcal L_{S,\rm agg}+\gamma\,\mathcal L_{S,\rm comp},
 \qquad \gamma\in[0,1],
 \label{eq:loss-blend}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal L_{S,\rm comp}$ is the equally weighted mean of the
three \emph{componentwise} relative-squared errors (each of $S_{11}$,
$S_{22}$, $S_{12}$ normalized by its own mean squared target before
averaging, rather than by the norm of the full vector, as
$\mathcal L_{S,\rm agg}$ is). At $\gamma=\ComponentStressWeightOld{}$,
the value used by the previously certified version of this model, the
optimizer is forced to reduce $S_{12}$'s \emph{relative} error to the
same standard as $S_{11}$, $S_{22}$, even though $S_{12}$ contributes
negligibly to the plane-stress equivalent (von Mises) stress
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_{\rm eq}=\sqrt{S_{11}^2-S_{11}S_{22}+S_{22}^2+3S_{12}^2},
 \label{eq:von-mises}
\end{equation}
the quantity of engineering interest here.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates this choice from the other change
made at the same time (widening the ICNN): widening alone, with
$\gamma=\ComponentStressWeightOld{}$ unchanged, gives a small
improvement over the original network, while setting $\gamma=0$ --
dropping $\mathcal L_{S,\rm comp}$ entirely, which is exactly
\eqref{eq:loss} above -- produces essentially all of the remaining
gain, including, counter-intuitively, on $S_{12}$'s \emph{own}
componentwise error, which improves rather than degrades. This is
consistent with the componentwise term having forced the optimizer to
chase a noisy, two-orders-of-magnitude-smaller relative target at the
expense of the rest of the fit, rather than with any genuine tension
between the components. Because $\gamma=0$ is what
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows to work best, \eqref{eq:loss-blend} is
recorded here only as the rejected alternative that motivates
\eqref{eq:loss}; it is not part of the loss used anywhere else in this
paper.

\subsection{Training protocol}
\label{sec:training}
The model uses all $109\,460$ saved states of the ten training
trajectories, an ICNN of widths $(\IcnnWidths{})$, and the loss weights
$(\EnergyWeight{},\StressWeight{})$ of \eqref{eq:loss} (i.e.\ $\gamma=0$
in the rejected componentwise blend~\eqref{eq:loss-blend} of
Section~\ref{sec:loss}). It is trained in CPU float64 because its energy--stress
loss differentiates the energy twice; its best epoch by the tracked
training score is $\BestEpoch{}$. The held-out test trajectory is not
used, in any form, during training.

\subsection{Held-out test-trajectory assessment: all six model variants}
\label{sec:results}
Table~\ref{tab:master} reports relative $L^2$ errors against the direct
FOM labels, including the plane-stress equivalent (von Mises) stress
\eqref{eq:von-mises}, for all six model variants considered in this
paper: the four ANNs of Section~\ref{sec:c1-regression} (tiers
1--3b), and the two hyper-reduced projection-based ROMs used elsewhere in
this project as an independent, non-ANN-constitutive-law point of
comparison on the identical held-out test path. HPROM--ANN and D-HPROM--ANN
are not two different models but two operating modes of the same
hyper-reduced solver and the same trained solution-manifold ANN
(mapping macro-strain directly to reduced POD coordinates via the
primary/secondary decoder of Section~\ref{sec:background-decoder}, unrelated to
the constitutive-law ANNs of tiers 1--3b): HPROM--ANN runs the full
ECM-weighted Newton corrector to convergence at every step (up to $25$
iterations), while D-HPROM--ANN sets the corrector iteration count to zero
and simply evaluates the manifold ANN's direct macro-strain-to-reduced-state
prediction, with no equilibrium solve at all. Both reconstruct stress from
the true microscale Neo-Hookean law at ECM-selected Gauss points, not from
a learned constitutive surrogate, so they measure a different source of
error (hyper-reduction and manifold-regression error) than tiers 1--3b
(constitutive-surrogate error).

A further complication is that HPROM--ANN's native output is a naive
volume-average of the converged microscopic strain/stress field, not the
exactly energy-conjugate macro-stress that tiers 1--3b are trained and
evaluated against (the same regression-vs.-conjugate distinction of
Section~\ref{sec:c1-regression}, one level up the pipeline). For a mixed
Dirichlet/free boundary-value problem these two macroscopic stress
definitions are not required to agree, and empirically do not (a direct
check found the two differing by roughly $30\%$ on this RVE). Comparing
HPROM--ANN's raw output against the other five rows below would therefore
not be comparing like with like. Instead, we compute the same
energy-conjugate quantity for HPROM--ANN's own converged state: by the
envelope theorem at an equilibrium point, it reduces to a
reaction-force-weighted sum over the Dirichlet-constrained boundary nodes
(no re-solving or finite differences needed). We verified this
reaction-force procedure reproduces the training data's own
energy-conjugate stress to a relative $L^2$ error of $\HpromControlFomStressError$
(effectively machine precision) on a held-out subset of the training
trajectories before applying it to the held-out test-trajectory
HPROM--ANN/D-HPROM--ANN states reported
below.

not a contribution of this paper; Section~\ref{sec:rom-comparison}
already fixes its notation, cites its sources in full, and places it
against the broader reduced-order-modeling literature.

\subsection{A second convex core: ICKAN results}
\label{sec:ickan}
Section~\ref{sec:ickan-construction} shows that the same construction
of Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction}, namely the balanced direction systems,
the $15$-dimensional feature vector, the reference-state cancellation,
and the volumetric barrier, carries over unchanged to the ICKAN core
following
Thakolkaran et al.~\cite{Thakolkaran2025}, and why the same
propositions apply to it without modification; Section~\ref{sec:related-cann}
places this relative to the ICKAN literature. This is confirmed
directly by measurement here: the reference stress norm for the trained
ICKAN model is $\IckanReferenceStressNorm$, at machine precision,
exactly as for the ICNN model.

\paragraph{Architecture and results.} The ICKAN core used here has hidden
widths $(\IckanHiddenWidths{})$, a grid of $\IckanGrid{}$ intervals, and
spline order $\IckanSplineOrder{}$; it is trained with the same loss
\eqref{eq:loss} as the ICNN model, reaching its best tracked training score at epoch
$\IckanBestEpoch{}$. The ICNN and ICKAN rows of Table~\ref{tab:master} and
Figure~\ref{fig:full-comparison} report its held-out test-trajectory accuracy alongside
the ICNN model. The smallest sampled rank-one curvature is
$\IckanRankOneCurvature$ and the broad sampled-energy audit gives a
minimum of $\IckanSampledMinimumEnergy$: both positive, consistent with
(not proof of) the polyconvexity certificate, exactly as for the ICNN
model.

The two cores are close, with neither uniformly better; the ICKAN reaches
comparable accuracy with a hidden-layer parameter count roughly two
orders of magnitude smaller than the ICNN's $(\IcnnWidths{})$, at the
cost of a slower per-epoch cost from the spline evaluations. Both
certificates are architectural and independent of which core is used;
choosing between them in practice is a question of parameter budget and
training cost, not of admissibility.

\subsection{What breaks without the certificate: a non-polyconvex baseline}
\label{sec:non-polyconvex}
Section~\ref{sec:related-asad} discussed why As'ad et al.~\cite{Asad2022}
and Klein et al.~\cite{Klein2022} each include a flexible, uncertified
baseline in their own work, specifically to show what the certificate is
protecting against: As'ad et al.\ show Newton's method diverging under
load; Klein et al.\ show their flexible model losing ellipticity even for
small deformations (their Fig.~5). This section repeats that exercise on
this project's own RVE, using the flexible ``free'' anisotropic PANN
already introduced for the original, three-model version of this study --
an unconstrained Softplus MLP on the material components of $\bm C$, with no
convexity constraint anywhere in the architecture -- retrained with the
identical loss \eqref{eq:loss} used for the two certified models above,
so the comparison isolates the convexity architecture as the only
difference.

\paragraph{Accuracy alone does not reveal the defect.} Trained this way,
the free model reaches $\FreeMatchedEnergyError$ energy error and
$\FreeMatchedVonMisesError{}$ von Mises error on the held-out test trajectory -- comparable
to, and on this metric marginally \emph{better} than, the certified
polyconvex models of Table~\ref{tab:master} (its $S_{12}$ error,
$\FreeMatchedSxyError$, is much worse, consistent with
Section~\ref{sec:loss}). Figure~\ref{fig:full-comparison} shows its von
Mises trajectory is visually indistinguishable from the certified models
and from the direct FOM. This is the central point of this section: held-out
accuracy is silent about the absence of a polyconvexity certificate. The
same observation was made about Abdolazizi et al.'s uncertified
CKAN~\cite{Abdolazizi2025} in Section~\ref{sec:related-cann} on the basis
of their reported results; here it is demonstrated directly on our own
model and our own data.

\paragraph{The certificate's absence is real, and findable.} We sampled
$\FreeRankOneSamples$ random rank-one perturbations $\bm F+t\,\bm a\otimes\bm b$ (unit
$\bm a,\bm b$, log-stretch range $\pm1$, i.e.\ stretches in $[e^{-1},e^{1}]$) and
evaluated $d^2/dt^2\,W$ at $t=0$ in float64, the same falsification
attempt of \eqref{eq:rank-one} run on the certified models in
Section~\ref{sec:c5-implies}, at a larger sample count. For the certified
ICNN and ICKAN models every sample was non-negative (Table~\ref{tab:master}'s
discussion and Section~\ref{sec:results}); for the free model,
$\FreeRankOneViolations$ of $\FreeRankOneSamples$ samples
($\FreeRankOneViolationFraction$) are negative, with a minimum of
$\FreeRankOneMinimumCurvature$, comparable in magnitude to the
certified models' minimum \emph{positive} sampled curvature. A specific
example violation is
\begin{equation}
 \bm F=\begin{bmatrix}0.534&-0.372\\-0.387&0.728\end{bmatrix},\quad
 \bm a=\begin{bmatrix}0.939\\-0.345\end{bmatrix},\quad
 \bm b=\begin{bmatrix}-0.064\\-0.998\end{bmatrix},\quad
 \frac{d^2W}{dt^2}\bigg|_{t=0}<0,
 \label{eq:free-violation}
\end{equation}
a moderate, unremarkable compressive-shear state ($\det \bm F\approx0.24$),
not an extreme or contrived one. This is exactly what
Section~\ref{sec:c5-implies} argues: rank-one convexity is the correct
necessary consequence of polyconvexity, and without an architectural
guarantee it can fail at ordinary deformation states while every accuracy
metric looks fine. The certified models in this paper do not merely
happen to avoid this on the states sampled; Proposition~\ref{prop:polyconvex}
proves they cannot fail it anywhere, by construction.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}
Four scope limits of the present study are stated here explicitly,
rather than left implicit, because each bears directly on how far the
claims above extend.

\paragraph{The empirical demonstration uses a $D_4$-symmetric RVE.} The
construction of Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction} assumes no material
symmetry group, and the balanced direction systems are not tied to one
(Section~\ref{sec:direction-selection}); this is a property of the
\emph{construction}. The specific RVE used to validate it, however -- a
square unit cell with a centered circular hole, Fig.~\ref{fig:rve-geometry}
-- does have $D_4$ symmetry, so the held-out accuracy of
Section~\ref{sec:results} does not by itself demonstrate the
construction's behavior on a genuinely asymmetric microstructure.
Repeating the same protocol on an RVE with no symmetry at all (an
off-center or elliptical hole, or a random inclusion pattern) would
close this gap directly.

\paragraph{Injectivity of the feature map and optimality of three
direction systems are open.} As stated in
Section~\ref{sec:direction-selection}, this paper does not prove that
two distinct admissible $\bm C$ always produce distinct feature
vectors~\eqref{eq:features}, nor that three direction systems are the
minimum or optimal number. The construction is verified sufficient for
this RVE's own held-out response, not shown to be representationally
optimal or complete for a general anisotropic material.

\paragraph{No downstream structural or multiscale demonstration.} As
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related-gap}, this paper does not embed
the certified PANN in an actual finite-element structural or
FE$^2$ multiscale solve to check whether its polyconvexity certificate
translates into the Newton-robustness benefit As'ad et
al.~\cite{Asad2022} demonstrate for their strictly stronger, $\bm E$-convex
construction. This is the natural next step toward making the
certificate's practical value defensible end-to-end, not only at the
level of the standalone constitutive surrogate.

\paragraph{Missing ablations and cost accounting.} This paper does not
report sensitivity to the number of direction systems (one or two
instead of three), to the specific angles within a system, or to the
random seed used for training; nor does it report the online evaluation
cost of the certified PANN relative to a direct microscale solve or to
HPROM--ANN, which would be needed to argue for the certified
construction on efficiency grounds rather than admissibility grounds
alone. The held-out comparison of Section~\ref{sec:results} also uses a
single test trajectory; a broader set of held-out paths, including
states well outside the convex hull of the training trajectories, would
give a fuller picture of extrapolation behavior.

None of these is a defect in the results already reported: every
admissibility proof in Sections~\ref{sec:c1}--\ref{sec:c6} holds
unconditionally, independent of which RVE, which direction systems, or
which downstream solver is used. They are open extensions of this work,
stated here rather than left for a reader to discover.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
Every item of the admissibility checklist in Section~\ref{sec:checklist}
is addressed with a closed-form argument, not a numerical penalty: (C1)
holds for any energy-derivative stress definition; (C2) holds because the
model is built from $\bm C$ and $\Cof(\bm C)$; (C3)'s actual content (tensor
symmetry) holds by parameterizing only the three independent stress
components, while material symmetry ($D_4$) is knowingly not imposed;
(C4) holds exactly by an additive-constant argument for the energy and by
the balanced-direction cancellation of Proposition~\ref{prop:cancellation}
for the stress; (C5), correctly stated as Ball's
\eqref{eq:poly-def} rather than the stronger and, for this RVE,
\emph{false} SPD-tangent shorthand, is proved by the explicit convex
construction of Proposition~\ref{prop:polyconvex}, with the correct
weaker (rank-one) necessary condition verified numerically; and (C6) is
supplied by the sixth-power directional terms and the logarithmic/quadratic
volumetric barrier. None of (C1)--(C6) depends on the loss weighting of
Section~\ref{sec:loss}, which is an orthogonal, purely statistical choice
that improved every reported held-out test-trajectory metric, including $S_{12}$'s own
componentwise error, contrary to the naive expectation that removing its
dedicated weighting should trade it away for a better fit of $S_{11}$,
$S_{22}$. Any future retraining that changes the relative magnitude of
$S_{12}$ against $S_{11}$, $S_{22}$ (e.g.\ a different RVE geometry or
load protocol) should revisit the component-stress weight rather than
assume it transfers unchanged.

The construction is also not tied to one convex architecture:
Section~\ref{sec:ickan} shows the identical proofs, and an equally clean
reference-stress cancellation at machine precision, carry over to an
input-convex Kolmogorov--Arnold core, at comparable held-out test-trajectory accuracy to
the ICNN core with far fewer parameters.

The remaining two sections make the case for a certificate, rather than
an accuracy check, from two independent directions, using the four-tier
ablation of Section~\ref{sec:c1-regression} (pure regression, free
hyperelastic ANN, polyconvex ICNN, polyconvex ICKAN). Section~\ref{sec:c1-regression}
shows a direct, quantitative violation: the tier-1 pure-regression model has
no energy potential at all, and a trapezoidal cyclic-work test
$\oint_{\mathcal L}\bm S\cdot d\bm\varepsilon$ around closed strain loops
-- refined to distinguish a genuine curl from quadrature error -- finds a
stable, non-vanishing residual for it and a residual that vanishes under
refinement for the three energy-based tiers, a direct, measured failure
of (C1). Section~\ref{sec:non-polyconvex} shows the analogous failure one
level up the hierarchy, for (C5): an otherwise identically trained,
uncertified flexible PANN (tier 2) matches or slightly beats the
certified models on every reported accuracy metric, while failing the
rank-one necessary condition of polyconvexity on
$\FreeRankOneViolationFraction$ of sampled states, at ordinary,
non-extreme deformations. In both cases the accuracy metrics on
Table~\ref{tab:master} are silent, or actively misleading: the tier-1
pure-regression model is in fact the \emph{most} accurate of the four ANNs on
the held-out test trajectory by every reported metric. Accuracy and the admissibility
certificates answer different questions; only a direct, targeted test of
each checklist item, not the training loss and not a held-out error
number, can confirm the certificate is actually present.

\subsection{Algebraic redundancy of the \texorpdfstring{$Q^H$}{Q\^{}H} features}
\label{app:redundancy}
This expands Remark~\ref{rem:redundancy}. Writing $T=\tr \bm C$ and
$t_{ki}=\bm d_{ki}\cdot \bm C\bm d_{ki}$, the elementary $2\times2$
identity $\Cof(\bm C)=T\bm I-\bm C$ (the same identity behind
Lemma~\ref{lem:isotropic}, applied to $\bm C$ itself rather than to a
rank-one $\bm d\otimes\bm d$) gives $\bm d_{ki}\cdot\Cof(\bm C)\bm
d_{ki}=T-t_{ki}$, and hence, using $\sum_iw_{ki}=2$ and
$\sum_iw_{ki}t_{ki}=T$ (both consequences of the balance
condition~\eqref{eq:balanced-directions} established in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:isotropic-features}),
\begin{equation}
 Q_{k,2}^H=Q_{k,2}^F,\qquad
 Q_{k,3}^H=-T^3+3T\,Q_{k,2}^F-Q_{k,3}^F,
 \label{eq:redundancy}
\end{equation}
exactly, for every system $k$ and every $\bm C$ (verified symbolically and
confirmed numerically to machine precision for random balanced systems
and random $\bm C$). So the fifteen entries of $\bm z_{\rm pc}$
in~\eqref{eq:features} are not fifteen algebraically independent
scalars: given $I_1=T$ and $\{Q_{k,2}^F,Q_{k,3}^F\}_{k=1}^3$ (seven
numbers), the corresponding $\{Q_{k,2}^H,Q_{k,3}^H\}_{k=1}^3$ are fixed
exactly by~\eqref{eq:redundancy}.

The redundancy does not, however, make $Q_{k,2}^H,Q_{k,3}^H$ useless as
separate network inputs, for an architectural reason specific to the
monotone ICNN of Section~\ref{sec:c5-construction}: reconstructing
$Q_{k,3}^H$ from $T,Q_{k,2}^F,Q_{k,3}^F$ via~\eqref{eq:redundancy}
requires the \emph{negative} coefficients $-1$ on $T^3$ and $-1$ on
$Q_{k,3}^F$, which a network constrained to non-negative weights at
every layer (needed for the convexity-composition argument of
Proposition~\ref{prop:polyconvex}) cannot represent internally.
Supplying $Q_{k,2}^H,Q_{k,3}^H$ directly as their own coordinates
therefore gives the monotone network access to combinations of $\bm C$
that no non-negative combination of the other coordinates could
reconstruct, even though those combinations carry no additional
information in the unconstrained, purely algebraic sense: much as a
monotone function of $x$ and, separately, of $-x$ is strictly more
expressive than a monotone function of $x$ alone.

\subsection{A closer reading of Klein et al.'s trivial-group case}
\label{app:klein-trivial}
This expands the pointer in Section~\ref{sec:related-klein-linden}.
Klein et al.'s~\cite{Klein2026} own Table~1 lists the \emph{trivial}
group $C_1$ ($|\mathcal G|=1$, identity only) among the finite groups
their construction covers, and substituting $\mathcal G=C_1$ into their
group-symmetrization operator collapses the symmetrization sum to its
single, un-symmetrized term. Read this way, their own machinery already
contains the seed of a fully general, no-symmetry construction
satisfying (C1), (C2), (C4)--(C6), a nice illustration of just how
general their underlying integrity basis is. They do not state,
highlight, verify, or numerically exercise this reading anywhere in
their paper: every one of their worked examples (their Section~5) uses
the cubic group specifically. Crediting them with having solved the
general case would therefore over-credit an inference they never made
or checked, which is why the main text states our own contribution as
complementary rather than as closing a gap no other construction could
possibly reach.

\subsection{The periodic-boundary-condition averaging identity, in full}
\label{app:periodicity}
This expands the pointer in Section~\ref{sec:background-fom}. Under
periodic boundary conditions, a classical boundary-integral identity
(the divergence theorem applied to the periodic fluctuation field)
gives $\langle\bm F\rangle=\bar{\bm F}$ exactly, where $\bar{\bm F}$ is
the applied macroscopic deformation gradient and $\langle\cdot\rangle$
denotes the volume average over the RVE, and the same argument applied
to the microscopic first Piola--Kirchhoff stress gives a
volume-averaged macro-stress that is automatically energy-conjugate.
Because the Green--Lagrange strain $\bm E=\tfrac12(\bm F^T\bm F-\bm I)$
is a \emph{nonlinear}, quadratic function of $\bm F$, this identity
does not by itself give $\langle\bm E\rangle=\bm E(\bar{\bm F})$: the
two differ, in general, by a Jensen-type gap driven by the fluctuation
$\bm F-\bar{\bm F}$. The exact, elementary averaging identity that
transfers without approximation under periodicity is the one stated
above, at the level of $\bm F$, not of $\bm E$.

\subsection{The D-HPROM--ANN reference-value discrepancy}
\label{app:dhprom-discrepancy}
This expands the pointer in Section~\ref{sec:results}. An earlier,
separately produced reference value for D-HPROM--ANN's energy-conjugate
stress error, from a generation script no longer present in this
project's history, reported $\DHpromOldStressError$ overall ($S_{12}$
component $\DHpromOldSxyError$). The value reported in the main text,
computed independently by the reaction-force procedure of
Section~\ref{sec:results} and itself cross-validated to
$\HpromControlFomStressError$ against the direct FOM on this path,
differs from that older figure by a factor of $\DHpromNewOverOldRatio$.
We report the new, independently verified value and record the
discrepancy explicitly rather than silently discard it. The most likely
explanation is a checkpoint-naming inconsistency uncovered during this
project's own repository reorganization: an animation script, and
possibly whatever process produced the older reference, pointed at a
since-identified superseded ANN checkpoint filed under the name later
reused for the corrected one. This has not been independently confirmed
and is recorded here as an open item rather than papered over.
